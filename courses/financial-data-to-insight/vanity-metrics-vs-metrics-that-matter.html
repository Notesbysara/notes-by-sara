Vanity metrics vs metrics that matter

A report can be full of big, rising numbers and still tell nobody what to do.

What you'll be able to do: spot a vanity metric and replace it with one that supports a decision, using a four-question test. You'll also pair results with early-warning metrics, and set targets that don't backfire.

Here's a line from a real-looking monthly report: “Website visits up 40%.” It sounds like great news. Now look underneath:

Last monthThis month
Website visits10,00014,000
Share of visits that buy3%2%
Orders300280
Online sales (orders × $65)$19,500$18,200
Sales per visit$1.95$1.30

Visits rose 40%, but online sales fell by about 7%. The headline number felt good and hid the one that mattered. This topic shows you how to tell the two apart.

Two kinds of number​

A vanity metric is a number that looks impressive but doesn't help anyone make a decision.

A metric that matters (often called an actionable metric) is one that, if it changed, would change what someone does. The few a team tracks most closely are its KPIs.

Vanity metrics aren't usually lies. They're often true. The problem is that they fill the page and push out the numbers that should drive decisions.

Four common traps, and what to use instead​

Each trap is a number that is fine to know, but misleading on its own. Pair it with a better partner.

1

Revenue on its own

Revenue can grow while the business gets weaker, for example when growth comes from heavy discounts.

BeforeWith a 20% discount
Price per item$50$40
Items sold1,0001,300
Revenue$50,000$52,000
Cost of the items ($30 each)$30,000$39,000
Gross profit$20,000$13,000

Example Revenue rose 4%, but gross profit fell 35%, and gross margin fell from 40% to 25%.

Better partner Gross margin or operating margin beside revenue.

2

Profit on its own

Profit is an accounting number. A business can report a profit and still run short of cash, for example when customers are slow to pay.

Example The shop in Topic 1 made $140k profit, yet its cash fell by $60k.

Better partner Operating cash flow beside profit. You'll find it on the cash flow statement. Free cash flow, which is operating cash flow minus spending on long-term assets, is another good partner. For the shop, operating cash flow was $40k and, after $80k of refits, free cash flow was −$40k.

3

Total customers

“10,000 customers so far” only ever goes up. It says nothing about whether people come back.

Example The shop has served 10,000 customers in total, but only 35% of last year's customers bought again this year.

Better partner How many customers you keep from one year to the next, known as customer retention.

4

An average with no spread

An average can hide the fact that a few customers or products carry the whole business.

Example The average business customer spends $6,500 a year, yet the top 10 business customers bring in 45% of all business sales. Losing one of them would hurt far more than the average suggests.

Better partner Show the spread, or the share of sales from the biggest customers, called revenue concentration.

Notice the pattern. Vanity metrics are usually totals, raw counts, or numbers with nothing to compare them to. Metrics that matter are usually ratios, rates or comparisons, and they connect to profit, cash or risk. Not always, though: cash in the bank is a total, and it matters a lot.

The four-question test​

Before you put any number in a report, ask these four questions. A metric that matters should pass all four.

QuestionWhy it matters
If this number changed, would we do anything differently?If not, it's decoration.
Can it be compared?A number needs a yardstick: last year, the budget or a competitor. Revenue of $2.31m means little on its own.
Can someone influence it?If nobody can act on it, it's just weather.
Is it linked to profit, cash or risk?Those are what a business depends on.

Leading and lagging metrics​

A lagging metric reports a result after it has happened, such as profit or sales. A leading metric tends to move earlier, so it can warn you where a result is heading.

A business is judged on its lagging metrics, but by the time they move, it's often too late to act. A useful report pairs each important result with something that can warn you early.

Result (lagging)An early warning (leading)
Cash at the end of the monthShare of customers paying within 30 days
Online salesItems added to online shopping carts, and email sign-ups
Gross marginAverage discount given, and changes in supplier prices

Example Had the owner been watching the share of customers paying within 30 days, its fall from 80% to 62% would have warned the owner that cash was being squeezed.

Tip A leading metric is a clue, not a guarantee. Check in your own data that it really does tend to move first.

When a number becomes a target

Once people are rewarded for hitting a number, they may find ways to hit it that don't help the business. This is often called Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

Example If store managers are rewarded only on sales, heavy discounts can push sales up while gross profit falls, just like trap 1.

Tip Pair every target with a check that would catch gaming it, such as sales with gross margin.

Let's see it in action​

The shop's latest report has six headline numbers. We run the test on each.

Number in the reportPasses?What we do
Website visits up 40%No: not linked to profit by itselfReplace with sales per visit
Instagram followers up 25%No: no decision depends on itDrop it from the report
Sales this year $2.31m, up 10%Partly: it needs a partnerKeep, beside gross margin
Gross margin 39%, down from 40%YesKeep
Cash $340k, down 15%YesKeep
Customers who paid within 30 days: 62%, down from 80%YesKeep, and investigate

The new report has five numbers instead of six, and every one of them points to a possible action. Gross margin and cash connect straight back to the questions in Topics 1 and 4.

This is an invented shop. All numbers are examples.

Try it yourself (optional)

A store manager's monthly report leads with “Store visits up 18%,” meaning more people walked into the store. Run the four-question test on it, and suggest a better metric.

Show the answer

Would we act differently? Not on its own. Can it be compared? Only with last month. Last year would be fairer, because store traffic is seasonal. Can someone influence it? Partly, through window displays and marketing. Is it linked to profit, cash or risk? Not directly. So it fails as a headline.

Better: the share of visitors who buy, or sales per visitor, beside gross margin. Store visits can still be useful as a leading metric, but it shouldn't be the headline.

What this looks like at work​

Choosing the right numbers is a big part of finance work. For example:

  • You're asked to build a monthly dashboard for your team.
  • A presentation leads with “record revenue”, and you're asked whether that's good news.
  • You're helping set targets, and need to decide what to measure.

Each time, run the four-question test and pair any total with a ratio or a comparison.

How you'd say it to your manager​

Suggest changes politely, with a reason and a replacement for each.

Example message

Hi Maya, I've been looking at our latest report. Two numbers, website visits and Instagram followers, look good but don't link to a decision. I'd suggest replacing visits with sales per visit, which fell from $1.95 to $1.30, and dropping followers. I'd also lead with the share of customers paying within 30 days, which fell from 80% to 62%, since slower payments are what's squeezing our cash. Happy to mock up the new version.

Notice it explains why, offers a replacement, and leads with the number that needs attention.

Remember

  • A vanity metric looks good but doesn't change a decision.
  • Pair totals with a ratio, a rate or a comparison.
  • Four questions: would we act differently, can it be compared, can someone influence it, is it linked to profit, cash or risk?
  • A short report where every number earns its place beats a long one.
