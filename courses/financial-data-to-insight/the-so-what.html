Topic 10 of 12 · Step 5: Interpret

The “So what?”

Every time you show a number, someone is quietly asking “so what?”. If you can't answer, the work isn't finished.

What you'll be able to do: turn a finding into an interpretation that explains why it happened, how much it matters and what to do. You'll also write a one-page executive summary that leads with the answer.

Description vs interpretation​

Interpretation is explaining what a result means for the business and recommending an action based on it.

A description tells people what happened. An interpretation tells them why, how much it matters, and what to do next. It uses the same data, but it's far more useful.

DescriptionInterpretation
“Cash fell 15% this year.”“Cash fell 15%, mainly because customers are taking twice as long to pay. If we collected as fast as last year, we'd have roughly $60k more in the bank, so let's chase late payers before we commit to the new store.”

Most people stop at the description. Managers need the interpretation.

Four steps: Finding, Why, So what, Now what​

1

Finding

One sentence, with the number. What is the main thing the data shows?

Example Cash fell $60k, or 15%, to $340k, even though the shop made a $140k profit.

2

Why

The cause, or your best explanation, and the evidence for it. Be honest about how sure you are. If you're not sure, say what you'd check.

Example The biggest reasons are $70k more owed by customers (days to collect rose from about 9 to 19) and $80k of one-off store refits. The likely cause of the slower payment is a few business customers paying late, which I'd confirm with the aging report.

Tip Separate facts from judgment. “Days to collect rose to 19” is a fact. “A few customers are paying late” is a judgment until you've checked.

3

So what

The impact. Size it in money, percent or risk. A $5k problem and a $500k problem need very different responses.

Example If customers paid as fast as last year (about 9 days), the shop would be owed roughly $57k instead of $120k, freeing about $60k of cash. That decides whether the shop can keep its safety buffer after buying the new store.

Tip When you estimate, show the working and round sensibly. “Roughly $60k” is more honest than “$63,040”.

4

Now what

A recommendation, or the decision you need from someone. Add the next step, with an owner and a date if you can.

Example Chase the late payers this month and tighten payment terms for business customers. Recheck cash at the end of next quarter, then decide on the new store.

The one-page executive summary​

An executive summary is a short document that gives a busy decision-maker the answer, the key evidence and the recommendation, usually on one page.

Many senior readers read the top of the page and little else, so the order matters:

PartWhat goes in it
1. The answerTwo sentences: the finding and what you recommend
2. Key pointsThree or four bullets, each one a finding with a number
3. One chartThe strongest one, with the finding as its title
4. RecommendationWhat to do, who should do it, and by when
5. Risks and limitsWhat could change the answer, and what you haven't checked yet

Example: the shop's executive summary

Cash is tighter than profit suggests, so we recommend delaying the new store by one quarter. The shop made $140k of profit, but cash fell $60k mainly because customers are paying more slowly and we spent $80k on refits.

  • Cash fell 15%, from $400k to $340k.
  • Money owed by customers rose from $50k to $120k. Days to collect rose from about 9 to 19.
  • Buying the $250k store now would leave $90k, about two months of fixed costs, below our three-month safety buffer of $120k.
  • Collecting as fast as last year would free roughly $60k.

Recommendation: chase late payers and tighten business payment terms this month (owner: finance), then review the store decision at the end of next quarter.

Limits: we haven't yet seen the aging report, so we don't know how much of the $120k is actually overdue. We also haven't assessed whether the new store would pay back its cost.

This is an invented shop. All numbers are examples.

Tip Keep one honest limit in every summary. Admitting what you haven't checked doesn't make you look weaker. It makes people trust the rest.

Habits that make interpretations land​

1

Answer first

Lead with the conclusion, not the story of how you got there. The method can come later, or in an appendix.

2

Use plain words

A clear sentence beats an impressive one. “Customers are paying more slowly” beats “receivables collection efficiency has deteriorated”.

3

Match the reader

An owner wants the decision. A finance manager wants the numbers behind it. Keep the same answer, but adjust the detail.

4

Take the 30-second test

Say your main point out loud in 30 seconds, without notes. If you can't, it isn't clear in your head yet, and it won't be clear on the page.

Try it yourself (optional)

Turn this description into an interpretation using the four steps: “Inventory rose from $300k to $330k this year.” You can say what you would check where you don't know the cause.

Show the answer

One good version:

Finding: inventory rose $30k, or 10%, to $330k. Why: sales also grew 10%, so the rise is in line with growth; I'd still check whether any lines are selling slowly. So what: the extra stock ties up $30k of cash at a time when cash is already tight. Now what: review slow-selling lines and slow down reorders on them this month, then recheck stock levels in three months.

Yours can differ, as long as it has all four steps and sizes the impact.

What this looks like at work​

Interpreting is the part of finance work that gets noticed. For example:

  • You present the month's results and someone asks, “So what does this mean for us?”
  • You're asked for a one-page summary before a board meeting.
  • Your manager forwards your analysis to a senior leader with no time to read the detail.

Each time, lead with the answer, size the impact, recommend a next step, and state one honest limit.

How you'd say it to your manager​

Give the answer first, then just enough evidence to trust it.

Example message

Hi Maya, short version: I'd suggest we hold off on the new store for one quarter. Cash fell $60k this year, mainly because customers are taking about 19 days to pay instead of 9, and we spent $80k on refits. Collecting as fast as last year would free roughly $60k. I've attached a one-page summary. One limit: I haven't seen the aging report yet, so I'll confirm how much is actually overdue by Friday.

Notice it starts with the recommendation, sizes the impact, and names what's still unconfirmed.

Remember

  • A description says what happened. An interpretation says why, how much it matters and what to do.
  • Use four steps: Finding, Why, So what, Now what.
  • Always size the impact in money, percent or risk.
  • An executive summary leads with the answer, fits on one page, and includes one honest limit.
  • If you can't say it in 30 seconds, it isn't clear yet.
