The headline number felt good and hid the one that mattered. This topic shows you how to tell the two apart.</p></div>

## Two kinds of number

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="def"><p>A <b>vanity metric</b> is a number that looks impressive but doesn't help anyone make a decision.</p></div> <div className="def"><p>A <b>metric that matters</b> (often called an actionable metric) is one that, if it changed, would change what someone does. The few a team tracks most closely are its <Term slug="key-performance-indicator" name="Key Performance Indicator (KPI)" def="A key performance indicator (KPI) is a measurable value that shows how well a business, team or individual is achieving an important objective.">KPIs</Term>.</p></div> <p>Vanity metrics aren't usually lies. They're often true. The problem is that they fill the page and push out the numbers that should drive decisions.</p></div>

## Four common traps, and what to use instead

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Each trap is a number that is fine to know, but misleading on its own. Pair it with a better partner.</p> <div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Revenue on its own</h3></div> <p>Revenue can grow while the business gets weaker, for example when growth comes from heavy discounts.</p><div className="tw small-t"><table><tbody><tr><th></th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Before</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>With a 20% discount</th></tr><tr><td>Price per item</td><td className="r">$50</td><td className="r">$40</td></tr><tr><td>Items sold</td><td className="r">1,000</td><td className="r">1,300</td></tr><tr><td>Revenue</td><td className="r">$50,000</td><td className="r">$52,000</td></tr><tr><td>Cost of the items ($30 each)</td><td className="r">$30,000</td><td className="r">$39,000</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Gross profit</td><td className="r">$20,000</td><td className="r">$13,000</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> Revenue rose 4%, but <Term slug="gross-profit" name="Gross Profit" def="Gross profit is the money left from revenue after subtracting the direct cost of the goods or services sold.">gross profit</Term> fell 35%, and <Term slug="gross-margin" name="Gross Margin" def="Gross margin is gross profit expressed as a percentage of revenue. It shows how much of each unit of sales is left after paying the direct costs of the goods or services sold.">gross margin</Term> fell from 40% to 25%.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Better partner</b> Gross margin or <Term slug="operating-margin" name="Operating Margin" def="Operating margin is operating income divided by revenue, showing the percentage of each sale that remains as profit after paying for the costs of running the business, before interest and tax.">operating margin</Term> beside revenue.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Profit on its own</h3></div> <p>Profit is an accounting number. A business can report a profit and still run short of cash, for example when customers are slow to pay.</p><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> The shop in Topic 1 made $140k profit, yet its cash fell by $60k.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Better partner</b> <Term slug="operating-cash-flow" name="Operating Cash Flow" def="Operating cash flow is the cash a business generates from its normal day-to-day activities, such as selling goods and paying suppliers, staff and taxes.">Operating cash flow</Term> beside profit. You'll find it on the <Term slug="cash-flow-statement" name="Cash Flow Statement" def="The cash flow statement shows how much cash came into and went out of a business during a period, grouped into operating, investing and financing activities.">cash flow statement</Term>. <Term slug="free-cash-flow" name="Free Cash Flow" def="Free cash flow is the cash a business generates from its operations after paying for the capital investments needed to maintain and grow it. It is the cash available to repay debt, pay dividends or reinvest.">Free cash flow</Term>, which is operating cash flow minus spending on long-term assets, is another good partner. For the shop, operating cash flow was $40k and, after $80k of refits, free cash flow was −$40k.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>Total customers</h3></div> <p>“10,000 customers so far” only ever goes up. It says nothing about whether people come back.</p><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> The shop has served 10,000 customers in total, but only 35% of last year's customers bought again this year.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Better partner</b> How many customers you keep from one year to the next, known as <Term slug="customer-retention" name="Customer Retention" def="Customer retention is a business's ability to keep its existing customers over time, and the strategies it uses to do so.">customer retention</Term>.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">4</span><h3>An average with no spread</h3></div> <p>An average can hide the fact that a few customers or products carry the whole business.</p><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> The average business customer spends $6,500 a year, yet the top 10 business customers bring in 45% of all business sales. Losing one of them would hurt far more than the average suggests.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Better partner</b> Show the spread, or the share of sales from the biggest customers, called <Term slug="revenue-concentration" name="Revenue Concentration" def="Revenue concentration is the degree to which a company's revenue depends on a small number of sources, such as a few customers, products, markets or contracts.">revenue concentration</Term>.</p> </div> </div> <p>Notice the pattern. Vanity metrics are usually totals, raw counts, or numbers with nothing to compare them to. Metrics that matter are usually ratios, rates or comparisons, and they connect to profit, cash or risk. Not always, though: cash in the bank is a total, and it matters a lot.</p></div>

## The four-question test

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Before you put any number in a report, ask these four questions. A metric that matters should pass all four.</p> <div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Question</th><th>Why it matters</th></tr><tr><td>If this number changed, would we do anything differently?</td><td>If not, it's decoration.</td></tr><tr><td>Can it be compared?</td><td>A number needs a yardstick: last year, the budget or a competitor. Revenue of $2.31m means little on its own.</td></tr><tr><td>Can someone influence it?</td><td>If nobody can act on it, it's just weather.</td></tr><tr><td>Is it linked to profit, cash or risk?</td><td>Those are what a business depends on.</td></tr></tbody></table></div></div>

## Leading and lagging metrics

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="def"><p>A <Term slug="lagging-indicator" name="Lagging metric" def="A lagging metric records a result after it has happened, such as profit or sales."><b>lagging metric</b></Term> reports a result after it has happened, such as profit or sales. A <Term slug="leading-indicator" name="Leading metric" def="A leading metric tends to change before a result does, so it can give early warning of where that result is heading."><b>leading metric</b></Term> tends to move earlier, so it can warn you where a result is heading.</p></div> <p>A business is judged on its lagging metrics, but by the time they move, it's often too late to act. A useful report pairs each important result with something that can warn you early.</p> <div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Result (lagging)</th><th>An early warning (leading)</th></tr><tr><td>Cash at the end of the month</td><td>Share of customers paying within 30 days</td></tr><tr><td>Online sales</td><td>Items added to online shopping carts, and email sign-ups</td></tr><tr><td>Gross margin</td><td>Average discount given, and changes in supplier prices</td></tr></tbody></table></div> <p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> Had the owner been watching the share of customers paying within 30 days, its fall from 80% to 62% would have warned the owner that cash was being squeezed.</p> <p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> A leading metric is a clue, not a guarantee. Check in your own data that it really does tend to move first.</p> <h3 style={{marginTop:"8px"}}>When a number becomes a target</h3> <p>Once people are rewarded for hitting a number, they may find ways to hit it that don't help the business. This is often called Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.</p> <p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> If store managers are rewarded only on sales, heavy discounts can push sales up while gross profit falls, just like trap 1.</p> <p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> Pair every target with a check that would catch gaming it, such as sales with gross margin.</p></div>

## Let's see it in action

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>The shop's latest report has six headline numbers. We run the test on each.</p> <div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Number in the report</th><th>Passes?</th><th>What we do</th></tr><tr><td>Website visits up 40%</td><td>No: not linked to profit by itself</td><td>Replace with sales per visit</td></tr><tr><td>Instagram followers up 25%</td><td>No: no decision depends on it</td><td>Drop it from the report</td></tr><tr><td>Sales this year $2.31m, up 10%</td><td>Partly: it needs a partner</td><td>Keep, beside gross margin</td></tr><tr><td>Gross margin 39%, down from 40%</td><td>Yes</td><td>Keep</td></tr><tr><td>Cash $340k, down 15%</td><td>Yes</td><td>Keep</td></tr><tr><td>Customers who paid within 30 days: 62%, down from 80%</td><td>Yes</td><td>Keep, and investigate</td></tr></tbody></table></div> <p>The new report has five numbers instead of six, and every one of them points to a possible action. Gross margin and cash connect straight back to the questions in Topics 1 and 4.</p> <p className="small">This is an invented shop. All numbers are examples.</p></div>

<div className="nbs-sec try"><p className="eyebrow">Try it yourself (optional)</p> <p>A store manager's monthly report leads with “Store visits up 18%,” meaning more people walked into the store. Run the four-question test on it, and suggest a better metric.</p> <details><summary>Show the answer</summary><p><b>Would we act differently?</b> Not on its own. <b>Can it be compared?</b> Only with last month. Last year would be fairer, because store traffic is seasonal. <b>Can someone influence it?</b> Partly, through window displays and marketing. <b>Is it linked to profit, cash or risk?</b> Not directly. So it fails as a headline.</p><p>Better: the share of visitors who buy, or sales per visitor, beside gross margin. Store visits can still be useful as a leading metric, but it shouldn't be the headline.</p></details></div>

## What this looks like at work

<div className="nbs-sec work"><p>Choosing the right numbers is a big part of finance work. For example:</p> <ul> <li>You're asked to build a monthly dashboard for your team.</li> <li>A presentation leads with “record revenue”, and you're asked whether that's good news.</li> <li>You're helping set targets, and need to decide what to measure.</li> </ul> <p>Each time, run the four-question test and pair any total with a ratio or a comparison.</p></div>

## How you'd say it to your manager

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Suggest changes politely, with a reason and a replacement for each.</p> <div className="msg"> <p className="eyebrow">Example message</p> <p className="body">Hi Maya, I've been looking at our latest report. Two numbers, website visits and Instagram followers, look good but don't link to a decision. I'd suggest replacing visits with sales per visit, which fell from $1.95 to $1.30, and dropping followers. I'd also lead with the share of customers paying within 30 days, which fell from 80% to 62%, since slower payments are what's squeezing our cash. Happy to mock up the new version.</p> </div> <p className="small">Notice it explains why, offers a replacement, and leads with the number that needs attention.</p></div>

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="remember"> <p className="eyebrow">Remember</p> <ul> <li>A vanity metric looks good but doesn't change a decision.</li> <li>Pair totals with a ratio, a rate or a comparison.</li> <li>Four questions: would we act differently, can it be compared, can someone influence it, is it linked to profit, cash or risk?</li> <li>A short report where every number earns its place beats a long one.</li> </ul> </div></div>
