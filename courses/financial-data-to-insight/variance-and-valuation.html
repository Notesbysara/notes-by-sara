Variance and valuation

Tools that every finance job uses. Variance looks back at what happened. Break-even and forecasts look ahead at what could happen. Valuation puts a value today on cash that arrives later.

What you'll be able to do: explain variances against the budget, work out a break-even point, and build a simple forecast with best, base and worst cases. You'll also value future cash in today's dollars and test whether an investment is worth its price.

None of them needs advanced math, just clear steps and careful assumptions.

Part 1: Variance​

A budget variance is the difference between a budgeted amount and the actual result. It is usually written as actual minus budget, and also shown as a percentage of the budget.

In statistics, “variance” means something different: how spread out a set of numbers is. In finance reports, it almost always means actual against plan, forecast or last year.

Here are the shop's results for this year against its budget:

$ thousandsBudgetActualVariance
Revenue2,2502,310+60+2.7%Favorable
Cost of goods sold1,3501,409+59+4.4%Unfavorable
Running costs700716+16+2.3%Unfavorable
Operating income200185−15−7.5%Unfavorable
1

Label it favorable or unfavorable

A favorable variance improves profit, such as revenue above budget or costs below budget. An unfavorable variance reduces profit.

The sign alone doesn't tell you if it's good news. Revenue +60 is good. Costs +59 is bad. Always add the label.

Tip In Excel, =IF(C2>B2,"Favorable","Unfavorable") works for revenue rows. For cost rows, flip it. A zero variance shows as Unfavorable, so check those by hand.

2

Ask why: split price and volume

A variance is a question, not an answer. A useful first split is price (did we charge or pay a different price?) and volume (did we sell or buy a different amount?).

ShoesBudgetActual
Pairs sold12,00012,500
Price per pair$65$63
Revenue$780,000$787,500
Split of the +$7,500 varianceHow it's worked outEffect
Volume: we sold more(12,500 − 12,000) × $65+$32,500 favorable

Example Shoe revenue beat the budget, but only because volume made up for a price cut. That's a very different story from “shoes did well.”

The same split works for costs. Did we pay a different price per item (a price variance), or buy or use a different amount (a quantity or usage variance)?

3

Check if the budget still fits

Some cost variances happen simply because you sold more. Selling more means buying more inventory, so cost of goods rises too.

A flexible budget adjusts the budget to the actual level of activity, so you can see which variances come from volume and which from everything else.

Example The budget assumed cost of goods would be 60% of sales. At the actual sales of $2,310k, that's $1,386k. So $36k of the $59k cost variance comes from the higher sales level, and only $23k comes from costs rising to 61% of sales, the problem Topic 4 investigated.

Tip You can't explain every variance. Many teams agree on a rule, such as explaining anything over 5% or $10,000, and focus there.

Part 2: Costs, break-even and forecasts​

Variance looks back. To plan ahead, you first need to know how costs behave when sales go up or down.

1

Split costs into fixed and variable

A fixed cost, such as rent, doesn't change when sales do, as long as the business stays at about its usual size. A variable cost rises and falls with sales, such as the cost of the goods you sell.

Shop costs this year, $ thousandsAmountType
Cost of goods sold1,409Variable
Card fees, delivery and other costs that move with sales216Variable
Rent, wages and bills480Fixed
Depreciation20Fixed
Total costs before interest and tax2,125

Sales of 2,310 minus these costs of 2,125 gives the same operating income as before: 185.

In reality, many costs are semi-variable. Wages, for example, rise with overtime in busy months. For planning, a simple split is usually good enough, as long as you write down the assumptions.

2

Work out the contribution margin

The contribution margin ratio is the share of each sales dollar left after variable costs. It pays for fixed costs first, and anything above that is profit.

$ thousandsAmount% of sales
Sales2,310100%
Variable costs (1,409 + 216)(1,625)70.3%
Contribution68529.7%
Fixed costs (480 + 20)(500)
Operating income185

Example Of every extra $100 of sales, about $29.70 is left after variable costs. Once fixed costs are covered, that $29.70 is extra operating income.

3

Find the break-even point

The break-even point is the level of sales at which contribution exactly covers fixed costs, so operating income is zero. Working it out is called break-even analysis.

FormulaBreak-even sales = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio
For the shop500 ÷ 0.2965 = about 1,686 ($ thousands)

The shop breaks even at about $1.69 million of sales a year. It sells $2.31 million, so sales could fall about 27% before it would make an operating loss. That cushion is called the margin of safety.

Example Back to Topic 1's new store. Suppose it would add $120k a year of fixed costs (rent, wages and bills), more than the $80k average per store, because new sites usually cost more to rent and earn the same 29.7% contribution margin. It would need about $405k of sales a year just to break even (120 ÷ 0.2965). The shop's six stores average $385k each (2,310 ÷ 6). So the new store would have to beat the average store just to cover its own costs, before paying back the $250k it cost to open.

In Excel Put fixed costs and the contribution margin ratio in their own labeled cells, and calculate break-even from them. Then you can change one assumption and watch the answer move.

4

Build a simple forecast with scenarios

A forecast is your best estimate of future results, built from stated assumptions. A budget is a target the business commits to. A forecast is updated as things change.

A useful forecast starts from a few drivers, such as sales growth and how costs behave. It shows more than one case, because nobody knows the future. Here we assume the contribution margin stays at 29.65% (685 ÷ 2,310, shown as 29.7%), fixed costs rise 3% to $515k, and growth slows as the stores mature, so the base case is 8%.

Next year, $ thousandsSales growthSalesContributionFixed costsOperating income
Worst case0%2,310685(515)170
Base case8%2,495740(515)225
Best case12%2,587767(515)252

Notice that operating income moves faster than sales. In the base case, sales grow 8%, but operating income grows about 22% (185 to 225), because fixed costs barely change. This is operating leverage, and it works in reverse too: in the worst case, flat sales and slightly higher fixed costs cut operating income by about 8%.

Watch out The base case is your most honest estimate, not the most hopeful one. Check growth against history (between 10% and 17% a year over the last three years here), and write down every assumption.

Tip Compare the forecast with actual results each month or quarter, and update it. Many companies keep a rolling forecast that always looks the same distance ahead.

All numbers are examples.

Part 3: Valuation in plain words​

Valuation starts from an idea you already believe: a dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year. You could invest today's dollar, and next year's dollar might not arrive. This is called the time value of money.

Present value is what a future amount of money is worth today, after allowing for the return that money could earn in the meantime.

A discount rate is the rate used to convert future cash into its value today. It reflects the return you could get elsewhere, plus extra for risk.

Inside companies, the discount rate is often based on the weighted average cost of capital (WACC): the blended return that the company's lenders and owners expect. A riskier project usually gets a higher rate.

FormulaPresent value = Cash ÷ (1 + r)^t
rThe discount rate, for example 10% = 0.10
tHow many years until the cash arrives
1

Discount each year's cash

Discounted cash flow (DCF) values a business or investment by forecasting its future cash flows and converting each one to its value today.

YearCash expectedDivide byWorth today at 10%
11001.1090.91
21101.2190.91
31201.33190.16
Total330271.98 ≈ 272

The 330 of future cash is worth about 272 today. The later the cash arrives, the more it shrinks.

In Excel =NPV(10%,B2:B4) gives 271.98 for cash flows in B2 to B4, when the first one arrives one year from now. Despite its name, Excel's NPV doesn't subtract any upfront cost. Subtract that yourself to get net present value.

2

See how much the discount rate matters

Change one assumption and the answer moves. At 12% instead of 10%, the same cash flows are worth about 262, about 10 less.

Value today of the same three years of cash, at different discount rates

When the future cash is positive, as here, a higher discount rate means a lower value today, because future cash counts for less.

3

Show a range, not one number

A sensitivity table shows how a result changes when two key assumptions are varied at the same time.

Discount rate →8%10%12%14%
Cash flows 10% lower254245236228
Cash flows as forecast282272262253
Cash flows 10% higher310299289279

Depending on just two assumptions, these three years of cash could be worth anywhere from about 228 to 310. That's why a careful analyst shows a range.

Terminal value is the estimated value of a business beyond the years you forecast in detail. In a real DCF it is often the largest part of the total, which is another reason to treat the answer with humility.

One common way to estimate it assumes the cash keeps growing at a steady rate forever, called the perpetual growth method. It only works when the growth rate is below the discount rate, and the growth rate should be modest, usually no higher than the long-run growth of the economy.

Terminal value, step by stepWorkingResult
Cash in year 4, growing 2% a year120 × 1.02122.4
Terminal value at the end of year 3122.4 ÷ (0.10 − 0.02)1,530
Worth today1,530 ÷ 1.331about 1,150
Total value today271.98 + 1,149.50about 1,421

Here the terminal value is about 81% of the total. Change the growth rate or the discount rate a little and the total moves a lot, which is why analysts test these assumptions in a sensitivity table too.

Watch out The forecast and the discount rate are both estimates. A precise-looking DCF answer is only as good as the guesses inside it.

4

Decide: is it worth the price?

Net present value (NPV) is what future cash is worth today, minus what you pay for it today.

Valuing future cash is only half the decision. The other half is comparing it with the price.

If we pay 250 today for the three years of cashAt 10%At 14%
Value today of the cash272253
Price paid today(250)(250)
Net present value+22+3

A positive NPV means the investment is expected to earn more than the discount rate, so it should add value. A negative one means you'd expect to do better with another investment of similar risk. At 14%, this one only just passes.

In Excel =NPV(10%,B2:B4)-250 gives the net present value, +21.98.

Watch out A positive NPV is only as reliable as the forecast behind it. Always look at the range, not just the base case.

All numbers are examples. This topic teaches how valuation works; it isn't investment advice.

Try it yourself (optional)

1. The budget for running costs was $700k and the actual was $716k. Work out the variance in dollars and as a percentage, and label it.

2. If the shop's fixed costs rose to $530k and its contribution margin ratio stayed at 29.7%, what would its break-even sales be?

3. At a 10% discount rate, what is $121 received in two years worth today?

Show the answer

1. 716 − 700 = +$16k, which is 16 ÷ 700 = 2.3% over budget. It's unfavorable, because higher costs reduce profit.

2. 530 ÷ 0.2965 = about $1,787k, or roughly $1.79 million.

3. 121 ÷ 1.10² = 121 ÷ 1.21 = $100.

What this looks like at work​

These tools come up all the time. For example:

  • Every month, finance teams explain the biggest variances between actual results and the budget.
  • At budget time, and in quarterly reforecasts, teams use break-even and scenario forecasts to plan.
  • Before a company buys equipment or another business, someone estimates whether the future cash is worth the price today.
  • In interviews for finance roles, candidates are often asked to walk through a DCF.

Each time, label the variance, find its cause, and show valuations as a range with the assumptions stated.

How you'd say it to your manager​

Lead with the biggest variance and its cause, then what you'd do.

Example message

Hi Maya, operating income came in $15k below budget at $185k. Revenue beat budget by $60k, but cost of goods was $59k over and running costs were $16k over. $36k of that is simply from selling more; the other $23k is cost of goods rising to 61% of sales. On shoes, we sold more pairs but at a lower average price, so volume covered a $25k price shortfall. I'd suggest we look at discounting on shoes first.

Notice it gives the total, splits the main variance, and ends with a next step.

Remember

  • Variance = actual − budget. Label it favorable or unfavorable; the sign alone isn't enough.
  • Split variances into price and volume to find the cause.
  • Fixed costs stay put and variable costs move with sales. Break-even sales = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio.
  • Forecast with worst, base and best cases, and write down every assumption.
  • Present value = cash ÷ (1 + r)^t. Later cash and higher rates mean lower values today.
  • Show valuations as a range with a sensitivity table, never as one confident number.
