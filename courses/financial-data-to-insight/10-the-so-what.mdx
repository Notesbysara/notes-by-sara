<div className="nbs-head banner"><p className="eyebrow">Topic 10 of 12 · Step 5: Interpret</p> <h1>The “So what?”</h1> <p className="lead">Every time you show a number, someone is quietly asking “so what?”. If you can't answer, the work isn't finished.</p> <p className="goal"><b>What you'll be able to do:</b> turn a finding into an interpretation that explains why it happened, how much it matters and what to do. You'll also write a one-page executive summary that leads with the answer.</p></div>

## Description vs interpretation

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="def"><p><b>Interpretation</b> is explaining what a result means for the business and recommending an action based on it.</p></div> <p>A description tells people what happened. An interpretation tells them why, how much it matters, and what to do next. It uses the same data, but it's far more useful.</p> <div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Description</th><th>Interpretation</th></tr><tr><td>“Cash fell 15% this year.”</td><td>“Cash fell 15%, mainly because customers are taking twice as long to pay. If we collected as fast as last year, we'd have roughly $60k more in the bank, so let's chase late payers before we commit to the new store.”</td></tr></tbody></table></div> <p>Most people stop at the description. Managers need the interpretation.</p></div>

## Four steps: Finding, Why, So what, Now what

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Finding</h3></div> <p>One sentence, with the number. What is the main thing the data shows?</p><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> Cash fell $60k, or 15%, to $340k, even though the shop made a $140k profit.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Why</h3></div> <p>The cause, or your best explanation, and the evidence for it. Be honest about how sure you are. If you're not sure, say what you'd check.</p><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> The biggest reasons are $70k more owed by customers (<Term slug="days-sales-outstanding" name="Days Sales Outstanding" def="Days sales outstanding (DSO) is the average number of days it takes a business to collect payment from its customers after a sale.">days to collect</Term> rose from about 9 to 19) and $80k of one-off store refits. The likely cause of the slower payment is a few business customers paying late, which I'd confirm with the <Term slug="accounts-receivable" name="Aging report" def="An aging report lists what each customer owes, grouped by how long it has been unpaid.">aging report</Term>.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> Separate facts from judgment. “Days to collect rose to 19” is a fact. “A few customers are paying late” is a judgment until you've checked.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>So what</h3></div> <p>The impact. Size it in money, percent or risk. A $5k problem and a $500k problem need very different responses.</p><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> If customers paid as fast as last year (about 9 days), the shop would be owed roughly $57k instead of $120k, freeing about $60k of cash. That decides whether the shop can keep its safety buffer after buying the new store.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> When you estimate, show the working and round sensibly. “Roughly $60k” is more honest than “$63,040”.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">4</span><h3>Now what</h3></div> <p>A recommendation, or the decision you need from someone. Add the next step, with an owner and a date if you can.</p><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> Chase the late payers this month and tighten payment terms for business customers. Recheck cash at the end of next quarter, then decide on the new store.</p> </div> </div></div>

## The one-page executive summary

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="def"><p>An <b>executive summary</b> is a short document that gives a busy decision-maker the answer, the key evidence and the recommendation, usually on one page.</p></div> <p>Many senior readers read the top of the page and little else, so the order matters:</p> <div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Part</th><th>What goes in it</th></tr><tr><td>1. The answer</td><td>Two sentences: the finding and what you recommend</td></tr><tr><td>2. Key points</td><td>Three or four bullets, each one a finding with a number</td></tr><tr><td>3. One chart</td><td>The strongest one, with the finding as its title</td></tr><tr><td>4. Recommendation</td><td>What to do, who should do it, and by when</td></tr><tr><td>5. Risks and limits</td><td>What could change the answer, and what you haven't checked yet</td></tr></tbody></table></div> <div className="msg"> <p className="eyebrow">Example: the shop's executive summary</p> <p className="body"><b>Cash is tighter than profit suggests, so we recommend delaying the new store by one quarter.</b> The shop made $140k of profit, but cash fell $60k mainly because customers are paying more slowly and we spent $80k on refits.</p> <ul style={{margin:"0",paddingLeft:"20px",display:"flex",flexDirection:"column",gap:"4px"}}><li>Cash fell 15%, from $400k to $340k.</li><li>Money owed by customers rose from $50k to $120k. Days to collect rose from about 9 to 19.</li><li>Buying the $250k store now would leave $90k, about two months of fixed costs, below our three-month safety buffer of $120k.</li><li>Collecting as fast as last year would free roughly $60k.</li></ul> <p className="body"><b>Recommendation:</b> chase late payers and tighten business payment terms this month (owner: finance), then review the store decision at the end of next quarter.</p> <p className="body"><b>Limits:</b> we haven't yet seen the aging report, so we don't know how much of the $120k is actually overdue. We also haven't assessed whether the new store would pay back its cost.</p> </div> <p className="small">This is an invented shop. All numbers are examples.</p> <p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> Keep one honest limit in every summary. Admitting what you haven't checked doesn't make you look weaker. It makes people trust the rest.</p></div>

## Habits that make interpretations land

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Answer first</h3></div> <p>Lead with the conclusion, not the story of how you got there. The method can come later, or in an appendix.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Use plain words</h3></div> <p>A clear sentence beats an impressive one. “Customers are paying more slowly” beats “receivables collection efficiency has deteriorated”.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>Match the reader</h3></div> <p>An owner wants the decision. A finance manager wants the numbers behind it. Keep the same answer, but adjust the detail.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">4</span><h3>Take the 30-second test</h3></div> <p>Say your main point out loud in 30 seconds, without notes. If you can't, it isn't clear in your head yet, and it won't be clear on the page.</p> </div> </div></div>

<div className="nbs-sec try"><p className="eyebrow">Try it yourself (optional)</p> <p>Turn this description into an interpretation using the four steps: “Inventory rose from $300k to $330k this year.” You can say what you would check where you don't know the cause.</p> <details><summary>Show the answer</summary><p>One good version:</p><p><b>Finding:</b> inventory rose $30k, or 10%, to $330k. <b>Why:</b> sales also grew 10%, so the rise is in line with growth; I'd still check whether any lines are selling slowly. <b>So what:</b> the extra stock ties up $30k of cash at a time when cash is already tight. <b>Now what:</b> review slow-selling lines and slow down reorders on them this month, then recheck stock levels in three months.</p><p>Yours can differ, as long as it has all four steps and sizes the impact.</p></details></div>

## What this looks like at work

<div className="nbs-sec work"><p>Interpreting is the part of finance work that gets noticed. For example:</p> <ul style={{margin:"0",paddingLeft:"20px",display:"flex",flexDirection:"column",gap:"4px"}}><li>You present the month's results and someone asks, “So what does this mean for us?”</li><li>You're asked for a one-page summary before a board meeting.</li><li>Your manager forwards your analysis to a senior leader with no time to read the detail.</li></ul> <p>Each time, lead with the answer, size the impact, recommend a next step, and state one honest limit.</p></div>

## How you'd say it to your manager

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Give the answer first, then just enough evidence to trust it.</p> <div className="msg"> <p className="eyebrow">Example message</p> <p className="body">Hi Maya, short version: I'd suggest we hold off on the new store for one quarter. Cash fell $60k this year, mainly because customers are taking about 19 days to pay instead of 9, and we spent $80k on refits. Collecting as fast as last year would free roughly $60k. I've attached a one-page summary. One limit: I haven't seen the aging report yet, so I'll confirm how much is actually overdue by Friday.</p> </div> <p className="small">Notice it starts with the recommendation, sizes the impact, and names what's still unconfirmed.</p></div>

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="remember"> <p className="eyebrow">Remember</p> <ul><li>A description says what happened. An interpretation says why, how much it matters and what to do.</li><li>Use four steps: Finding, Why, So what, Now what.</li><li>Always size the impact in money, percent or risk.</li><li>An executive summary leads with the answer, fits on one page, and includes one honest limit.</li><li>If you can't say it in 30 seconds, it isn't clear yet.</li></ul> </div></div>
