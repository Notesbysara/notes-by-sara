## Five rules for a clear chart

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>One chart, one message</h3></div> <p>If you need to explain three things, make three charts. A chart that tries to say everything says nothing clearly.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Write the finding as the title</h3></div> <p>The title is the first thing people read, so let it carry the message.</p><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> Not “Sales by quarter,” but “Sales grew every quarter.”</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>Remove clutter</h3></div> <p>Heavy gridlines, borders, 3D effects, shadows and a legend when you could label the data directly. If removing something changes nothing, remove it.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">4</span><h3>Be careful with the axis</h3></div> <p>Bar charts should start at zero, because the length of the bar is the message. Cutting the axis can make a small difference look huge. Line charts can start higher, as long as the axis is clearly labeled.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">5</span><h3>Use color on purpose</h3></div> <p>Make most things gray, and use one strong color for what you want people to notice. About 1 in 12 men has some form of color blindness (and about 1 in 200 women), most often trouble telling red from green, so don&#39;t rely on those two colors alone to carry the message.</p> </div> </div> <p className="tip"><b className="lab">The five-second test</b> Show your chart to someone for five seconds, then hide it. Ask what it was about. If they can't tell you, the chart isn't finished.</p> <p className="tip"><b className="lab">In Excel</b> Sort your data before you insert the chart. For a column chart, sort from largest to smallest. For a horizontal bar chart, sort from smallest to largest, because Excel draws the first row at the bottom (or tick “Categories in reverse order” under Format Axis). To remove gridlines or add data labels, select the chart and use the Chart Elements (+) button next to it.</p></div>

## When a chart isn't the answer

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Use a table for exact numbers</h3></div> <p>Charts show patterns. Tables show exact values. If people need to look up or copy specific figures, compare many measures in different units, or check a total, a clean table works better.</p><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> An income statement is almost always a table. A chart of the same lines would hide the numbers people need.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Be careful with two y-axes</h3></div> <p>A chart with a different scale on each side can mislead. Where the lines cross, and how steep each looks, depends largely on how each axis was set, so the chart can suggest a link that isn't there.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> Show two charts side by side, or chart one ratio instead, such as margin rather than sales and profit on separate axes.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>Try small multiples</h3></div> <p>Instead of one chart with many crowded lines, draw several small charts, one per region or product, all with the same axes. The eye compares them easily.</p> </div> </div></div>

## What this looks like at work

<div className="nbs-sec work"><p>Charts are how most people will see your work. For example:</p> <ul> <li>You turn a monthly report into a few slides for a meeting.</li> <li>A senior manager has two minutes to read your summary.</li> <li>You're asked to “make the dashboard clearer.”</li> </ul> <p>Each time, ask three things before you insert a chart: what's the question, which chart answers it, and what will the title say?</p></div>

## How you'd say it to your manager

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>When you share a chart, say what it shows in one sentence, so the reader knows where to look.</p> <div className="msg"> <p className="eyebrow">Example message</p> <p className="body">Hi Maya, I've attached the regional sales chart for the board deck. North brings in about a third of our sales, and West is the smallest at about 17%. I switched it from a pie to a sorted bar chart so the ranking is easier to read. Happy to add last year's figures beside it if that would help.</p> </div> <p className="small">Notice it states the finding first, explains the one design change, and offers a useful next step.</p></div>

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="remember"> <p className="eyebrow">Remember</p> <ul> <li>Start with the question, then pick the chart.</li> <li>Line for time, bar for comparison, stacked bar for parts, scatter for relationships, histogram for spread.</li> <li>Write the finding as the title, and check the numbers behind it.</li> <li>Bar charts start at zero. Use color to point, not to decorate.</li> <li>Use the five-second test before you share.</li> </ul> </div></div>
