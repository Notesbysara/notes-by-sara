Topic 4 of 12 · Step 2: Inquire

Ask a question worth answering

“Can you take a look at the numbers?” is one of the most common requests in finance, and one of the hardest to answer well.

What you'll be able to do: turn a vague request into a sharp question tied to a decision, and break it into parts you can test. You'll also define each metric clearly and agree on the plan before you start.

A vague question can keep you busy for weeks and still leave everyone unsure what you found. A sharp question tells you what data to pull, what to calculate and when you're done. Your analysis can only be as good as the question behind it.

Four tests for a good question​

A well-formed business question is a specific, measurable question about a defined number and time period, whose answer will inform a particular decision.

Check your question against these four tests before you start.

1

Is it specific?

It names exactly what you're looking at: which number, which part of the business.

Example “How are sales?” is broad. “How did sales at store S03 change?” is specific.

2

Is it measurable?

You know which number would answer it, and you can get that data. Teams often track their most important numbers as KPIs.

Example “Are customers happy?” is hard to measure. “Did repeat purchases fall this quarter?” can be checked in the sales data.

3

Is it tied to a decision?

Someone will do something differently depending on the answer. If nothing would change, the question probably isn't worth the time. The answer should also be worth more than the effort: two weeks of analysis to save $500 usually isn't.

Example “…so we can decide whether to raise prices next year.”

4

Is it bounded in time?

It names the period you're looking at, and ideally when the answer is needed.

Example “…between 2023 and 2024, before the budget meeting in January.”

Here's what that looks like on four everyday questions:

VagueSharp
“Are we doing well?”“Did our operating margin in 2024 reach our 10% target, and should we change prices for 2025?”
“Is marketing working?”“Did the spring email campaign bring in more extra profit than it cost, between March and May?”
“Can you prove the campaign worked?”“Did sales in the spring campaign weeks (March to May) rise more than in similar weeks without it, so we can decide whether to run it again?”
“Should we worry about cash?”“Will our cash at the end of each month cover at least three months of fixed costs over the next six months?”

Notice the “prove the campaign worked” question. It assumes the answer before you start. A neutral question lets the data decide.

Four kinds of question​

Most business questions fall into one of four kinds. Knowing which one you're asking tells you what work it needs.

KindIt asksExampleUsually needs
DescriptiveWhat happened?How much did sales grow last year?Totals, comparisons
DiagnosticWhy did it happen?Why did margin fall?Breaking the number into its parts
PredictiveWhat is likely to happen?What will sales be next quarter?Trends, forecasts
PrescriptiveWhat should we do?Should we raise prices?All of the above, plus judgment

Many real questions combine two: “why did it happen, and what should we do?”

Break a big question into smaller ones​

A hypothesis is a possible explanation that can be tested with data. A hypothesis tree splits one main question into possible causes, then splits those again until each one can be tested.

Start with the main question at the top. Below it, list the possible reasons. Keep splitting until each branch tells you exactly which data to look at.

MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive) means the branches don't overlap and, together, cover every likely cause.

In plain words: no double counting and no gaps. Real causes can still interact, so treat MECE as the aim when you build the tree, not a guarantee.

Tip Write each branch as something data could prove wrong. “Costs went up” is too loose. “Cost per unit rose more than 5% between 2023 and 2024” can be tested.

Define every metric​

An operational definition states exactly how a metric is measured: what is included and excluded, which data it comes from, and which period it covers.

Two people can calculate “sales” and get different answers, and both believe they're right. One counts sales before discounts, the other after. One includes refunds, the other doesn't. Agree on the definition before the work starts, and most of those arguments never happen.

MetricLooseDefined
Sales“Sales this year”Sales after discounts and refunds, before sales tax, for January to December, from the general ledger
Operating margin“Our margin”Operating income ÷ sales, both from the year-end income statement

Tip Put the definitions in your question brief. If a number is ever challenged, you can point to exactly how it was calculated.

Let's see it in action​

The shop's owner says: “Sales are up, but profit feels lower. Can you look into it?” That's a worry, not a question yet. First, we check the numbers:

+10%Sales: $2.10m → $2.31m
9% → 8%Operating margin

Operating income actually fell slightly, from $189k (9% of $2.10m) to about $185k (8% of $2.31m), even though sales grew by $210k. So the owner is right. Now we look at where each $100 of sales went:

Where each $100 of sales went

Running costs (all the other costs of operating the shop, fixed and variable) stayed at 31% of sales. All of the drop shows up in cost of goods, which rose from 60% to 61% of sales. That tells us where to look. For a shop, cost of goods is mainly what it paid suppliers for the items it sold, plus costs such as delivery to the store.

The sharp question

“Why did our operating margin fall from 9% to 8% this year, and should we change our prices, suppliers or stock controls before next year's budget?”

Now the hypothesis tree. Cost of goods can rise as a share of sales for two broad reasons, and each splits again:

Why did cost of goods rise from 60% to 61% of sales?
↓
We earn less per item, on average
Each item sold costs us more
↓
Prices were cut or discounted moreData: average selling price by product
Customers bought more low-margin productsData: sales and margin by product
Suppliers charged moreData: purchase cost per unit
More stock was damaged or lostData: stock write-offs (if they're counted in cost of goods)

Notice that a price cut and a supplier price rise both push cost of goods up as a share of sales. The 61% tells us which side of the business to look at, but not which leaf is the cause. Only the data under each leaf can tell them apart. (Measuring how much of a change comes from price and how much from product mix is part of price-volume-mix analysis, which comes in Topic 7.) The tree covers the most likely causes, not every possible one: higher delivery costs, or a change in how costs are classified, could also move the number.

Tip Test first the leaf that's most likely to matter and quickest to check. Here, supplier prices are a good start: a look at purchase invoices shows quickly whether cost per unit rose.

Finally, we write it all on one page so everyone agrees before the work starts:

Question brief
QuestionWhy did operating margin fall from 9% to 8% this year?
Decision it informsWhether to change prices, suppliers or stock controls in next year's budget
Who decidesThe owner
MeasureOperating margin, and cost of goods as a % of sales
Time frameThis year vs last year; answer needed before the budget meeting
Possible causesPrice cuts, product mix, supplier costs, stock losses
Data neededPrices, sales and cost per unit by product; stock write-offs
Data available?Prices, sales and purchase costs: yes, in the shop's systems. Stock write-offs: ask the warehouse team.
Done whenWe can say how much of the 1-point fall each cause explains

This is an invented shop. All numbers are examples.

Try it yourself (optional)

Your manager asks: “Is the online shop worth it?” Rewrite it as a sharp question that passes the four tests.

Show the answer

One good version: “Did the online shop earn more gross profit than it cost to run between January and June this year, so we can decide whether to keep it in next year's budget?”

It's specific (the online shop), measurable (gross profit and running costs), tied to a decision (keep it or not) and bounded in time (January to June, before the budget). Your version can be different, as long as it passes all four tests.

What this looks like at work​

You'll often be handed a worry rather than a question. For example:

  • Your manager says, “Costs look high this month. Can you check?”
  • In a meeting, someone asks why one region is behind the others.
  • You're asked to “pull some numbers” for a presentation.

Each time, spend five minutes turning it into a sharp question and confirm it with the person who asked. It saves days of work on the wrong thing.

How you'd say it to your manager​

Before you start, check that you're answering the question they actually have.

Example message

Hi Maya, before I dig in, I'd like to check I'm answering the right question. I'm planning to look at why operating margin fell from 9% to 8% this year, so we can decide on prices, suppliers or stock controls before the budget. The drop shows up in cost of goods, so I'll start there. Does that match what you need, and when do you need it by?

Notice it states the question, the decision it supports, where you'll start, and asks for a deadline.

Remember

  • Your analysis can only be as good as your question.
  • A good question is specific, measurable, tied to a decision and bounded in time.
  • Break big questions into a hypothesis tree, aiming for no overlaps and no gaps.
  • A ratio points you to where to look. The detailed data tells you why.
  • Agree on the question with the person who asked before you start.
