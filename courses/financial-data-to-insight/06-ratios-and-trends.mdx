## What a ratio is

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="def"><p>A <b>ratio</b> divides one number by another, so that things of different sizes can be compared. Using ratios to judge a business is called <Term slug="ratio-analysis" name="Ratio Analysis" def="Ratio analysis is the use of relationships between figures in a company's financial statements, expressed as ratios, to assess its profitability, liquidity, efficiency, debt and value.">ratio analysis</Term>.</p></div> <p>Ratios fall into four families, each answering one question. Below, every example uses this year's numbers for the shop from the earlier topics.</p> <div className="tw small-t"><table><tbody><tr><th>The shop, this year</th><th></th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>$ thousands</th></tr><tr><td>Revenue</td><td></td><td className="r">2,310</td></tr><tr><td>Cost of goods sold (61%)</td><td></td><td className="r">1,409</td></tr><tr><td>Operating income (8%)</td><td></td><td className="r">185</td></tr><tr><td>Interest expense</td><td></td><td className="r">10</td></tr><tr><td>Tax</td><td></td><td className="r">35</td></tr><tr><td>Net profit</td><td></td><td className="r">140</td></tr><tr><td>Cash</td><td></td><td className="r">340</td></tr><tr><td>Money owed by customers (last year 50)</td><td></td><td className="r">120</td></tr><tr><td>Inventory (last year 300)</td><td></td><td className="r">330</td></tr><tr><td>Current assets</td><td></td><td className="r">790</td></tr><tr><td>Current liabilities</td><td></td><td className="r">400</td></tr><tr><td>Debt (the $50k due within a year is also counted in current liabilities)</td><td></td><td className="r">150</td></tr><tr><td>Equity</td><td></td><td className="r">700</td></tr><tr><td>Total assets (last year 1,100)</td><td></td><td className="r">1,200</td></tr></tbody></table></div></div>

## The four families

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Profitability: how well does it turn sales into profit?</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>Profitability ratios compare profit with revenue or with the money invested.</p></div><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Ratio</th><th>Formula</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>The shop</th></tr><tr><td><Term slug="gross-margin" name="Gross Margin" def="Gross margin is gross profit expressed as a percentage of revenue. It shows how much of each unit of sales is left after paying the direct costs of the goods or services sold.">Gross margin</Term></td><td>Gross profit ÷ revenue</td><td className="r">39%</td></tr><tr><td><Term slug="operating-margin" name="Operating Margin" def="Operating margin is operating income divided by revenue, showing the percentage of each sale that remains as profit after paying for the costs of running the business, before interest and tax.">Operating margin</Term></td><td>Operating income ÷ revenue</td><td className="r">8%</td></tr><tr><td><Term slug="net-profit-margin" name="Net Profit Margin" def="Net profit margin is net income divided by revenue, showing the percentage of each sale that remains as profit after all expenses, interest and taxes.">Net profit margin</Term></td><td>Net profit ÷ revenue</td><td className="r">6.1%</td></tr><tr><td><Term slug="return-on-equity" name="Return on Equity (ROE)" def="Return on equity (ROE) is a company's net income divided by its shareholders' equity. It shows how much profit the business earns for each unit of money its owners have invested.">Return on equity</Term></td><td>Net profit ÷ equity</td><td className="r">20%</td></tr><tr><td><Term slug="return-on-assets" name="Return on Assets (ROA)" def="Return on assets (ROA) is net income divided by total assets, showing how much profit a company generates from everything it owns.">Return on assets</Term></td><td>Net profit ÷ total assets</td><td className="r">11.7%</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> The shop keeps about 6 cents of profit from every dollar of sales.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> Return on equity and return on assets are often calculated with averages over the year. Here we use year-end figures to keep it simple.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Liquidity: can it pay its bills soon?</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>Liquidity ratios compare what the business can turn into cash soon with what it must pay soon.</p></div><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Ratio</th><th>Formula</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>The shop</th></tr><tr><td><Term slug="current-ratio" name="Current Ratio" def="The current ratio is current assets divided by current liabilities, showing whether a company has enough short-term resources to cover its short-term obligations.">Current ratio</Term></td><td>Current assets ÷ current liabilities</td><td className="r">1.98</td></tr><tr><td><Term slug="quick-ratio" name="Quick Ratio" def="The quick ratio is a company's most liquid current assets, typically cash, short-term investments and receivables, divided by its current liabilities. It tests whether short-term obligations could be met without selling inventory.">Quick ratio</Term></td><td>(Cash + money owed by customers) ÷ current liabilities</td><td className="r">1.15</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> The shop has about $1.98 of short-term assets for every $1 it owes soon. Without inventory, which can be slow to sell, that falls to $1.15.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> Above 1 means more short-term assets than short-term bills, but what counts as healthy depends on the industry.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>Leverage: how much does it rely on debt?</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>Leverage ratios show how much of the business is funded by borrowing, and how easily it can pay the interest.</p></div><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Ratio</th><th>Formula</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>The shop</th></tr><tr><td><Term slug="debt-to-equity-ratio" name="Debt-to-Equity Ratio" def="The debt-to-equity ratio is a company's total debt divided by its shareholders' equity, showing how much it relies on borrowed money compared with owners' money.">Debt-to-equity</Term></td><td>Debt ÷ equity</td><td className="r">0.21</td></tr><tr><td><Term slug="interest-coverage-ratio" name="Interest Coverage Ratio" def="The interest coverage ratio is a company's operating profit, usually EBIT, divided by its interest expense. It shows how many times over the company can pay its interest from its earnings.">Interest coverage</Term></td><td>Operating income ÷ interest</td><td className="r">18.5×</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> Operating income covers the interest bill about 18 times over, so the shop is not stretched by its debt.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> A business with very low debt is not automatically better. Some borrowing can help it grow.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">4</span><h3>Efficiency: how well does it use what it owns?</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>Efficiency ratios show how hard the business works its stock and other assets.</p></div><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Ratio</th><th>Formula</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>The shop</th></tr><tr><td><Term slug="inventory-turnover" name="Inventory Turnover" def="Inventory turnover is the number of times a business sells and replaces its inventory during a period, usually calculated as cost of goods sold divided by average inventory.">Inventory turnover</Term></td><td>Cost of goods sold ÷ average inventory</td><td className="r">4.5×</td></tr><tr><td><Term slug="asset-turnover" name="Asset Turnover" def="Asset turnover is revenue divided by total assets, showing how much sales a company generates for each unit of assets it owns.">Asset turnover</Term></td><td>Revenue ÷ average total assets</td><td className="r">2.0×</td></tr><tr><td><Term slug="days-sales-outstanding" name="Days Sales Outstanding" def="Days sales outstanding (DSO) is the average number of days it takes a business to collect payment from its customers after a sale.">Days to collect from customers (DSO)</Term></td><td>Money owed by customers ÷ revenue × 365</td><td className="r">19 days</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> The shop sells through its stock about 4.5 times a year, so an item sits on the shelf for about 82 days on average (365 ÷ 4.47, using the unrounded figure).</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> Some sources use year-end figures instead of averages. Whichever you use, use it the same way every year.</p><p>Days to collect uses year-end figures here. Last year it was about 9 days (50 ÷ 2,100 × 365), so customers are taking more than twice as long to pay. That's the late-payment problem from Topic 5, showing up in a ratio. Because most of the shop's customers pay at the register, the figure for business customers alone would be much higher.</p> </div> </div></div>

## A ratio needs a yardstick

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>A ratio on its own still isn't an answer. Compare it with the same business in earlier years, and with similar businesses in the same industry. Comparing with competitors is called <Term slug="benchmarking" name="Benchmarking" def="Benchmarking is comparing a business's performance, processes or costs with those of competitors, industry averages or recognized best performers, to identify gaps and opportunities for improvement.">benchmarking</Term>.</p> <p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> Grocery chains usually earn thin margins and sell their stock fast. Software companies often earn much higher margins and hold almost no stock. Neither is “better”; they're different businesses. That's why Project 01 compares two similar retailers, Walmart and Target.</p></div>

## Reading a trend

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Year-over-year growth</h3></div> <div className="def"><p><Term slug="year-over-year-growth" name="Year-over-Year Growth" def="Year-over-year growth compares a figure for one period with the same period a year earlier, expressed as a percentage change."><b>Year-over-year growth</b></Term> is the change from last year to this year, as a percentage of last year.</p></div><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><td>Formula</td><td>(This year − last year) ÷ last year</td><td className="r"></td></tr><tr><td>The shop's sales</td><td>(2.31 − 2.10) ÷ 2.10</td><td className="r">10%</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p className="tip"><b className="lab">In Excel</b> With the yearly sales in B2 to E2 (2021 to 2024), this year's growth is =(E2-D2)/D2. Format the cell as a percentage.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Growth over several years</h3></div> <div className="def"><p><Term slug="compound-annual-growth-rate" name="Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR)" def="The compound annual growth rate (CAGR) is the steady yearly growth rate that would take a value from its starting point to its ending point over a number of years, assuming growth compounds each year."><b>Compound annual growth rate (CAGR)</b></Term> is the steady yearly growth rate that would take a value from its starting point to its ending point.</p></div><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><td>Formula</td><td>(End ÷ start)^(1 ÷ years) − 1</td><td className="r"></td></tr><tr><td>The shop's sales, 2021 to 2024</td><td>(2.31 ÷ 1.62)^(1 ÷ 3) − 1</td><td className="r">12.6% a year</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>The actual growth was uneven (11%, 17%, then 10%), but 12.6% a year, compounded for three years, gets you to the same place.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">In Excel</b> With the same layout, =(E2/B2)^(1/3)-1, where 3 is the number of years between the first and last value, not the number of values.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>Common-size statements</h3></div> <div className="def"><p><Term slug="common-size-analysis" name="Common-Size Analysis" def="Common-size analysis expresses every line of a financial statement as a percentage of a single base figure, such as revenue for the income statement or total assets for the balance sheet."><b>Common-size analysis</b></Term> shows every line of a statement as a percentage of one base figure: revenue for the income statement, or total assets for the balance sheet.</p></div><div className="tw small-t"><table><tbody><tr><th>Per $100 of sales</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Last year</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>This year</th></tr><tr><td>Cost of goods sold</td><td className="r">$60</td><td className="r">$61</td></tr><tr><td>Running costs</td><td className="r">$31</td><td className="r">$31</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Operating income</td><td className="r">$9</td><td className="r">$8</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>Both cost lines grew in dollars along with sales, so they look harmless. As a percentage of sales, you can see straight away where the margin went, just like in Topic 4.</p> </div> </div></div>

## Look behind the ratio

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>A ratio gives you a clue, not the whole story. The story is in the numbers that built it.</p> <p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> The shop's current ratio of 1.98 looks comfortable. But part of it comes from $30k more inventory and $70k more money owed by customers, while cash fell by $60k. The ratio looks healthy partly because money got stuck, not because the shop got richer.</p> <p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> Whenever a ratio changes, check whether the top number, the bottom number, or both moved, and why.</p></div>

<div className="nbs-sec try"><p className="eyebrow">Try it yourself (optional)</p> <p>At the start of the year, the shop's current assets were $750k and its current liabilities were $390k. Work out last year's current ratio. Is this year's 1.98 really an improvement?</p> <details><summary>Show the answer</summary><p>Last year: 750 ÷ 390 = 1.92. So the ratio rose slightly, from 1.92 to 1.98.</p><p>But look behind it: cash fell by $60k, while money owed by customers rose $70k and inventory rose $30k. More of the shop's short-term assets are now stuck in unpaid bills and stock. The ratio went up, but the shop's ability to pay its bills from cash got weaker.</p></details></div>

## What this looks like at work

<div className="nbs-sec work"><p>Ratios and trends are everyday tools in finance. For example:</p> <ul> <li>A monthly report shows margins and growth compared with last year and the budget.</li> <li>A bank checks a company's interest coverage before lending to it.</li> <li>You compare a company with its competitors before an investment discussion.</li> </ul> <p>Each time, show the ratio, the yardstick, and what moved underneath.</p></div>

## How you'd say it to your manager

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Give the ratio, the comparison, and the reason in one breath.</p> <div className="msg"> <p className="eyebrow">Example message</p> <p className="body">Hi Maya, sales grew 10% this year, and 12.6% a year since 2021. Operating margin slipped from 9% to 8% because cost of goods rose to 61% of sales. Our current ratio looks healthy at 1.98, but that's partly stock and unpaid invoices building up, so I'd watch cash rather than the ratio. Debt isn't a concern: operating income covers interest about 18 times.</p> </div> <p className="small">Notice each number comes with a comparison and an explanation.</p></div>

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="remember"> <p className="eyebrow">Remember</p> <ul> <li>A ratio lets you compare businesses of different sizes.</li> <li>Four families: profitability, liquidity, leverage and efficiency.</li> <li>Always compare a ratio with past years and with similar businesses.</li> <li>Use year-over-year growth for one year, CAGR for several.</li> <li>When a ratio moves, look at the numbers that built it.</li> </ul> </div></div>
