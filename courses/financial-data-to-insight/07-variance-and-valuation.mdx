## Part 1: Variance

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="def"><p><Term slug="budget-variance" name="Budget Variance" def="A budget variance is the difference between a budgeted amount and the actual result, such as actual sales or costs compared with what was planned.">A <b>budget variance</b></Term> is the difference between a budgeted amount and the actual result. It is usually written as actual minus budget, and also shown as a percentage of the budget.</p></div> <p>In statistics, “variance” means something different: how spread out a set of numbers is. In finance reports, it almost always means actual against plan, forecast or last year.</p> <p>Here are the shop's results for this year against its budget:</p> <div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>$ thousands</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Budget</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Actual</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Variance</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}></th><th></th></tr><tr><td>Revenue</td><td className="r">2,250</td><td className="r">2,310</td><td className="r">+60</td><td className="r">+2.7%</td><td>Favorable</td></tr><tr><td>Cost of goods sold</td><td className="r">1,350</td><td className="r">1,409</td><td className="r">+59</td><td className="r">+4.4%</td><td>Unfavorable</td></tr><tr><td>Running costs</td><td className="r">700</td><td className="r">716</td><td className="r">+16</td><td className="r">+2.3%</td><td>Unfavorable</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Operating income</td><td className="r">200</td><td className="r">185</td><td className="r">−15</td><td className="r">−7.5%</td><td>Unfavorable</td></tr></tbody></table></div> <div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Label it favorable or unfavorable</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>A <b>favorable variance</b> improves profit, such as revenue above budget or costs below budget. An <b>unfavorable variance</b> reduces profit.</p></div><p>The sign alone doesn&#39;t tell you if it&#39;s good news. Revenue +60 is good. Costs +59 is bad. Always add the label.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> In Excel, =IF(C2&gt;B2,"Favorable","Unfavorable") works for revenue rows. For cost rows, flip it. A zero variance shows as Unfavorable, so check those by hand.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Ask why: split price and volume</h3></div> <p>A variance is a question, not an answer. A useful first split is <b>price</b> (did we charge or pay a different price?) and <b>volume</b> (did we sell or buy a different amount?).</p><div className="tw small-t"><table><tbody><tr><th>Shoes</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Budget</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Actual</th></tr><tr><td>Pairs sold</td><td className="r">12,000</td><td className="r">12,500</td></tr><tr><td>Price per pair</td><td className="r">$65</td><td className="r">$63</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Revenue</td><td className="r">$780,000</td><td className="r">$787,500</td></tr></tbody></table></div><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Split of the +$7,500 variance</th><th>How it's worked out</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Effect</th></tr><tr><td>Volume: we sold more</td><td>(12,500 − 12,000) × $65</td><td className="r">+$32,500 favorable</td></tr><tr><td>Price: we charged less</td><td>($63 − $65) × 12,500</td><td className="r">−$25,000 unfavorable</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Total</td><td></td><td className="r">+$7,500</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> Shoe revenue beat the budget, but only because volume made up for a price cut. That&#39;s a very different story from “shoes did well.”</p><p>The same split works for costs. Did we pay a different price per item (a price variance), or buy or use a different amount (a quantity or usage variance)?</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>Check if the budget still fits</h3></div> <p>Some cost variances happen simply because you sold more. Selling more means buying more inventory, so cost of goods rises too.</p><div className="def"><p>A <Term slug="flexible-budget" name="Flexible Budget" def="A flexible budget is a budget that adjusts to the actual level of activity, such as units produced or sold, so that actual results can be compared fairly with what costs should have been at that level."><b>flexible budget</b></Term> adjusts the budget to the actual level of activity, so you can see which variances come from volume and which from everything else.</p></div><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> The budget assumed cost of goods would be 60% of sales. At the actual sales of $2,310k, that&#39;s $1,386k. So $36k of the $59k cost variance comes from the higher sales level, and only $23k comes from costs rising to 61% of sales, the problem Topic 4 investigated.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> You can&#39;t explain every variance. Many teams agree on a rule, such as explaining anything over 5% or $10,000, and focus there.</p> </div> </div></div>

## Part 2: Costs, break-even and forecasts

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Variance looks back. To plan ahead, you first need to know how costs behave when sales go up or down.</p> <div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Split costs into fixed and variable</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>A <Term slug="fixed-cost" name="Fixed Cost" def="A fixed cost is a cost that stays the same in total regardless of how much a business produces or sells, at least within a certain range of activity and time period."><b>fixed cost</b></Term>, such as rent, doesn't change when sales do, as long as the business stays at about its usual size. A <Term slug="variable-cost" name="Variable Cost" def="A variable cost is a cost that changes in total in direct proportion to the amount a business produces or sells."><b>variable cost</b></Term> rises and falls with sales, such as the cost of the goods you sell.</p></div><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Shop costs this year, $ thousands</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Amount</th><th>Type</th></tr><tr><td>Cost of goods sold</td><td className="r">1,409</td><td>Variable</td></tr><tr><td>Card fees, delivery and other costs that move with sales</td><td className="r">216</td><td>Variable</td></tr><tr><td>Rent, wages and bills</td><td className="r">480</td><td>Fixed</td></tr><tr><td>Depreciation</td><td className="r">20</td><td>Fixed</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Total costs before interest and tax</td><td className="r">2,125</td><td></td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>Sales of 2,310 minus these costs of 2,125 gives the same operating income as before: 185.</p><p>In reality, many costs are <Term slug="semi-variable-cost" name="Semi-Variable Cost" def="A semi-variable cost is a cost that has both a fixed part, paid regardless of activity, and a variable part that changes with the level of activity.">semi-variable</Term>. Wages, for example, rise with overtime in busy months. For planning, a simple split is usually good enough, as long as you write down the assumptions.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Work out the contribution margin</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>The <Term slug="contribution-margin-ratio" name="Contribution Margin Ratio" def="The contribution margin ratio is the contribution margin divided by the selling price, showing the percentage of each sale available to cover fixed costs and generate profit."><b>contribution margin ratio</b></Term> is the share of each sales dollar left after variable costs. It pays for fixed costs first, and anything above that is profit.</p></div><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>$ thousands</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Amount</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>% of sales</th></tr><tr><td>Sales</td><td className="r">2,310</td><td className="r">100%</td></tr><tr><td>Variable costs (1,409 + 216)</td><td className="r">(1,625)</td><td className="r">70.3%</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Contribution</td><td className="r">685</td><td className="r">29.7%</td></tr><tr><td>Fixed costs (480 + 20)</td><td className="r">(500)</td><td className="r"></td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Operating income</td><td className="r">185</td><td className="r"></td></tr></tbody></table></div><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> Of every extra $100 of sales, about $29.70 is left after variable costs. Once fixed costs are covered, that $29.70 is extra operating income.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>Find the break-even point</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>The <b>break-even point</b> is the level of sales at which contribution exactly covers fixed costs, so operating income is zero. Working it out is called <Term slug="break-even-analysis" name="Break-Even Analysis" def="Break-even analysis works out the level of sales at which a business's total revenue exactly covers its total costs, so it makes neither a profit nor a loss.">break-even analysis</Term>.</p></div><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><td>Formula</td><td>Break-even sales = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio</td></tr><tr><td>For the shop</td><td>500 ÷ 0.2965 = about 1,686 ($ thousands)</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>The shop breaks even at about $1.69 million of sales a year. It sells $2.31 million, so sales could fall about 27% before it would make an operating loss. That cushion is called the margin of safety.</p><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> Back to Topic 1's new store. Suppose it would add $120k a year of fixed costs (rent, wages and bills), more than the $80k average per store, because new sites usually cost more to rent and earn the same 29.7% contribution margin. It would need about $405k of sales a year just to break even (120 ÷ 0.2965). The shop's six stores average $385k each (2,310 ÷ 6). So the new store would have to beat the average store just to cover its own costs, before paying back the $250k it cost to open.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">In Excel</b> Put fixed costs and the contribution margin ratio in their own labeled cells, and calculate break-even from them. Then you can change one assumption and watch the answer move.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">4</span><h3>Build a simple forecast with scenarios</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>A <Term slug="forecasting" name="Forecasting" def="Forecasting is the process of estimating future financial results, such as sales, costs, profits or cash flows, based on past data, current trends and assumptions about what will happen."><b>forecast</b></Term> is your best estimate of future results, built from stated assumptions. A budget is a target the business commits to. A forecast is updated as things change.</p></div><p>A useful forecast starts from a few drivers, such as sales growth and how costs behave. It shows more than one case, because nobody knows the future. Here we assume the contribution margin stays at 29.65% (685 ÷ 2,310, shown as 29.7%), fixed costs rise 3% to $515k, and growth slows as the stores mature, so the base case is 8%.</p><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Next year, $ thousands</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Sales growth</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Sales</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Contribution</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Fixed costs</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Operating income</th></tr><tr><td>Worst case</td><td className="r">0%</td><td className="r">2,310</td><td className="r">685</td><td className="r">(515)</td><td className="r">170</td></tr><tr><td>Base case</td><td className="r">8%</td><td className="r">2,495</td><td className="r">740</td><td className="r">(515)</td><td className="r">225</td></tr><tr><td>Best case</td><td className="r">12%</td><td className="r">2,587</td><td className="r">767</td><td className="r">(515)</td><td className="r">252</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>Notice that operating income moves faster than sales. In the base case, sales grow 8%, but operating income grows about 22% (185 to 225), because fixed costs barely change. This is <Term slug="operating-leverage" name="Operating Leverage" def="Operating leverage is the degree to which a company's costs are fixed rather than variable, which determines how strongly its operating profit changes when sales change.">operating leverage</Term>, and it works in reverse too: in the worst case, flat sales and slightly higher fixed costs cut operating income by about 8%.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Watch out</b> The base case is your most honest estimate, not the most hopeful one. Check growth against history (between 10% and 17% a year over the last three years here), and write down every assumption.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> Compare the forecast with actual results each month or quarter, and update it. Many companies keep a <Term slug="rolling-forecast" name="Rolling Forecast" def="A rolling forecast is a forecast that is updated regularly, typically every month or quarter, by adding a new period at the end as each period passes, so it always looks a fixed time ahead.">rolling forecast</Term> that always looks the same distance ahead.</p> </div> </div> <p className="small">All numbers are examples.</p></div>

## Part 3: Valuation in plain words

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Valuation starts from an idea you already believe: a dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year. You could invest today's dollar, and next year's dollar might not arrive. This is called the <Term slug="time-value-of-money" name="Time Value of Money" def="The time value of money is the idea that money available today is worth more than the same amount received in the future, because money today can be invested to earn a return.">time value of money</Term>.</p> <div className="def"><p><Term slug="present-value" name="Present Value" def="Present value is what a future amount of money is worth today, after allowing for the return that money could earn in the meantime."><b>Present value</b></Term> is what a future amount of money is worth today, after allowing for the return that money could earn in the meantime.</p></div> <div className="def"><p>A <Term slug="discount-rate" name="Discount Rate" def="A discount rate is the interest rate used to convert future cash flows into their value today. A higher discount rate makes future money worth less in today's terms."><b>discount rate</b></Term> is the rate used to convert future cash into its value today. It reflects the return you could get elsewhere, plus extra for risk.</p></div><p>Inside companies, the discount rate is often based on the <Term slug="weighted-average-cost-of-capital" name="Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)" def="The weighted average cost of capital (WACC) is the average rate of return a company must pay to all its funders, lenders and shareholders, weighted by how much of each type of funding it uses.">weighted average cost of capital (WACC)</Term>: the blended return that the company's lenders and owners expect. A riskier project usually gets a higher rate.</p> <div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><td>Formula</td><td>Present value = Cash ÷ (1 + r)^t</td></tr><tr><td>r</td><td>The discount rate, for example 10% = 0.10</td></tr><tr><td>t</td><td>How many years until the cash arrives</td></tr></tbody></table></div> <div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Discount each year's cash</h3></div> <div className="def"><p><Term slug="discounted-cash-flow" name="Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)" def="Discounted cash flow (DCF) is a valuation method that estimates the value of an investment or company by forecasting the cash it will generate in the future and discounting that cash back to its value today."><b>Discounted cash flow (DCF)</b></Term> values a business or investment by forecasting its future cash flows and converting each one to its value today.</p></div><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Year</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Cash expected</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Divide by</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Worth today at 10%</th></tr><tr><td>1</td><td className="r">100</td><td className="r">1.10</td><td className="r">90.91</td></tr><tr><td>2</td><td className="r">110</td><td className="r">1.21</td><td className="r">90.91</td></tr><tr><td>3</td><td className="r">120</td><td className="r">1.331</td><td className="r">90.16</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Total</td><td className="r">330</td><td className="r"></td><td className="r">271.98 ≈ 272</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>The 330 of future cash is worth about 272 today. The later the cash arrives, the more it shrinks.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">In Excel</b> =NPV(10%,B2:B4) gives 271.98 for cash flows in B2 to B4, when the first one arrives one year from now. Despite its name, Excel&#39;s NPV doesn&#39;t subtract any upfront cost. Subtract that yourself to get net present value.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>See how much the discount rate matters</h3></div> <p>Change one assumption and the answer moves. ## What this looks like at work

<div className="nbs-sec work"><p>These tools come up all the time. For example:</p> <ul> <li>Every month, finance teams explain the biggest variances between actual results and the budget.</li><li>At budget time, and in quarterly reforecasts, teams use break-even and scenario forecasts to plan.</li> <li>Before a company buys equipment or another business, someone estimates whether the future cash is worth the price today.</li> <li>In interviews for finance roles, candidates are often asked to walk through a DCF.</li> </ul> <p>Each time, label the variance, find its cause, and show valuations as a range with the assumptions stated.</p></div>

## How you'd say it to your manager

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Lead with the biggest variance and its cause, then what you'd do.</p> <div className="msg"> <p className="eyebrow">Example message</p> <p className="body">Hi Maya, operating income came in $15k below budget at $185k. Revenue beat budget by $60k, but cost of goods was $59k over and running costs were $16k over. $36k of that is simply from selling more; the other $23k is cost of goods rising to 61% of sales. On shoes, we sold more pairs but at a lower average price, so volume covered a $25k price shortfall. I'd suggest we look at discounting on shoes first.</p> </div> <p className="small">Notice it gives the total, splits the main variance, and ends with a next step.</p></div>

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="remember"> <p className="eyebrow">Remember</p> <ul> <li>Variance = actual − budget. Label it favorable or unfavorable; the sign alone isn't enough.</li> <li>Split variances into price and volume to find the cause.</li> <li>Fixed costs stay put and variable costs move with sales. Break-even sales = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio.</li><li>Forecast with worst, base and best cases, and write down every assumption.</li><li>Present value = cash ÷ (1 + r)^t. Later cash and higher rates mean lower values today.</li> <li>Show valuations as a range with a sensitivity table, never as one confident number.</li> </ul> </div></div>
