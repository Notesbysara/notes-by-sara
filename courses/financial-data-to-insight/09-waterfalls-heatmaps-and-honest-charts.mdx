## The heatmap

A table of 16 or 40 numbers takes a while to scan. A heatmap shows the odd one out at once.</p> <div className="tw"><table style={{textAlign:"center"}}><tbody><tr><th style={{textAlign:"left"}}>Gross margin, %</th><th>Q1</th><th>Q2</th><th>Q3</th><th>Q4</th></tr><tr><td style={{textAlign:"left"}}>North</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.08)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>40</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.08)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>40</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.22)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>39</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.08)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>40</td></tr><tr><td style={{textAlign:"left"}}>South</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.22)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>39</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.22)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>39</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.90)",color:"#fff",fontWeight:"600"}}>34</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.36)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>38</td></tr><tr><td style={{textAlign:"left"}}>East</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.22)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>39</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.08)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>40</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.22)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>39</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.22)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>39</td></tr><tr><td style={{textAlign:"left"}}>West</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.22)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>39</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.36)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>38</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.22)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>39</td><td style={{background:"rgba(180,69,47,0.08)",color:"var(--ink)",fontWeight:"400"}}>40</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p className="small">Rows are in order of sales, largest first. ## Five ways charts mislead

If you need to show small changes, chart the change itself, or use a line chart with a clearly labeled axis.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>A convenient time window</h3></div> <p>Showing only the six months that went up, when the longer trend is down. Choose the period that answers the question, not the one that looks best, and show enough history to be fair.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>Two different y-axes</h3></div> <p>Two lines on two scales can be made to look related, or not, just by stretching one axis. Topic 8 covers safer alternatives. If you must use two axes, label both clearly.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">4</span><h3>3D and decoration</h3></div> <p>A 3D pie makes the slices at the front look bigger than they are. Shadows, textures and pictures add nothing and can distort. Keep charts flat and plain.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">5</span><h3>No comparison</h3></div> <p>A number on its own can look impressive. Add a yardstick: last year, the budget or a competitor. Without one, the reader can't tell whether the result is good.</p> </div> </div> <p className="tip"><b className="lab">The skeptic test</b> Before you share a chart, imagine the most skeptical person in the room. If the chart wouldn't survive their first question, change it. Your credibility is worth more than a dramatic picture.</p></div>

## What this looks like at work

<div className="nbs-sec work"><p>You'll meet both charts, and the honesty checks, all the time. For example:</p> <ul style={{margin:"0",paddingLeft:"20px",display:"flex",flexDirection:"column",gap:"4px"}}><li>A monthly report explains the gap between budget and actual profit with a waterfall.</li><li>A sales director wants to see which regions and months are weak, on one page.</li><li>A colleague's slide shows a dramatic jump, and you're asked whether it's real.</li></ul> <p>Each time, check that the waterfall adds up, that the heatmap's colors mean something, and that every chart would survive a skeptical question.</p></div>

## How you'd say it to your manager

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Share the chart with the one thing it shows, so the reader knows where to look.</p> <div className="msg"> <p className="eyebrow">Example message</p> <p className="body">Hi Maya, I've added a waterfall to the monthly pack showing how operating income went from the $200k budget to $185k. Revenue beat plan by $60k, but cost of goods ran $59k over and running costs $16k over. I've also attached a heatmap of gross margin by region and quarter. South in Q3 stands out at 34%, so I'll look into what happened there next.</p> </div> <p className="small">Notice it states what each chart shows and what you'll do next.</p></div>

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="remember"> <p className="eyebrow">Remember</p> <ul><li>A waterfall shows how you got from one number to another. Keep it to about seven steps or fewer, and make sure it adds up.</li><li>A heatmap shows patterns across two categories. Use one color scale, show the numbers, and order the rows meaningfully.</li><li>Check for five traps: cut axes, convenient time windows, two y-axes, 3D decoration and no comparison.</li><li>If a chart wouldn't survive a skeptical question, change it.</li></ul> </div></div>
