Ratios and trends

A raw number means little until you compare it with something.

What you'll be able to do: calculate the main ratios in each of the four families and explain what each says about a business. You'll also measure growth over one and several years, and check what moved behind every ratio.

A profit of $140k is a good result for a small shop and a disaster for a supermarket chain. Ratios let you compare businesses of different sizes, and trends let you compare a business with its own past. Together they're two of the tools analysts use most.

What a ratio is​

A ratio divides one number by another, so that things of different sizes can be compared. Using ratios to judge a business is called ratio analysis.

Ratios fall into four families, each answering one question. Below, every example uses this year's numbers for the shop from the earlier topics.

The four families​

1

Profitability: how well does it turn sales into profit?

Profitability ratios compare profit with revenue or with the money invested.

RatioFormulaThe shop
Gross marginGross profit ÷ revenue39%
Operating marginOperating income ÷ revenue8%
Net profit marginNet profit ÷ revenue6.1%
Return on equityNet profit ÷ equity20%
Return on assetsNet profit ÷ total assets11.7%

Example The shop keeps about 6 cents of profit from every dollar of sales.

Tip Return on equity and return on assets are often calculated with averages over the year. Here we use year-end figures to keep it simple.

2

Liquidity: can it pay its bills soon?

Liquidity ratios compare what the business can turn into cash soon with what it must pay soon.

RatioFormulaThe shop
Current ratioCurrent assets ÷ current liabilities1.98
Quick ratio(Cash + money owed by customers) ÷ current liabilities1.15

Example The shop has about $1.98 of short-term assets for every $1 it owes soon. Without inventory, which can be slow to sell, that falls to $1.15.

Tip Above 1 means more short-term assets than short-term bills, but what counts as healthy depends on the industry.

3

Leverage: how much does it rely on debt?

Leverage ratios show how much of the business is funded by borrowing, and how easily it can pay the interest.

RatioFormulaThe shop
Debt-to-equityDebt ÷ equity0.21
Interest coverageOperating income ÷ interest18.5×

Example Operating income covers the interest bill about 18 times over, so the shop is not stretched by its debt.

Tip A business with very low debt is not automatically better. Some borrowing can help it grow.

4

Efficiency: how well does it use what it owns?

Efficiency ratios show how hard the business works its stock and other assets.

RatioFormulaThe shop
Inventory turnoverCost of goods sold ÷ average inventory4.5×
Asset turnoverRevenue ÷ average total assets2.0×
Days to collect from customers (DSO)Money owed by customers ÷ revenue × 36519 days

Example The shop sells through its stock about 4.5 times a year, so an item sits on the shelf for about 82 days on average (365 ÷ 4.47, using the unrounded figure).

Tip Some sources use year-end figures instead of averages. Whichever you use, use it the same way every year.

Days to collect uses year-end figures here. Last year it was about 9 days (50 ÷ 2,100 × 365), so customers are taking more than twice as long to pay. That's the late-payment problem from Topic 5, showing up in a ratio. Because most of the shop's customers pay at the register, the figure for business customers alone would be much higher.

A ratio needs a yardstick​

A ratio on its own still isn't an answer. Compare it with the same business in earlier years, and with similar businesses in the same industry. Comparing with competitors is called benchmarking.

Example Grocery chains usually earn thin margins and sell their stock fast. Software companies often earn much higher margins and hold almost no stock. Neither is “better”; they're different businesses. That's why Project 01 compares two similar retailers, Walmart and Target.

Reading a trend​

1

Year-over-year growth

Year-over-year growth is the change from last year to this year, as a percentage of last year.

Formula(This year − last year) ÷ last year
The shop's sales(2.31 − 2.10) ÷ 2.1010%

In Excel With the yearly sales in B2 to E2 (2021 to 2024), this year's growth is =(E2-D2)/D2. Format the cell as a percentage.

2

Growth over several years

Compound annual growth rate (CAGR) is the steady yearly growth rate that would take a value from its starting point to its ending point.

Formula(End ÷ start)^(1 ÷ years) − 1
The shop's sales, 2021 to 2024(2.31 ÷ 1.62)^(1 ÷ 3) − 112.6% a year

The actual growth was uneven (11%, 17%, then 10%), but 12.6% a year, compounded for three years, gets you to the same place.

In Excel With the same layout, =(E2/B2)^(1/3)-1, where 3 is the number of years between the first and last value, not the number of values.

3

Common-size statements

Common-size analysis shows every line of a statement as a percentage of one base figure: revenue for the income statement, or total assets for the balance sheet.

Per $100 of salesLast yearThis year
Cost of goods sold$60$61
Running costs$31$31
Operating income$9$8

Both cost lines grew in dollars along with sales, so they look harmless. As a percentage of sales, you can see straight away where the margin went, just like in Topic 4.

Look behind the ratio​

A ratio gives you a clue, not the whole story. The story is in the numbers that built it.

Example The shop's current ratio of 1.98 looks comfortable. But part of it comes from $30k more inventory and $70k more money owed by customers, while cash fell by $60k. The ratio looks healthy partly because money got stuck, not because the shop got richer.

Tip Whenever a ratio changes, check whether the top number, the bottom number, or both moved, and why.

Try it yourself (optional)

At the start of the year, the shop's current assets were $750k and its current liabilities were $390k. Work out last year's current ratio. Is this year's 1.98 really an improvement?

Show the answer

Last year: 750 ÷ 390 = 1.92. So the ratio rose slightly, from 1.92 to 1.98.

But look behind it: cash fell by $60k, while money owed by customers rose $70k and inventory rose $30k. More of the shop's short-term assets are now stuck in unpaid bills and stock. The ratio went up, but the shop's ability to pay its bills from cash got weaker.

What this looks like at work​

Ratios and trends are everyday tools in finance. For example:

  • A monthly report shows margins and growth compared with last year and the budget.
  • A bank checks a company's interest coverage before lending to it.
  • You compare a company with its competitors before an investment discussion.

Each time, show the ratio, the yardstick, and what moved underneath.

How you'd say it to your manager​

Give the ratio, the comparison, and the reason in one breath.

Example message

Hi Maya, sales grew 10% this year, and 12.6% a year since 2021. Operating margin slipped from 9% to 8% because cost of goods rose to 61% of sales. Our current ratio looks healthy at 1.98, but that's partly stock and unpaid invoices building up, so I'd watch cash rather than the ratio. Debt isn't a concern: operating income covers interest about 18 times.

Notice each number comes with a comparison and an explanation.

Remember

  • A ratio lets you compare businesses of different sizes.
  • Four families: profitability, liquidity, leverage and efficiency.
  • Always compare a ratio with past years and with similar businesses.
  • Use year-over-year growth for one year, CAGR for several.
  • When a ratio moves, look at the numbers that built it.
