Topic 8 of 12 · Step 4: Visualize

Pick the right chart

Your analysis is done. Now comes the part that decides whether anyone understands it.

What you'll be able to do: match a question to the right chart and apply five rules that make any chart clearer. You'll also know when a table beats a chart, and how to test a chart before you share it.

One idea fixes most chart problems: start with the question, not the chart. Excel offers dozens of chart types, and it's tempting to click through until one looks nice. Instead, decide what you want people to see, then pick the chart that shows exactly that.

Match the chart to the question​

Data visualization is the presentation of data in graphical form, such as charts, graphs and maps, so information is easier to understand and communicate.

Use this as a cheat sheet. Each row starts with what you want the reader to see.

I want to show…UseExample
How something changes over timeLine chartSales by year
A comparison between categoriesBar chart, sorted largest to smallestSales by region
The parts that make up a wholeStacked bar (a pie only for 2 or 3 parts)Where each $100 of sales goes
Whether two things move togetherScatter plotAdvertising spend vs sales, one dot per month
How values are spread outHistogramHow big most orders are
How you got from one number to anotherWaterfallCash at the start to cash at the end
Patterns across two categories at onceHeatmapSales by store and by month

Waterfalls and heatmaps get their own detail in Topic 9. If a pie would need more than three slices, use a sorted bar chart instead: people compare lengths far more accurately than angles.

Five rules for a clear chart​

1

One chart, one message

If you need to explain three things, make three charts. A chart that tries to say everything says nothing clearly.

2

Write the finding as the title

The title is the first thing people read, so let it carry the message.

Example Not “Sales by quarter,” but “Sales grew every quarter.”

3

Remove clutter

Heavy gridlines, borders, 3D effects, shadows and a legend when you could label the data directly. If removing something changes nothing, remove it.

4

Be careful with the axis

Bar charts should start at zero, because the length of the bar is the message. Cutting the axis can make a small difference look huge. Line charts can start higher, as long as the axis is clearly labeled.

5

Use color on purpose

Make most things gray, and use one strong color for what you want people to notice. About 1 in 12 men has some form of color blindness (and about 1 in 200 women), most often trouble telling red from green, so don't rely on those two colors alone to carry the message.

The five-second test Show your chart to someone for five seconds, then hide it. Ask what it was about. If they can't tell you, the chart isn't finished.

In Excel Sort your data before you insert the chart. For a column chart, sort from largest to smallest. For a horizontal bar chart, sort from smallest to largest, because Excel draws the first row at the bottom (or tick “Categories in reverse order” under Format Axis). To remove gridlines or add data labels, select the chart and use the Chart Elements (+) button next to it.

When a chart isn't the answer​

1

Use a table for exact numbers

Charts show patterns. Tables show exact values. If people need to look up or copy specific figures, compare many measures in different units, or check a total, a clean table works better.

Example An income statement is almost always a table. A chart of the same lines would hide the numbers people need.

2

Be careful with two y-axes

A chart with a different scale on each side can mislead. Where the lines cross, and how steep each looks, depends largely on how each axis was set, so the chart can suggest a link that isn't there.

Tip Show two charts side by side, or chart one ratio instead, such as margin rather than sales and profit on separate axes.

3

Try small multiples

Instead of one chart with many crowded lines, draw several small charts, one per region or product, all with the same axes. The eye compares them easily.

What changed

The pie became a bar chart sorted from largest to smallest, so the ranking is obvious. The title now states the finding. The values sit on the bars, so there's no legend to match up. Only North is in color, because that's the point of the chart. Red and green no longer sit side by side.

Is the title right?

North brings in $0.80m of $2.31m, which is 35%, about a third. Always check the numbers behind your title.

Try it yourself (optional)

You need to show how the shop's total sales changed month by month over the last two years, and you want people to notice the December peaks. Which chart would you use, and what would you do to make the peaks stand out?

Show the answer

A line chart, because the question is about change over time, with months along the bottom.

Make the line gray and mark each December point in one strong color, with the value labeled. Then write the finding as the title, for example “Sales peak every December” (once you've checked the numbers say so).

What this looks like at work​

Charts are how most people will see your work. For example:

  • You turn a monthly report into a few slides for a meeting.
  • A senior manager has two minutes to read your summary.
  • You're asked to “make the dashboard clearer.”

Each time, ask three things before you insert a chart: what's the question, which chart answers it, and what will the title say?

How you'd say it to your manager​

When you share a chart, say what it shows in one sentence, so the reader knows where to look.

Example message

Hi Maya, I've attached the regional sales chart for the board deck. North brings in about a third of our sales, and West is the smallest at about 17%. I switched it from a pie to a sorted bar chart so the ranking is easier to read. Happy to add last year's figures beside it if that would help.

Notice it states the finding first, explains the one design change, and offers a useful next step.

Remember

  • Start with the question, then pick the chart.
  • Line for time, bar for comparison, stacked bar for parts, scatter for relationships, histogram for spread.
  • Write the finding as the title, and check the numbers behind it.
  • Bar charts start at zero. Use color to point, not to decorate.
  • Use the five-second test before you share.
