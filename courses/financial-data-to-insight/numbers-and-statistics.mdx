## Percentages done right

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Percent change: divide by where you started</h3></div> <p>Percent change = (new − old) ÷ old. The number you divide by is always the starting value.</p><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th></th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Working</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Result</th></tr><tr><td>Cash fell from $400k to $340k</td><td className="r">(340 − 400) ÷ 400</td><td className="r">−15%</td></tr><tr><td>If it rose back from $340k to $400k</td><td className="r">(400 − 340) ÷ 340</td><td className="r">+17.6%</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>The same $60k is a 15% fall but a 17.6% rise, because the starting point is different. Dividing by the wrong number is one of the most common errors in reports, and AI tools make it too (Topic 11).</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">In Excel</b> <code style={{fontSize:".9em",background:"var(--cream)",padding:"1px 6px",borderRadius:"5px",overflowWrap:"anywhere"}}>=(C2-B2)/B2</code>, formatted as a percentage, with the old value in B2 and the new one in C2.</p><p className="small">Helps with Topics 1, 6 and 11.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Percentage points vs percent</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>A <Term slug="percentage-point" name="Percentage Point" def="A percentage point is the simple arithmetic difference between two percentages. It is different from a percentage change, which measures how much a figure has grown relative to its starting level."><b>percentage point</b></Term> is the simple difference between two percentages.</p></div><p>When a percentage changes, there are two ways to describe it, and they give very different numbers:</p><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Operating margin fell from 9% to 8%</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Working</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Result</th></tr><tr><td>In percentage points</td><td className="r">8 − 9</td><td className="r">−1 point</td></tr><tr><td>As a percent change</td><td className="r">(8 − 9) ÷ 9</td><td className="r">−11%</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>Both are correct, but they mean different things. Say “points” when you mean the simple difference, so nobody misreads a one-point fall as a one percent fall.</p><p className="small">Helps with Topics 4, 10 and 12.</p> </div> </div></div>

## Averages: which one?

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>The average (mean)</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>The <Term slug="arithmetic-mean" name="Arithmetic Mean" def="The arithmetic mean is the sum of a set of values divided by the number of values. It is what most people mean by &quot;the average&quot;."><b>mean</b></Term>, or average, is the total divided by the number of values.</p></div><p>Here are seven days of sales at store S01. One day includes a large business order.</p><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Day</th><th>Mon</th><th>Tue</th><th>Wed</th><th>Thu</th><th>Fri</th><th>Sat</th><th>Sun</th></tr><tr><td>Sales ($)</td><td>980</td><td>1,020</td><td>1,050</td><td>1,100</td><td>1,120</td><td>1,150</td><td>4,500</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>The mean is 10,920 ÷ 7 = $1,560. But six of the seven days were below $1,160, so $1,560 doesn't describe a typical day.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>The median</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>The <Term slug="median" name="Median" def="The median is the middle value in a set of numbers arranged in order. Half the values are above it and half are below."><b>median</b></Term> is the middle value when the values are sorted. Half are above it and half below.</p></div><p>Sort the values and take the middle one. With an even number of values, average the two in the middle. Here the middle (4th) value is $1,100. That's a much better picture of a normal day. One unusual value pulls the mean a long way but barely moves the median.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> When the mean and median are far apart, look for unusual values before you report either one. Report both if it helps.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">In Excel</b> <code style={{fontSize:".9em",background:"var(--cream)",padding:"1px 6px",borderRadius:"5px",overflowWrap:"anywhere"}}>=AVERAGE(B2:H2)</code> and <code style={{fontSize:".9em",background:"var(--cream)",padding:"1px 6px",borderRadius:"5px",overflowWrap:"anywhere"}}>=MEDIAN(B2:H2)</code>.</p><p className="small">Helps with Topic 3, when you check unusual values.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>The weighted average</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>A <Term slug="weighted-average" name="Weighted Average" def="A weighted average is an average in which some values count more than others, according to assigned weights that reflect their importance or size."><b>weighted average</b></Term> gives each value more or less influence depending on its size, such as its share of sales.</p></div><p>Cost of goods was 61.9% of sales for shoes and 60.5% for everything else. The simple average of those two is 61.2%. But shoes are only about a third of sales, so the true overall figure is lower:</p><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th></th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Sales ($k)</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Cost of goods ($k)</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Cost as % of sales</th></tr><tr><td>Shoes</td><td className="r">787.5</td><td className="r">487.5</td><td className="r">61.9%</td></tr><tr><td>Everything else</td><td className="r">1,522.5</td><td className="r">921.5</td><td className="r">60.5%</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>All products</td><td className="r">2,310</td><td className="r">1,409</td><td className="r">61.0%</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>The right way is total cost ÷ total sales: 1,409 ÷ 2,310 = 61.0%. Averaging percentages directly treats a small group as if it were as big as a large one.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">In Excel</b> <code style={{fontSize:".9em",background:"var(--cream)",padding:"1px 6px",borderRadius:"5px",overflowWrap:"anywhere"}}>=SUMPRODUCT(B2:B3, D2:D3)/SUM(B2:B3)</code> gives a weighted average of the percentages in column D, weighted by the sales in column B. Or simply <code style={{fontSize:".9em",background:"var(--cream)",padding:"1px 6px",borderRadius:"5px",overflowWrap:"anywhere"}}>=SUM(C2:C3)/SUM(B2:B3)</code>.</p><p className="small">Helps with Topics 6, 9 and 12.</p> </div> </div></div>

## Spread: how far apart are the values?

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Two stores can have the same average sales, with one steady and one swinging wildly. An average alone hides that, so describe the spread too.</p> <div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Range and quartiles</h3></div> <p>The <b>range</b> is the highest value minus the lowest. It's simple, but one unusual value can stretch it.</p><div className="def"><p><b>Quartiles</b> split sorted values into four equal parts. About a quarter of the values are below the first quartile (Q1), and about three quarters are below the third (Q3). The gap between them is the interquartile range (IQR).</p></div><p>For S01's seven days, <code style={{fontSize:".9em",background:"var(--cream)",padding:"1px 6px",borderRadius:"5px",overflowWrap:"anywhere"}}>=QUARTILE.INC(B2:H2,1)</code> gives Q1 = 1,035 and <code style={{fontSize:".9em",background:"var(--cream)",padding:"1px 6px",borderRadius:"5px",overflowWrap:"anywhere"}}>=QUARTILE.INC(B2:H2,3)</code> gives Q3 = 1,135. The IQR is 100. A common rule flags anything more than 1.5 × IQR beyond the quartiles as an <Term slug="outlier" name="Outlier" def="An outlier is a data point that is very different from most of the other values in a data set.">outlier</Term>: here, anything above 1,285 or below 885. The $4,500 day is flagged.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> An outlier is a value to check, not a value to delete. This one is a real business order. Topic 3 shows what to do with each kind.</p><p className="small">Helps with Topic 3.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Standard deviation</h3></div> <div className="def"><p><Term slug="standard-deviation" name="Standard Deviation" def="Standard deviation is a measure of how much a set of values typically differs from the mean. It is the square root of the variance."><b>Standard deviation</b></Term> measures how far values typically sit from their mean. A bigger number means more spread.</p></div><p>You'll see it in reports and in Excel (<code style={{fontSize:".9em",background:"var(--cream)",padding:"1px 6px",borderRadius:"5px",overflowWrap:"anywhere"}}>=STDEV.S(B2:H2)</code>). Like the mean, it's pulled a long way by unusual values, so look at the data before you trust it.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>Read a histogram</h3></div> <p>A histogram groups values into ranges and shows how many fall in each. ## Rounding and showing numbers honestly

<div className="nbs-sec"><ul style={{margin:"0",paddingLeft:"20px",display:"flex",flexDirection:"column",gap:"4px"}}><li>Round to a level that fits the decision. “$2.31 million” is clearer than “$2,310,417”, and no less true for a strategy discussion.</li><li>Keep full precision in your calculations, and round only what you show.</li><li>When rounded parts don't add up to the rounded total, say so in a note rather than changing a number.</li><li>Don't show more decimals than the data supports. A forecast of “12.47% growth” suggests far more certainty than any forecast has.</li></ul> <p className="small">Helps with every topic.</p></div>

<div className="nbs-sec try"><p className="eyebrow">Try it yourself (optional)</p> <p>1. Sales rose from $2.10m to $2.31m. What's the percent change? If they then fell back to $2.10m, what would that percent change be?</p><p>2. Operating margin rose from 8% to 10%. Describe the change in percentage points and as a percent change.</p><p>3. Five business invoices are $1k, $2k, $2k, $3k and $42k. Which better describes a typical invoice, the mean or the median?</p> <details><summary>Show the answer</summary><p>1. (2.31 − 2.10) ÷ 2.10 = +10%. Falling back: (2.10 − 2.31) ÷ 2.31 = −9.1%.</p><p>2. Up 2 percentage points, which is a 25% increase (2 ÷ 8).</p><p>3. The median, $2k. The mean is $10k (50 ÷ 5), pulled up by one very large invoice.</p></details></div>

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="remember"> <p className="eyebrow">Remember</p> <ul><li>Percent change = (new − old) ÷ old. Always divide by the starting value.</li><li>Say “points” for the difference between two percentages.</li><li>The median resists unusual values. When the mean and median differ a lot, look at the data.</li><li>For an overall percentage, use totals (a weighted average), not the average of percentages.</li><li>Describe the spread as well as the average, and check outliers rather than deleting them.</li><li>Correlation is not causation.</li></ul> </div></div>
