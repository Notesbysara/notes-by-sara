Waterfalls, heatmaps and honest charts

Two chart types every finance team uses, and the checks that keep any chart honest.

What you'll be able to do: build a waterfall that explains how one number became another, and a heatmap that makes a pattern jump out. You'll also spot the most common ways charts mislead, in your own work and in what you read.

The waterfall chart​

A waterfall chart shows how a starting value becomes an ending value through a series of increases and decreases, each drawn as a floating bar.

It answers one question: how did we get from this number to that one? The first and last bars stand on the axis. Every bar in between starts where the previous one ended, so you can see which changes helped, which hurt, and by how much.

Here's the shop's operating income, from the budget to the actual result, using the variances from Topic 7:

Operating income, budget to actual, $ thousands

$ thousandsAmount
Budget operating income200
Higher revenue+60
Higher cost of goods sold−59
Higher running costs−16
Actual operating income185

In a few seconds, the owner sees that revenue beat the plan, but higher costs more than ate it up.

1

Keep it to a few steps

About seven bars or fewer is plenty. Group small items into one “Other” bar, so the big drivers stand out.

2

Color gains and losses differently

Use one color for increases and another for decreases, and a neutral color for the start and end totals. Put the value on every bar.

Tip Choose colors that differ in lightness as well as hue, so the chart still works for people with red-green color blindness.

3

Make sure it adds up

The steps must add up exactly to the end bar. Here, 200 + 60 − 59 − 16 = 185. If rounding stops it adding up, say so in a note.

In Excel Insert › Charts › Waterfall builds one from a two-column list, with decreases entered as negative numbers (Excel 2016 and later). Then click the end bar twice so only that bar is selected, right-click it, and choose “Set as Total” so it stands on the axis.

The heatmap​

A heatmap is a grid where the color of each cell shows the size of its value, so patterns across two categories are easy to see.

It works when you have two categories, such as region and quarter, and one number for each pair. A table of 16 or 40 numbers takes a while to scan. A heatmap shows the odd one out at once.

Gross margin, %Q1Q2Q3Q4
North40403940
South39393438
East39403939
West39383940

Rows are in order of sales, largest first. Darker means a lower margin.

One cell stands out: the South region in the third quarter, at 34% against about 39% elsewhere. The heatmap doesn't tell you why. It tells you where to look first, for example at a clearance sale or a pricing change in that region.

Tip Use one color going from light to dark, so the order is obvious at a glance, and say which end is good, for example “Darker means a lower margin.” Write the number in every cell, and order the rows in a meaningful way, such as by sales, not alphabetically.

In Excel Select the numbers, then Home › Conditional Formatting › Color Scales. Pick a two-color scale, or set your own colors under More Rules.

Five ways charts mislead​

Charts can mislead without anyone meaning them to. Check for these five, in your own work and in what you read.

1

A cut axis

A bar chart that doesn't start at zero makes small differences look big. Here, June's sales are 2.7% higher than May's, but on the left, June looks twice as big.

Tip Bars should start at zero. If you need to show small changes, chart the change itself, or use a line chart with a clearly labeled axis.

2

A convenient time window

Showing only the six months that went up, when the longer trend is down. Choose the period that answers the question, not the one that looks best, and show enough history to be fair.

3

Two different y-axes

Two lines on two scales can be made to look related, or not, just by stretching one axis. Topic 8 covers safer alternatives. If you must use two axes, label both clearly.

4

3D and decoration

A 3D pie makes the slices at the front look bigger than they are. Shadows, textures and pictures add nothing and can distort. Keep charts flat and plain.

5

No comparison

A number on its own can look impressive. Add a yardstick: last year, the budget or a competitor. Without one, the reader can't tell whether the result is good.

The skeptic test Before you share a chart, imagine the most skeptical person in the room. If the chart wouldn't survive their first question, change it. Your credibility is worth more than a dramatic picture.

Try it yourself (optional)

1. Gross profit was budgeted at $900k (2,250 − 1,350) and came in at $901k. Revenue was $60k above budget and cost of goods sold was $59k above budget. List the bars of a waterfall from budget to actual, and check that they add up.

2. A bar chart of monthly sales starts its axis at $180k. May is $185k and June is $190k, and June's bar looks twice as tall. What's the real difference?

Show the answer

1. Start at 900, then +60 for revenue, then −59 for cost of goods, ending at 901. Check: 900 + 60 − 59 = 901.

2. June is $5k higher, which is 5 ÷ 185 = about 2.7%. The bar looks twice as tall only because the axis starts at 180, so May's bar shows 5 and June's shows 10.

What this looks like at work​

You'll meet both charts, and the honesty checks, all the time. For example:

  • A monthly report explains the gap between budget and actual profit with a waterfall.
  • A sales director wants to see which regions and months are weak, on one page.
  • A colleague's slide shows a dramatic jump, and you're asked whether it's real.

Each time, check that the waterfall adds up, that the heatmap's colors mean something, and that every chart would survive a skeptical question.

How you'd say it to your manager​

Share the chart with the one thing it shows, so the reader knows where to look.

Example message

Hi Maya, I've added a waterfall to the monthly pack showing how operating income went from the $200k budget to $185k. Revenue beat plan by $60k, but cost of goods ran $59k over and running costs $16k over. I've also attached a heatmap of gross margin by region and quarter. South in Q3 stands out at 34%, so I'll look into what happened there next.

Notice it states what each chart shows and what you'll do next.

Remember

  • A waterfall shows how you got from one number to another. Keep it to about seven steps or fewer, and make sure it adds up.
  • A heatmap shows patterns across two categories. Use one color scale, show the numbers, and order the rows meaningfully.
  • Check for five traps: cut axes, convenient time windows, two y-axes, 3D decoration and no comparison.
  • If a chart wouldn't survive a skeptical question, change it.
