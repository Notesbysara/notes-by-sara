Topic 12 of 12 · All six steps

The whole pipeline, start to finish

Eleven topics, six steps. Now we run them all on one real question, from a vague worry to a clear decision.

What you'll be able to do: run the six-step method on a business question from beginning to end. You'll also see how each step's output feeds the next, and plan your own first project.

In Topic 4, the owner said: “Sales are up, but profit feels lower. Can you look into it?” We found that operating margin fell from 9% to 8%, and that the drop showed up in cost of goods, which rose from 60% to 61% of sales. Now we finish the job.

This is an invented shop. All numbers are examples.

The six steps on one question​

1

Assess: check the data

The owner sends a product-level export of this year's and last year's sales and costs. We write the schema map: one row = one product line in one month, amounts in US dollars. Then we run the nine checks from Topic 3. We find one month copied twice and one cost entered in thousands instead of dollars. We fix both, log the changes, and confirm the cleaned totals tie to the income statement: sales of $2,310k and cost of goods of $1,409k.

Produces: a schema map, a clean file and a change log.

2

Inquire: sharpen the question

“Why did cost of goods rise from 60% to 61% of sales, and what should we change before next year's budget?” From the hypothesis tree in Topic 4, we test the leaves that are most likely and quickest to check: prices and discounts, product mix, and supplier costs. We skip vanity numbers, such as total store visits.

Produces: a question brief and a short list of hypotheses.

3

Analyze: find the cause

If cost of goods had stayed at 60% of sales, it would have been $1,386k. It was $1,409k, so the rise cost $23k. Splitting by product shows where it came from:

Shoes787.5487.561.9%15
Bags and other products1,522.5921.560.5%8

Shoes: the average price fell from $65 last year (also the budget price) to $63 a pair, while the cost per pair stayed at $39 (60% of $65), so each sale kept less. Bags and other products: purchase invoices show several suppliers raised prices, and our selling prices didn't follow.

Both product groups ran at about 60% last year, so the shift in mix between them barely moved the total, and stock write-offs didn't change. Those leaves are ruled out. (Running costs also held at 31% of sales.)

Produces: the size of each cause, with evidence.

4

Visualize: show the bridge

A waterfall from last year's operating income to this year's. Growth added about $19k (more sales at last year's margins), but the two cost-of-goods causes took $23k away.

Operating income, last year to this year, $ thousands

Title: “Sales grew, but lower shoe prices and higher supplier prices cost us $23k.” Check: 189 + 19 − 15 − 8 = 185.

Produces: one chart with the finding as its title.

5

Interpret: say what it means

StepFor the shop
FindingOperating margin fell from 9% to 8%, because cost of goods rose from 60% to 61% of sales.
WhyAbout $15k came from shoes: at $63 a pair instead of $65, cost of goods rose to 61.9% of shoe sales, $15k more than at 60%. About $8k came from higher supplier prices on other products. (The full price cut gave up $25k of sales, as Topic 7 showed. This bridge works in percentages of sales, so the rest of that is inside the other bars.)
So whatEach point of margin is worth about $23k a year at today's sales. If both causes continue, margin stays a point lower; if discounts deepen or more suppliers raise prices, it could slip further.
Now whatReview the shoe discount policy, and either renegotiate with the suppliers who raised prices or adjust our prices. Recheck margin in three months.

Limit: the shoe discounts may have brought in extra sales. We sold 12,500 pairs against a budget of 12,000 (Topic 7), so some of the cost may have paid for itself.

Produces: a recommendation, with its impact and limits.

6

Prompt: make it repeatable

We save a prompt for each step, such as the data check and the product split, so next quarter's update takes an hour, not a day. We still recalculate the key numbers ourselves.

Produces: a reusable set of prompts.

It's a loop​

Notice what happened at step 5. Answering one question raised a new one: did the shoe discounts bring in enough extra sales to pay for themselves? That sends us back to step 2 (the data is already clean) with a new, sharper question, and around the loop again.

That's normal. Good analysis rarely ends with a full stop. It ends with a decision and, often, the next question.

Your turn: a first project​

Pick one small, real question, from your work, your studies or a public company. Don't aim for perfect. Aim for finishing once. Use this checklist:

StepWhat you'll produceDone when
AssessA schema map and a clean fileThe totals tie to the source
InquireA one-page question briefSomeone else agrees it's the right question
AnalyzeThe numbers that answer itEach hypothesis is supported or ruled out
VisualizeOne or two chartsEach passes the five-second test
InterpretA one-page summaryYou can say it in 30 seconds
PromptSaved prompts for next timeYou've checked the AI's numbers yourself

Try it yourself (optional)

Online sales fell from $19,500 to $18,200 in a month, even though website visits rose 40% (Topic 5). Write one sentence for each of the six steps, describing what you'd do.

Show the answer

One good version:

Assess: check the visits and orders data covers the same days, with no duplicate orders. Inquire: “Why did online sales fall 7% while visits rose 40%, and what should we change on the website this quarter?” Analyze: compare the share of visits that buy (3% to 2%) and the average order, by traffic source. Visualize: a line chart of the share of visits that buy, by week, with the finding as the title. Interpret: size the lost sales and recommend a fix for the weakest traffic source. Prompt: save a prompt that runs the same check next month.

What this looks like at work​

At work, nobody will say “please run the six steps.” But this is what good analysts do, quietly, every time. For example:

  • Your manager forwards a worry and asks you to “take a look”.
  • You're given a week to answer a question before a budget meeting.
  • You join a new team and want your first piece of work to land well.

Each time, move through the steps in order, produce something at each one, and finish with a decision.

How you'd say it to your manager​

At the end of a full analysis, keep the message short and point to the detail.

Example message

Hi Maya, I've finished the margin review. Margin fell from 9% to 8% because cost of goods rose a point: about $15k from lower shoe prices and $8k from supplier price increases. I'd suggest we review the shoe discount policy and talk to the suppliers who raised prices before the budget. One open question is whether the discounts paid for themselves in extra sales, which I can check next. Summary and workings attached.

Notice it gives the answer, the sized causes, a recommendation, and the next question.

Remember

  • Assess the data, Inquire with a sharp question, Analyze to find the cause, Visualize the finding, Interpret with a so-what, and Prompt to repeat it.
  • Each step produces something the next step uses.
  • The method is a loop: interpreting often raises the next question.
  • Aim to finish one small project, not to make it perfect.
