## The three financial statements

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="def"><p><Term slug="financial-statement" name="Financial Statement" def="A financial statement is a formal report that summarizes a business's financial activity and position. The main ones are the balance sheet, the income statement and the cash flow statement."><b>Financial statements</b></Term> are formal reports that summarize a business's financial activity and position.</p></div> <p>Three of them matter most. Each one answers a different question, and together they give you the full picture.</p> <div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Statement</th><th>The question it answers</th><th>What it covers</th></tr><tr><td>Income statement</td><td>Did we make money?</td><td>A period, such as a year</td></tr><tr><td>Balance sheet</td><td>What do we own and owe?</td><td>One date, such as December 31</td></tr><tr><td>Cash flow statement</td><td>Where did the cash come from, and where did it go?</td><td>A period, such as a year</td></tr></tbody></table></div> <div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>The income statement: did we make money?</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>The <Term slug="income-statement" name="Income Statement" def="An income statement is a financial statement that shows a business's revenue, expenses and resulting profit or loss over a period, such as a quarter or a year."><b>income statement</b></Term> shows revenue, expenses and profit for a period of time. It is also called the profit and loss statement, or P&amp;L.</p></div><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>$ thousands, this year</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Amount</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>% of revenue</th></tr><tr><td>Revenue (sales, after discounts)</td><td className="r">2,310</td><td className="r">100%</td></tr><tr><td>Cost of goods sold</td><td className="r">(1,409)</td><td className="r">61.0%</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Gross profit</td><td className="r">901</td><td className="r">39.0%</td></tr><tr><td>Running costs (rent, wages, bills, depreciation and other costs)</td><td className="r">(716)</td><td className="r">31.0%</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Operating income</td><td className="r">185</td><td className="r">8.0%</td></tr><tr><td>Interest on the loan</td><td className="r">(10)</td><td className="r">0.4%</td></tr><tr><td>Tax</td><td className="r">(35)</td><td className="r">1.5%</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Net profit</td><td className="r">140</td><td className="r">6.1%</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>Read it from the top down. Each line takes away a cost, and each profit line shows what is left at that stage. Numbers in brackets are subtracted.</p><ul style={{margin:"0",paddingLeft:"20px",display:"flex",flexDirection:"column",gap:"4px"}}><li><Term slug="gross-profit" name="Gross Profit" def="Gross profit is the money left from revenue after subtracting the direct cost of the goods or services sold."><b>Gross profit</b></Term> is what is left after paying for the goods you sold.</li><li><Term slug="operating-income" name="Operating Income" def="Operating income is the profit a business makes from its core operations, calculated as revenue minus cost of goods sold and operating expenses, before interest and taxes."><b>Operating income</b></Term> is what is left after running the business too.</li><li><b>Net profit</b> is what is left after interest and tax. It's the “bottom line”.</li></ul><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> Of every $100 of sales, about $61 paid for the goods, $31 ran the stores, and about $6 was left as net profit.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> When someone says “profit”, ask which one they mean. Gross profit, operating income and net profit answer different questions.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>The balance sheet: what do we own and owe?</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>The <Term slug="balance-sheet" name="Balance Sheet" def="A balance sheet is a financial statement that shows what a business owns, what it owes and what belongs to its owners at a specific date."><b>balance sheet</b></Term> shows what a business owns, what it owes and what belongs to its owners, at a single date.</p></div><p>What it owns are its <Term slug="asset" name="Asset" def="An asset is anything you own or control that has value and is expected to provide a future benefit, such as cash, investments, property, equipment or money others owe you.">assets</Term>. What it owes are its <Term slug="liability" name="Liability" def="A liability is something you owe to another person or organization, such as a loan, a mortgage, a credit card balance or an unpaid bill.">liabilities</Term>. What is left for the owners is <Term slug="equity" name="Equity" def="Equity is the value of ownership that remains after all debts are subtracted. For a person, it is the part of an asset, such as a home, that you own outright; for a business, it is the owners' share of the company.">equity</Term>. The two sides always match:</p><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><td>The balance sheet rule</td><td>Assets = Liabilities + Equity</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>It balances because equity is whatever is left after you subtract what is owed from what is owned. Every asset was paid for with either borrowed money or the owner&#39;s money.</p><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>$ thousands</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Start of year</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>End of year</th></tr><tr><td>Cash</td><td className="r">400</td><td className="r">340</td></tr><tr><td>Money owed by customers</td><td className="r">50</td><td className="r">120</td></tr><tr><td>Inventory (stock on the shelves)</td><td className="r">300</td><td className="r">330</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Current assets</td><td className="r">750</td><td className="r">790</td></tr><tr><td>Store fittings and equipment</td><td className="r">350</td><td className="r">410</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Total assets</td><td className="r">1,100</td><td className="r">1,200</td></tr><tr><td>Owed to suppliers</td><td className="r">370</td><td className="r">350</td></tr><tr><td>Loan, part due within a year</td><td className="r">20</td><td className="r">50</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Current liabilities</td><td className="r">390</td><td className="r">400</td></tr><tr><td>Loan, part due later</td><td className="r">150</td><td className="r">100</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Total liabilities</td><td className="r">540</td><td className="r">500</td></tr><tr><td>Owner's equity</td><td className="r">560</td><td className="r">700</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Total liabilities and equity</td><td className="r">1,100</td><td className="r">1,200</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>“Current” means expected to turn into cash, or to be paid, within about a year. Everything else is longer term.</p><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> At the end of the year, the shop owed $500k and the owner's stake was $700k. Together they match the $1,200k of assets.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> A balance sheet is a snapshot. Compare two dates to see what changed. Here, customers owe $70k more and the shop holds $30k more stock.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>The cash flow statement: where did the cash go?</h3></div> <div className="def"><p>The <Term slug="cash-flow-statement" name="Cash Flow Statement" def="The cash flow statement shows how much cash came into and went out of a business during a period, grouped into operating, investing and financing activities."><b>cash flow statement</b></Term> shows the cash that came into and went out of a business during a period.</p></div><p>It sorts the cash into three groups:</p><ul style={{margin:"0",paddingLeft:"20px",display:"flex",flexDirection:"column",gap:"4px"}}><li><b>Operating:</b> running the business day to day.</li><li><b>Investing:</b> buying or selling long-term things, such as store fittings. This spending is called <Term slug="capital-expenditure" name="Capital Expenditure" def="Capital expenditure is money a business spends to buy, upgrade or extend the life of long-term physical assets, such as buildings, machinery, vehicles or technology.">capital expenditure</Term>.</li><li><b>Financing:</b> money to and from lenders and owners, such as loan repayments.</li></ul><div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>$ thousands, this year</th><th style={{textAlign:"right"}}>Amount</th></tr><tr><td>Net profit</td><td className="r">140</td></tr><tr><td>Add back depreciation (a cost that used no cash this year)</td><td className="r">20</td></tr><tr><td>More money owed by customers</td><td className="r">(70)</td></tr><tr><td>More stock on the shelves</td><td className="r">(30)</td></tr><tr><td>Less owed to suppliers (we paid them down)</td><td className="r">(20)</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Cash from operating activities</td><td className="r">40</td></tr><tr><td>Refitting the stores and a new till system</td><td className="r">(80)</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Cash from investing activities</td><td className="r">(80)</td></tr><tr><td>Loan repaid</td><td className="r">(20)</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Cash from financing activities</td><td className="r">(20)</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Change in cash</td><td className="r">(60)</td></tr><tr><td>Cash at the start of the year</td><td className="r">400</td></tr><tr className="total"><td>Cash at the end of the year</td><td className="r">340</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> The shop made $140k of profit, but its day-to-day operations brought in only $40k of cash. That gap is exactly what a manager needs to understand.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> <Term slug="depreciation" name="Depreciation" def="Depreciation is the process of spreading the cost of a physical long-term asset, such as a machine, vehicle or building, over the years it is expected to be used.">Depreciation</Term> is added back because it spreads the cost of the fittings over several years. It reduces profit, but no cash leaves when it is recorded. The cash left when the fittings were bought.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">4</span><h3>How the three connect</h3></div> <div className="def"><p><Term slug="accrual-accounting" name="Accrual Accounting" def="Accrual accounting records income when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, regardless of when cash actually changes hands."><b>Accrual accounting</b></Term> records income when it is earned and costs when they are incurred, whether or not cash has changed hands yet.</p></div><p>Most businesses keep their accounts this way. A sale counts on the day it's made, even if the customer pays next month. That's why profit and cash can tell different stories. The statements are linked like this:</p><ul style={{margin:"0",paddingLeft:"20px",display:"flex",flexDirection:"column",gap:"4px"}}><li>Net profit of $140k is the first line of the cash flow statement.</li><li>It also adds to the owner's equity, which grew from $560k to $700k, because the owner took no money out.</li><li>Changes on the balance sheet (money owed by customers, stock, money owed to suppliers) explain why operating cash was $100k lower than profit.</li><li>The cash at the end of the cash flow statement, $340k, is the cash on the balance sheet.</li></ul><p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> A business customer buys $5,000 of bags in December and pays in January. The sale counts in this year's revenue and profit, but the cash arrives next year.</p><p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> If profit looks healthy but cash is falling, look first at money owed by customers and inventory on the balance sheet.</p> </div> </div></div>

## The method: six steps from numbers to a decision

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Reading statements is only the start. The whole course follows one method, the Financial Data-to-Insight (FDI) framework. Each step answers one question. Do them in this order.</p> <div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Assess: what am I looking at?</h3></div><div className="def"><p><b>Assessing data</b> means checking what each column represents, where it came from, and whether it is complete, consistent and measured correctly, before you use it.</p></div> <p>In practice, open the file and read it before you calculate anything: the column names, the first few rows, and any notes at the top or bottom.</p> <p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> One report shows sales in thousands of dollars and another in dollars. If you mix them up, your answer is 1,000 times wrong.</p> <p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> Always check the unit (dollars, thousands, millions) and the time period before anything else.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Inquire: what do I want to know?</h3></div><div className="def"><p>A well-formed <b>business question</b> is a specific, measurable question about a defined number and time period, whose answer will inform a particular decision.</p></div> <p>Pick one clear question. A vague question like “How are we doing?” has no clear answer. A specific one tells you exactly where to look.</p> <p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> Instead of “Is the business okay?”, ask “Why did our cash go down this year, even though sales went up?”</p> <p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> A good question names the number, the time period, and the decision it will help with.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>Analyze: what do the numbers say?</h3></div><div className="def"><p><b>Analysis</b> is applying calculations or methods to data to answer a defined question.</p></div> <p>Now do the math that answers your question. This could be comparing two years, working out a percentage, or adding up where money came from and where it went.</p> <p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> To find out why cash fell, list the money that came in and the money that went out, and see which items were biggest.</p> <p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> Your question decides the math, not the other way around.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">4</span><h3>Visualize: how do I show it?</h3></div><div className="def"><p><Term slug="data-visualization" name="Data Visualization" def="Data visualization is the presentation of data in graphical form, such as charts, graphs and maps, to make information easier to understand, analyze and communicate."><b>Data visualization</b></Term> is the presentation of data in graphical form, such as charts, graphs and maps, so information is easier to understand and communicate.</p></div> <p>The right chart depends on your message: a line for change over time, bars to compare things side by side, and a waterfall to show what added to a total or took away from it.</p> <p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> A bar chart showing each reason cash went up or down is much clearer than a list of numbers.</p> <p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> One chart, one message. If it needs a long explanation, it's too complicated.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">5</span><h3>Interpret: so what?</h3></div><div className="def"><p><b>Interpretation</b> is explaining what a result means for the business and recommending an action based on it.</p></div> <p>This is the step people care about most. Your manager rarely needs every number. They need to know what it means for them and what to do.</p> <p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> “Cash fell 15%” is only a fact. “Cash fell 15%, partly because customers owe us $70k more, so let's find out why before we spend” is an answer.</p> <p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> Always finish with a recommendation, not just a description.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">6</span><h3>Prompt: can AI help me do this faster?</h3></div><div className="def"><p>A <b>prompt</b> is the set of instructions and data you give an AI tool to produce an answer. A good prompt states the role, the context, the task, the data, the format you want, and any rules to follow.</p></div> <p>Once you understand the steps, AI tools can help you repeat them quickly on new data. But AI only gives good answers when you give it clear instructions and clean data.</p> <p className="eg"><b className="lab">Example</b> Tell the AI what the data is, what question you're asking, and ask it to show its working.</p> <p className="tip"><b className="lab">Tip</b> Check AI's math and its conclusions yourself, because it can sound sure and still be wrong. And never paste confidential company data into a public AI tool.</p> </div> </div> <p>One more thing: it's a loop. When you reach step 5, you'll often find a new question, and you start again from step 1.</p> <h3 style={{marginTop:"8px"}}>Your course map</h3> <p>Every topic in this course teaches one step in depth. Here's where each one fits, and what you'll be able to produce by the end.</p> <div className="tw map"><table><tbody><tr><th>Step</th><th>The question</th><th>Topics</th><th>What you'll produce</th></tr> <tr><td>Toolkits</td><td>Do I have the basic tools?</td><td>A Excel for this course<br />B Numbers and statistics for this course</td><td>Confidence with the formulas and math the topics use (optional)</td></tr> <tr><td>Foundations</td><td>How does a business show up in numbers?</td><td>1 Read a business through its numbers</td><td>The ability to read the three statements</td></tr> <tr><td>1 Assess</td><td>What am I looking at?</td><td>2 Know your data<br />3 Clean and check your data</td><td>A schema map and a clean file that ties to its source</td></tr> <tr><td>2 Inquire</td><td>What do I want to know?</td><td>4 Ask a question worth answering<br />5 Vanity metrics vs metrics that matter</td><td>A question brief and the right metrics</td></tr> <tr><td>3 Analyze</td><td>What do the numbers say?</td><td>6 Ratios and trends<br />7 Variance and valuation</td><td>Ratios, variances, a break-even point, a forecast and a valuation range</td></tr> <tr><td>4 Visualize</td><td>How do I show it?</td><td>8 Pick the right chart<br />9 Waterfalls, heatmaps and honest charts</td><td>Clear charts with the finding as the title</td></tr> <tr><td>5 Interpret</td><td>So what?</td><td>10 The “So what?”</td><td>A recommendation someone can act on</td></tr> <tr><td>6 Prompt</td><td>Can AI help me do this faster?</td><td>11 Prompting AI for financial analysis</td><td>Prompts you can reuse and check</td></tr> <tr><td>All six</td><td>Can I do it start to finish?</td><td>12 The whole pipeline</td><td>One complete analysis</td></tr> </tbody></table></div></div>

## What this looks like at work

<div className="nbs-sec work"><p>In your first finance job, you'll use these six steps all the time, often without anyone calling them that. For example:</p> <ul> <li>Your manager asks why this month's costs came in higher than the <Term slug="budget" name="Budget" def="A budget is a plan for how money will be earned and spent over a set period, such as a month or a year. It sets limits for spending in different categories and shows how much can be saved.">budget</Term>.</li> <li>A team wants money for a new project, and you're asked whether it's worth it.</li> <li>Sales are up, but the bank balance is down, and someone wants to know why.</li> </ul> <p>Each time, the order is the same: check the data, pin down the question, do the math, show it simply, and say what to do.</p></div>

## How you'd say it to your manager

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Your analysis is only useful if people understand it. Keep your message short: what happened, why, and what you suggest.</p> <div className="msg"> <p className="eyebrow">Example message</p> <p className="body">Hi Maya, I looked at why our cash fell $60k this year while sales grew 10%. The main reasons are $70k more owed to us by customers, which looks like slower payment (I'll confirm with the aging report), and $80k of one-off store refits. If we open the new store now, we'd have about $90k left, which is under three months of fixed running costs. I'd suggest we sort out any slow payers first, then look at whether the new store would pay for itself before we commit. Happy to walk you through the numbers.</p> </div> <p className="small">Notice it starts with the answer, gives the two biggest reasons, and ends with a clear next step.</p></div>

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="remember"> <p className="eyebrow">Remember</p> <ul><li>The income statement asks “did we make money?”, the balance sheet asks “what do we own and owe?”, and the cash flow statement asks “where did the cash go?”</li><li>Assets = liabilities + equity, always.</li><li>Profit is not cash. Money owed by customers and stock can soak up the cash a profit brings in.</li><li>The six FDI steps: Assess, Inquire, Analyze, Visualize, Interpret, Prompt.
