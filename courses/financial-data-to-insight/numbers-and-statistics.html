Numbers and statistics for this course

Most mistakes in finance aren't advanced math. They're a percentage worked out from the wrong number, or an average that hides what's really going on.

What you'll be able to do: work out percent changes and percentage points correctly, and choose between an average, a median and a weighted average. You'll also describe spread, read a histogram and tell a correlation from a cause.

Already confident? Check yourself

If you can already do all of these, you can skip this toolkit and come back to it when you need it.

  • Work out a percent change, and know which number to divide by
  • Explain why a fall from 9% to 8% is one point but an 11% drop
  • Say when the median is a better summary than the average
  • Work out a weighted average
  • Explain why two things moving together doesn't prove one causes the other

Percentages done right​

1

Percent change: divide by where you started

Percent change = (new − old) ÷ old. The number you divide by is always the starting value.

WorkingResult
Cash fell from $400k to $340k(340 − 400) ÷ 400−15%
If it rose back from $340k to $400k(400 − 340) ÷ 340+17.6%

The same $60k is a 15% fall but a 17.6% rise, because the starting point is different. Dividing by the wrong number is one of the most common errors in reports, and AI tools make it too (Topic 11).

In Excel =(C2-B2)/B2, formatted as a percentage, with the old value in B2 and the new one in C2.

Helps with Topics 1, 6 and 11.

2

Percentage points vs percent

A percentage point is the simple difference between two percentages.

When a percentage changes, there are two ways to describe it, and they give very different numbers:

Operating margin fell from 9% to 8%WorkingResult
In percentage points8 − 9−1 point
As a percent change(8 − 9) ÷ 9−11%

Both are correct, but they mean different things. Say “points” when you mean the simple difference, so nobody misreads a one-point fall as a one percent fall.

Helps with Topics 4, 10 and 12.

Averages: which one?​

1

The average (mean)

The mean, or average, is the total divided by the number of values.

Here are seven days of sales at store S01. One day includes a large business order.

DayMonTueWedThuFriSatSun
Sales ($)9801,0201,0501,1001,1201,1504,500

The mean is 10,920 ÷ 7 = $1,560. But six of the seven days were below $1,160, so $1,560 doesn't describe a typical day.

2

The median

The median is the middle value when the values are sorted. Half are above it and half below.

Sort the values and take the middle one. With an even number of values, average the two in the middle. Here the middle (4th) value is $1,100. That's a much better picture of a normal day. One unusual value pulls the mean a long way but barely moves the median.

Tip When the mean and median are far apart, look for unusual values before you report either one. Report both if it helps.

In Excel =AVERAGE(B2:H2) and =MEDIAN(B2:H2).

Helps with Topic 3, when you check unusual values.

3

The weighted average

A weighted average gives each value more or less influence depending on its size, such as its share of sales.

Cost of goods was 61.9% of sales for shoes and 60.5% for everything else. The simple average of those two is 61.2%. But shoes are only about a third of sales, so the true overall figure is lower:

Sales ($k)Cost of goods ($k)Cost as % of sales
Shoes787.5487.561.9%
Everything else1,522.5921.560.5%
All products2,3101,40961.0%

The right way is total cost ÷ total sales: 1,409 ÷ 2,310 = 61.0%. Averaging percentages directly treats a small group as if it were as big as a large one.

In Excel =SUMPRODUCT(B2:B3, D2:D3)/SUM(B2:B3) gives a weighted average of the percentages in column D, weighted by the sales in column B. Or simply =SUM(C2:C3)/SUM(B2:B3).

Helps with Topics 6, 9 and 12.

Spread: how far apart are the values?​

Two stores can have the same average sales, with one steady and one swinging wildly. An average alone hides that, so describe the spread too.

1

Range and quartiles

The range is the highest value minus the lowest. It's simple, but one unusual value can stretch it.

Quartiles split sorted values into four equal parts. About a quarter of the values are below the first quartile (Q1), and about three quarters are below the third (Q3). The gap between them is the interquartile range (IQR).

For S01's seven days, =QUARTILE.INC(B2:H2,1) gives Q1 = 1,035 and =QUARTILE.INC(B2:H2,3) gives Q3 = 1,135. The IQR is 100. A common rule flags anything more than 1.5 × IQR beyond the quartiles as an outlier: here, anything above 1,285 or below 885. The $4,500 day is flagged.

Tip An outlier is a value to check, not a value to delete. This one is a real business order. Topic 3 shows what to do with each kind.

Helps with Topic 3.

2

Standard deviation

Standard deviation measures how far values typically sit from their mean. A bigger number means more spread.

You'll see it in reports and in Excel (=STDEV.S(B2:H2)). Like the mean, it's pulled a long way by unusual values, so look at the data before you trust it.

3

Read a histogram

A histogram groups values into ranges and shows how many fall in each. The shape tells you where most values sit and whether there's a long tail.

This month's 280 online orders, by order value (number of orders)

Most orders are between $25 and $75. A smaller group of larger orders sits on the right, and the $100+ bar hides how large the biggest ones are. Large orders like these tend to pull the mean above the median.

Helps with Topic 8.

Correlation is not causation​

Correlation means two things tend to move together. It doesn't tell you that one causes the other.

Each dot is one month: advertising spend vs sales, $ thousands

Months with more advertising had higher sales, so it's tempting to say advertising drove sales. But the shop spends most on advertising in November and December, when people are buying gifts anyway. The season could explain both, so the chart alone can't tell you how much the advertising added.

Before you claim one thing causes another, ask:

  • Could a third thing, like the season, be driving both?
  • Could it run the other way? Busy months may simply get bigger advertising budgets.
  • Is there a fair comparison, such as the same months last year, or stores that didn't advertise?

Tip Say “moved together” or “is linked to” when that's all you know. Save “caused” for when you've ruled out the other explanations.

Helps with Topics 8, 10 and 12.

Rounding and showing numbers honestly​

  • Round to a level that fits the decision. “$2.31 million” is clearer than “$2,310,417”, and no less true for a strategy discussion.
  • Keep full precision in your calculations, and round only what you show.
  • When rounded parts don't add up to the rounded total, say so in a note rather than changing a number.
  • Don't show more decimals than the data supports. A forecast of “12.47% growth” suggests far more certainty than any forecast has.

Helps with every topic.

Try it yourself (optional)

1. Sales rose from $2.10m to $2.31m. What's the percent change? If they then fell back to $2.10m, what would that percent change be?

2. Operating margin rose from 8% to 10%. Describe the change in percentage points and as a percent change.

3. Five business invoices are $1k, $2k, $2k, $3k and $42k. Which better describes a typical invoice, the mean or the median?

Show the answer

1. (2.31 − 2.10) ÷ 2.10 = +10%. Falling back: (2.10 − 2.31) ÷ 2.31 = −9.1%.

2. Up 2 percentage points, which is a 25% increase (2 ÷ 8).

3. The median, $2k. The mean is $10k (50 ÷ 5), pulled up by one very large invoice.

Remember

  • Percent change = (new − old) ÷ old. Always divide by the starting value.
  • Say “points” for the difference between two percentages.
  • The median resists unusual values. When the mean and median differ a lot, look at the data.
  • For an overall percentage, use totals (a weighted average), not the average of percentages.
  • Describe the spread as well as the average, and check outliers rather than deleting them.
  • Correlation is not causation.
