Read a business through its numbers

Every business decision ends up as numbers: what was sold, what it cost, what's left, and where the cash went.

What you'll be able to do: read an income statement, a balance sheet and a cash flow statement, and explain how a business can make a profit and still run short of cash. You'll also learn the six-step method the whole course is built on.

This topic teaches you to read the three reports every business produces, and the six-step method this whole course uses to turn numbers into decisions. By the end, you'll be able to explain how a business can make a good profit and still run short of cash.

Meet the shop​

Throughout this course, you'll follow one business: a shop with six stores in four regions (North, South, East and West). It sells shoes, bags and other products in its stores and online, and it also sells to some business customers, who pay later on an invoice.

This year its sales were $2.31 million, up 10% from $2.10 million. The owner is thinking about opening a seventh store. By the end of this topic, you'll be able to tell her whether she can afford it.

This is an invented shop. All numbers are examples.

The three financial statements​

Financial statements are formal reports that summarize a business's financial activity and position.

Three of them matter most. Each one answers a different question, and together they give you the full picture.

StatementThe question it answersWhat it covers
Income statementDid we make money?A period, such as a year
Balance sheetWhat do we own and owe?One date, such as December 31
Cash flow statementWhere did the cash come from, and where did it go?A period, such as a year
1

The income statement: did we make money?

The income statement shows revenue, expenses and profit for a period of time. It is also called the profit and loss statement, or P&L.

Read it from the top down. Each line takes away a cost, and each profit line shows what is left at that stage. Numbers in brackets are subtracted.

  • Gross profit is what is left after paying for the goods you sold.
  • Operating income is what is left after running the business too.
  • Net profit is what is left after interest and tax. It's the “bottom line”.

Example Of every $100 of sales, about $61 paid for the goods, $31 ran the stores, and about $6 was left as net profit.

Tip When someone says “profit”, ask which one they mean. Gross profit, operating income and net profit answer different questions.

2

The balance sheet: what do we own and owe?

The balance sheet shows what a business owns, what it owes and what belongs to its owners, at a single date.

What it owns are its assets. What it owes are its liabilities. What is left for the owners is equity. The two sides always match:

The balance sheet ruleAssets = Liabilities + Equity

It balances because equity is whatever is left after you subtract what is owed from what is owned. Every asset was paid for with either borrowed money or the owner's money.

Tip A balance sheet is a snapshot. Compare two dates to see what changed. Here, customers owe $70k more and the shop holds $30k more stock.

3

The cash flow statement: where did the cash go?

The cash flow statement shows the cash that came into and went out of a business during a period.

It sorts the cash into three groups:

  • Operating: running the business day to day.
  • Investing: buying or selling long-term things, such as store fittings. This spending is called capital expenditure.
  • Financing: money to and from lenders and owners, such as loan repayments.

Tip Depreciation is added back because it spreads the cost of the fittings over several years. It reduces profit, but no cash leaves when it is recorded. The cash left when the fittings were bought.

4

How the three connect

Accrual accounting records income when it is earned and costs when they are incurred, whether or not cash has changed hands yet.

Most businesses keep their accounts this way. A sale counts on the day it's made, even if the customer pays next month. That's why profit and cash can tell different stories. The statements are linked like this:

  • Net profit of $140k is the first line of the cash flow statement.
  • It also adds to the owner's equity, which grew from $560k to $700k, because the owner took no money out.
  • Changes on the balance sheet (money owed by customers, stock, money owed to suppliers) explain why operating cash was $100k lower than profit.
  • The cash at the end of the cash flow statement, $340k, is the cash on the balance sheet.

Example A business customer buys $5,000 of bags in December and pays in January. The sale counts in this year's revenue and profit, but the cash arrives next year.

Tip If profit looks healthy but cash is falling, look first at money owed by customers and inventory on the balance sheet.

The method: six steps from numbers to a decision​

Reading statements is only the start. The whole course follows one method, the Financial Data-to-Insight (FDI) framework. Each step answers one question. Do them in this order.

1

Assess: what am I looking at?

Assessing data means checking what each column represents, where it came from, and whether it is complete, consistent and measured correctly, before you use it.

In practice, open the file and read it before you calculate anything: the column names, the first few rows, and any notes at the top or bottom.

Example One report shows sales in thousands of dollars and another in dollars. If you mix them up, your answer is 1,000 times wrong.

Tip Always check the unit (dollars, thousands, millions) and the time period before anything else.

2

Inquire: what do I want to know?

A well-formed business question is a specific, measurable question about a defined number and time period, whose answer will inform a particular decision.

Pick one clear question. A vague question like “How are we doing?” has no clear answer. A specific one tells you exactly where to look.

Example Instead of “Is the business okay?”, ask “Why did our cash go down this year, even though sales went up?”

Tip A good question names the number, the time period, and the decision it will help with.

3

Analyze: what do the numbers say?

Analysis is applying calculations or methods to data to answer a defined question.

Now do the math that answers your question. This could be comparing two years, working out a percentage, or adding up where money came from and where it went.

Example To find out why cash fell, list the money that came in and the money that went out, and see which items were biggest.

Tip Your question decides the math, not the other way around.

4

Visualize: how do I show it?

Data visualization is the presentation of data in graphical form, such as charts, graphs and maps, so information is easier to understand and communicate.

The right chart depends on your message: a line for change over time, bars to compare things side by side, and a waterfall to show what added to a total or took away from it.

Example A bar chart showing each reason cash went up or down is much clearer than a list of numbers.

Tip One chart, one message. If it needs a long explanation, it's too complicated.

5

Interpret: so what?

Interpretation is explaining what a result means for the business and recommending an action based on it.

This is the step people care about most. Your manager rarely needs every number. They need to know what it means for them and what to do.

Example “Cash fell 15%” is only a fact. “Cash fell 15%, partly because customers owe us $70k more, so let's find out why before we spend” is an answer.

Tip Always finish with a recommendation, not just a description.

6

Prompt: can AI help me do this faster?

A prompt is the set of instructions and data you give an AI tool to produce an answer. A good prompt states the role, the context, the task, the data, the format you want, and any rules to follow.

Once you understand the steps, AI tools can help you repeat them quickly on new data. But AI only gives good answers when you give it clear instructions and clean data.

Example Tell the AI what the data is, what question you're asking, and ask it to show its working.

Tip Check AI's math and its conclusions yourself, because it can sound sure and still be wrong. And never paste confidential company data into a public AI tool.

One more thing: it's a loop. When you reach step 5, you'll often find a new question, and you start again from step 1.

Let's see it in action​

The owner wants to open a seventh store, which would cost about $250,000. Sales are up 10% and profit is $140k, so she thinks she can afford it. Let's use the six steps to check.

Step 1 · Assess

We have the three statements for the year, January to December, in $ thousands. The balance sheet balances, and the cash at the end of the cash flow statement matches the balance sheet. The data looks fit to use.

Step 2 · Inquire

Our question: “Why did cash fall by $60k when the shop made a $140k profit, and can it afford a $250k store?”

Step 3 · Analyze

The cash flow statement shows that operations brought in only $40k of cash. Most of the profit is tied up in $70k more owed by customers and $30k more stock, and $20k went to paying suppliers down. Then $80k went on refits and $20k on the loan.

Step 4 · Visualize

A waterfall chart shows each reason cash went up or down. Each bar starts where the last one ended.

How the shop's cash moved this year, $ thousands

Step 5 · Interpret

If she spends $250k on a new store, she's left with $90k. The shop's fixed running costs (rent, wages and bills, not counting the stock it buys) are about $40k a month (about $480k of the $716k a year; the rest either changes with sales, such as card fees and delivery, or isn't cash, such as depreciation), so $90k covers just over two months. A common rule of thumb is to keep enough cash for about three months of fixed running costs, in case sales dip. Here that's $120k, so she'd be $30k short. And having enough cash isn't the whole answer: she'd still need to check whether the new store would earn enough to pay back its cost.

Step 6 · Prompt

Next year, she could give the same statements to an AI tool and ask it to repeat the check, as long as she checks its math and doesn't paste confidential data into a public tool.

The decision

Don't commit to the new store yet. Find out why customers owe more and fix any late payments first. Then check whether the store would earn enough to be worth it. Topic 7 shows how.

This is an invented shop. All numbers are examples.

Try it yourself (optional)

The shop's net profit was $140k, but its cash from operating activities was only $40k. Using the cash flow statement, which lines explain the $100k gap?

Show the answer

Three lines used cash: customers owed $70k more, $30k more went into stock, and the shop paid suppliers down by $20k. That's $120k.

Depreciation went the other way: it reduced profit by $20k without using any cash this year. So the gap is $120k − $20k = $100k.

What this looks like at work​

In your first finance job, you'll use these six steps all the time, often without anyone calling them that. For example:

  • Your manager asks why this month's costs came in higher than the budget.
  • A team wants money for a new project, and you're asked whether it's worth it.
  • Sales are up, but the bank balance is down, and someone wants to know why.

Each time, the order is the same: check the data, pin down the question, do the math, show it simply, and say what to do.

How you'd say it to your manager​

Your analysis is only useful if people understand it. Keep your message short: what happened, why, and what you suggest.

Example message

Hi Maya, I looked at why our cash fell $60k this year while sales grew 10%. The main reasons are $70k more owed to us by customers, which looks like slower payment (I'll confirm with the aging report), and $80k of one-off store refits. If we open the new store now, we'd have about $90k left, which is under three months of fixed running costs. I'd suggest we sort out any slow payers first, then look at whether the new store would pay for itself before we commit. Happy to walk you through the numbers.

Notice it starts with the answer, gives the two biggest reasons, and ends with a clear next step.

Remember

  • The income statement asks “did we make money?”, the balance sheet asks “what do we own and owe?”, and the cash flow statement asks “where did the cash go?”
  • Assets = liabilities + equity, always.
  • Profit is not cash. Money owed by customers and stock can soak up the cash a profit brings in.
  • The six FDI steps: Assess, Inquire, Analyze, Visualize, Interpret, Prompt. Don't skip steps 1 and 2.
  • At work, lead with the answer, then the reasons, then what to do.
