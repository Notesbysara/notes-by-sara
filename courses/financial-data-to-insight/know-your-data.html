Know your data

Before you do any math, you need to know exactly what you're looking at. It sounds obvious, but this is where a lot of expensive mistakes begin.

What you'll be able to do: tell what type, shape and level of detail a dataset has, and where it came from. You'll also check its unit, currency and period before you trust a single number.

Here's a quick example. A spreadsheet shows revenue of 4,218. No notes, no labels. What does it mean?

If it's in dollars$4,218
If it's in thousands of dollars$4.2 million
If it's in millions of dollars$4.2 billion

Same number, a million times apart, and nothing in the cell tells you which. This topic shows you the five questions to ask so that never trips you up.

Five questions to ask any data​

Ask these every time you open a new file, in this order.

1

What type is each column?

A data type is the kind of value a column holds. It decides which operations make sense on that column.

What counts: if a value can be meaningfully added or averaged, it's numerical. If it labels a group, it's categorical, even when it's written in digits.

Most columns fall into one of four broad groups:

NumbersSales, units, price. You can add, average and compare them.
CategoriesRegion, product, store ID. You can count and group them, but not add them.
DatesSale date, month end. You can sort them and group them by month or year.
TextDescriptions, notes. You can read and search them, but not calculate with them.

Tip If you type a ZIP code like 02139 into Excel, it drops the zero and shows 2139. Format ID and code columns as Text before you type or import them.

2

What shape is the data?

Financial data usually comes in one of three shapes, and each one answers a different kind of question.

Time series data: a sequence of data points for one thing, recorded at successive, usually equally spaced, points in time.

In plain words: one thing tracked over time, like one shop's sales year by year. It shows you the trend.

Cross-sectional data: observations of one or more variables for many entities at a single point or period in time.

In plain words: many things at one moment, like the profit margin of every store this year. It shows you who's ahead right now.

Panel data (also called longitudinal data): observations of the same entities across multiple time periods.

What counts: it follows the same entities over time. If some are missing in some periods, for example a store that opened partway through, it's still panel data. This is called an unbalanced panel, and it needs extra care when you compare.

In plain words: the same group of things tracked over time, like each store, year by year. It shows you both, and whether the gap is growing. Here, store S01's lead over S03 grew from $0.05m to $0.08m.

Example Comparing Walmart and Target over five years is a panel: two companies, many years.

Tip If your data can't answer your question, check the shape first. One company's numbers alone can't tell you whether it's doing better than its rivals.

3

What does one row stand for?

Granularity (or grain) is the level of detail a single row represents: which entity and which time period each row describes.

What counts: every row in one table should have the same grain. Mixing daily and monthly rows in the same table can make the totals wrong, for example by counting the same sales twice.

In plain words: is one row a single sale? A day? A month? A whole year?

The easiest way to answer it is to finish this sentence: “One row = …”. For example, “One row = one store, on one day.”

Each row should be unique at that level. If two rows match on every part of the grain (here, the same store and the same day), something is wrong: it's probably a duplicate. Topic 3 shows you how to find them.

Tip Totals like sales and units add up from detail, but you can't split a total back into detail, so keep the most detailed version you can get. Averages, percentages and ratios don't add up: margins of 11% and 8% don't make 19%. Recalculate them from the totals instead.

4

Where did it come from?

The general ledger is the main record of all a business's accounts. The financial statements are built from it.

Financial data usually comes from a few kinds of source. Each one is useful for different things, and each has its own traps.

SourceWhat it holdsWatch out for
Sales system (the till, or point of sale)Every sale, item by itemRefunds and voids may be recorded later or elsewhere
General ledgerEvery accounting entry, sorted into accounts (often daily or monthly totals from other systems, not each sale)Numbers can still change until the month-end close is finished
Bank statementsCash in and out of the accountTiming: a payment can reach the bank days after it's recorded
Published reportsAnnual and quarterly figures (the annual ones are audited)Summary figures, not transactions. Past figures are sometimes restated
Spreadsheets someone madeAnythingThe easiest to get wrong. Ask who made it, and from what

Two sources can disagree and both be right. The till might show sales before refunds, while the ledger shows them after refunds. Before you call a difference an error, check whether the two are measuring the same thing over the same period.

Example For a US public company like Walmart, the most reliable annual numbers are in its 10-K, the yearly report it files with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). They are audited, which gives reasonable assurance, not a guarantee.

Tip Write the source of every column in your notes, and ask: “Is this the final, closed number, or could it still change?”

5

What unit, currency and year?

A fiscal year is the accounting year a business or government uses for its financial reporting. It is usually twelve months, though some companies use 52 or 53 weeks, and it may or may not match the calendar year.

Check three things for every number: the scale (dollars, thousands or millions), the currency, and the time period.

Let's see it in action​

Remember the shop from Topic 1? The owner sends you its sales file. Here are the first few rows:

DateStoreRegionProductUnitsSales
2025-03-03S01NorthShoes12780
2025-03-03S01NorthBags8384
2025-03-03S03SouthShoes201,235
2025-03-04S01NorthShoes15975

Let's ask the five questions.

Question 1 · Types

Date is a date. Store, Region and Product are categories. Units and Sales are numbers. Store is a code, not a quantity, so we'll never add it up.

Question 2 · Shape

Many stores over many days, so it's a panel. That means we can see trends and compare stores.

Question 3 · One row

One row = one product, in one store, on one day. So each row is a daily total, not a single sale.

Question 4 · Source

The owner exported it from the till system. So it shows sales as they were rung up, and refunds handled later may not be in it. We note that, and ask how refunds are recorded.

Question 5 · Unit and year

The file doesn't say whether Sales is in dollars or thousands, or whether it's before or after discounts. We ask the owner. Answer: US dollars, after discounts, before tax.

Now we write it all down in a small table called a schema map. It takes two minutes and saves hours later.

A schema map is a table that records, for every column in a dataset, its name, data type, meaning and unit. Many also add where the data came from.

Grain: one row = one product, in one store, on one day.

Show the answer

Invoice no. is an ID, so treat it as a category even though it's written in digits. Customer is a category. Invoice date is a date. Amount and Days late are numbers.

One row = one invoice. The trap: Amount is in thousands of dollars, so 6.5 means $6,500, not $6.50.

What this looks like at work​

Knowing your data is usually the first thing you do in any finance task. For example:

  • In your first week, someone sends you a report and asks you to “take a look”.
  • You're asked to compare this month's actual results with the budget.
  • Two teams send you numbers, and you need to combine them into one report.

In each case, the five questions stop you comparing numbers in different units, different years or different levels of detail.

How you'd say it to your manager​

Asking good questions about the data early shows you're careful, not slow. Keep it short and say what you'll do next.

Example message

Hi Maya, before I start on the sales analysis, two quick checks. Is the Sales column before or after discounts? And is the 2024 report a calendar year or our fiscal year? Also, the budget is monthly by region, so I'll add up the daily store sales to match before comparing. I'll send the first numbers tomorrow.

Notice it asks specific questions, explains the one change you'll make, and gives a clear next step.

Remember

  • A number without a unit, currency and time period can't be trusted.
  • Five questions: what type, what shape, what does one row stand for, where it came from, and what unit and year.
  • Codes and IDs look like numbers, but they're categories.
  • Totals add up from detail, never back down. Averages and percentages must be recalculated, not added.
  • Write a schema map before you analyze anything.
