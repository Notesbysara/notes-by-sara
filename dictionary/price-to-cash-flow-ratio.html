Price-to-Cash-Flow Ratio

Intermediate

The price-to-cash-flow ratio compares a company's share price with its operating cash flow per share, showing how much investors are paying for each unit of cash the business generates.

The ratio is calculated by dividing the share price by operating cash flow per share, or equivalently, market capitalization by total operating cash flow.

For example, a company with a market capitalization of 1.2 billion and operating cash flow of 150 million has a price-to-cash-flow ratio of 8.

Some investors prefer cash flow to earnings because it is harder to influence through accounting choices and is not reduced by non-cash charges such as depreciation and amortization. A company with steady profits but weak cash flow may be facing problems, such as customers paying slowly, that the price-to-earnings ratio would not reveal.

A low price-to-cash-flow ratio can suggest a share is good value, while a high one suggests investors expect strong growth. As with other multiples, comparisons are most useful within the same industry.

The ratio does not account for capital expenditure, the money a business must spend to maintain and grow its assets. For that reason, analysts often also look at the price-to-free-cash-flow ratio, which subtracts capital spending.
