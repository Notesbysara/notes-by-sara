Price Elasticity of Demand

Intermediate

Price elasticity of demand measures how strongly the quantity of a good that people buy responds to a change in its price.

Elasticity compares the percentage change in quantity demanded with the percentage change in price. If a small price rise causes a large fall in purchases, demand is elastic. If purchases barely change, demand is inelastic.

Suppose a café raises the price of coffee from 4.00 to 4.40, about a 10% increase, and weekly sales fall from 1,000 to 920 cups, about an 8% decrease. The elasticity is roughly −0.8. Because the figure is less than 1 in absolute size, demand is inelastic: sales fell, but proportionally less than the price rose. The café's revenue actually rises, from 4,000 to 4,048 a week.

Demand tends to be more elastic when close substitutes exist, when the product takes up a large share of a budget, when it is a luxury and over longer periods, as people have time to adjust. Essential medicines and fuel in the short term tend to have inelastic demand. A particular brand of soft drink tends to have elastic demand.

Elasticity helps businesses set prices and helps governments predict how taxes will affect consumption and revenue.
