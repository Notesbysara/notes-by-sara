<div className="nbs-head"><p className="eyebrow">Topic 4 of 12 · Step 2: Inquire</p> <h1>Ask a question worth answering</h1> <p className="lead">“Can you take a look at the numbers?” is one of the most common requests in finance, and one of the hardest to answer well.</p> <p>A vague question can keep you busy for weeks and still leave everyone unsure what you found. A sharp question tells you what data to pull, what to calculate and when you're done. Your analysis can only be as good as the question behind it.</p></div>

## Four tests for a good question

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="def"><p>A well-formed <b>business question</b> is a specific, measurable question about a defined number and time period, whose answer will inform a particular decision.</p></div> <p>Check your question against these four tests before you start.</p> <div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Is it specific?</h3></div> <p>It names exactly what you're looking at: which number, which part of the business.</p><p className="eg"><b>Example:</b> “How are sales?” is broad. “How did sales at store S03 change?” is specific.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Is it measurable?</h3></div> <p>You know which number would answer it, and you can get that data. Teams often track their most important numbers as <Term slug="key-performance-indicator" name="Key Performance Indicator (KPI)" def="A key performance indicator (KPI) is a measurable value that shows how well a business, team or individual is achieving an important objective.">KPIs</Term>.</p><p className="eg"><b>Example:</b> “Are customers happy?” is hard to measure. “Did repeat purchases fall this quarter?” can be checked in the sales data.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>Is it tied to a decision?</h3></div> <p>Someone will do something differently depending on the answer. If nothing would change, the question probably isn't worth the time. The answer should also be worth more than the effort: two weeks of analysis to save $500 usually isn't.</p><p className="eg"><b>Example:</b> “…so we can decide whether to raise prices next year.”</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">4</span><h3>Is it bounded in time?</h3></div> <p>It names the period you're looking at, and ideally when the answer is needed.</p><p className="eg"><b>Example:</b> “…between 2023 and 2024, before the budget meeting in January.”</p> </div> </div> <p>Here's what that looks like on four everyday questions:</p> <div className="tw"><table className="vs"><tbody><tr><th>Vague</th><th>Sharp</th></tr> <tr><td>“Are we doing well?”</td><td>“Did our operating margin in 2024 reach our 10% target, and should we change prices for 2025?”</td></tr> <tr><td>“Is marketing working?”</td><td>“Did the spring email campaign bring in more extra profit than it cost, between March and May?”</td></tr> <tr><td>“Can you prove the campaign worked?”</td><td>“Did sales in the spring campaign weeks (March to May) rise more than in similar weeks without it, so we can decide whether to run it again?”</td></tr> <tr><td>“Should we worry about cash?”</td><td>“Will our cash at the end of each month cover at least three months of fixed costs over the next six months?”</td></tr></tbody></table></div> <p>Notice the “prove the campaign worked” question. It assumes the answer before you start. A neutral question lets the data decide.</p></div>

## Four kinds of question

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Most business questions fall into one of four kinds. Knowing which one you're asking tells you what work it needs.</p> <div className="tw"><table><tbody><tr><th>Kind</th><th>It asks</th><th>Example</th><th>Usually needs</th></tr> <tr><td><b>Descriptive</b></td><td>What happened?</td><td>How much did sales grow last year?</td><td>Totals, comparisons</td></tr> <tr><td><b>Diagnostic</b></td><td>Why did it happen?</td><td>Why did margin fall?</td><td>Breaking the number into its parts</td></tr> <tr><td><b>Predictive</b></td><td>What is likely to happen?</td><td>What will sales be next quarter?</td><td>Trends, forecasts</td></tr> <tr><td><b>Prescriptive</b></td><td>What should we do?</td><td>Should we raise prices?</td><td>All of the above, plus judgment</td></tr></tbody></table></div> <p>Many real questions combine two: “why did it happen, and what should we do?”</p></div>

## Break a big question into smaller ones

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="def"><p>A <b>hypothesis</b> is a possible explanation that can be tested with data. A <b>hypothesis tree</b> splits one main question into possible causes, then splits those again until each one can be tested.</p></div> <p>Start with the main question at the top. Below it, list the possible reasons. Keep splitting until each branch tells you exactly which data to look at.</p> <div className="def"><p><b>MECE</b> (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive) means the branches don't overlap and, together, cover every likely cause.</p></div> <p>In plain words: no double counting and no gaps. Real causes can still interact, so treat MECE as the aim when you build the tree, not a guarantee.</p> <p className="tip"><b>Tip:</b> write each branch as something data could prove wrong. “Costs went up” is too loose. “Cost per unit rose more than 5% between 2023 and 2024” can be tested.</p></div>

## What this looks like at work

<div className="nbs-sec work"><p>You'll often be handed a worry rather than a question. For example:</p> <ul> <li>Your manager says, “Costs look high this month. Can you check?”</li> <li>In a meeting, someone asks why one region is behind the others.</li> <li>You're asked to “pull some numbers” for a presentation.</li> </ul> <p>Each time, spend five minutes turning it into a sharp question and confirm it with the person who asked. It saves days of work on the wrong thing.</p></div>

## How you'd say it to your manager

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Before you start, check that you're answering the question they actually have.</p> <div className="msg"> <p className="eyebrow">Example message</p> <p className="body">Hi Maya, before I dig in, I'd like to check I'm answering the right question. I'm planning to look at why operating margin fell from 9% to 8% this year, so we can decide on prices, suppliers or stock controls before the budget. The drop shows up in cost of goods, so I'll start there. Does that match what you need, and when do you need it by?</p> </div> <p className="small">Notice it states the question, the decision it supports, where you'll start, and asks for a deadline.</p></div>

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="remember"> <p className="eyebrow">Remember</p> <ul> <li>Your analysis can only be as good as your question.</li> <li>A good question is specific, measurable, tied to a decision and bounded in time.</li> <li>Break big questions into a hypothesis tree, aiming for no overlaps and no gaps.</li> <li>A ratio points you to where to look. The detailed data tells you why.</li> <li>Agree on the question with the person who asked before you start.</li> </ul> </div></div>
