<div className="nbs-head"><p className="eyebrow">Topic 1 of 12</p> <h1>From raw numbers to decisions</h1> <p className="lead">You open a spreadsheet. There are hundreds of numbers. Someone asks, “So what should we do?” and you don't know where to start.</p> <p>That's normal. Numbers on their own don't tell you anything. What you need is a simple order to think in. This topic gives you six steps that take you from a pile of numbers to a clear decision.</p></div>

## The six steps

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Each step answers one question. Do them in this order.</p> <div className="steps"> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">1</span><h3>Assess: what am I looking at?</h3></div><div className="def"><p><b>Assessing data</b> means checking what each column represents, where it came from, and whether it is complete, consistent and measured correctly, before you use it.</p></div> <p>In practice, open the file and read it before you calculate anything: the column names, the first few rows, and any notes at the top or bottom.</p> <p className="eg"><b>Example:</b> one report shows sales in thousands of dollars and another in dollars. If you mix them up, your answer is 1,000 times wrong.</p> <p className="tip"><b>Tip:</b> always check the unit (dollars, thousands, millions) and the time period before anything else.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">2</span><h3>Inquire: what do I want to know?</h3></div><div className="def"><p>A well-formed <b>business question</b> is a specific, measurable question about a defined number and time period, whose answer will inform a particular decision.</p></div> <p>Pick one clear question. A vague question like “How are we doing?” has no clear answer. A specific one tells you exactly where to look.</p> <p className="eg"><b>Example:</b> instead of “Is the business okay?”, ask “Why did our cash go down this year, even though sales went up?”</p> <p className="tip"><b>Tip:</b> a good question names the number, the time period, and the decision it will help with.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">3</span><h3>Analyze: what do the numbers say?</h3></div><div className="def"><p><b>Analysis</b> is applying calculations or methods to data to answer a defined question.</p></div> <p>Now do the math that answers your question. This could be comparing two years, working out a percentage, or adding up where money came from and where it went.</p> <p className="eg"><b>Example:</b> to find out why cash fell, list the money that came in and the money that went out, and see which items were biggest.</p> <p className="tip"><b>Tip:</b> your question decides the math, not the other way around.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">4</span><h3>Visualize: how do I show it?</h3></div><div className="def"><p><Term slug="data-visualization" name="Data Visualization" def="Data visualization is the presentation of data in graphical form, such as charts, graphs and maps, to make information easier to understand, analyse and communicate."><b>Data visualization</b></Term> is the presentation of data in graphical form, such as charts, graphs and maps, so information is easier to understand and communicate.</p></div> <p>The right chart depends on your message: a line for change over time, bars to compare things side by side, and a waterfall to show what added to a total or took away from it.</p> <p className="eg"><b>Example:</b> a bar chart showing each reason cash went up or down is much clearer than a list of numbers.</p> <p className="tip"><b>Tip:</b> one chart, one message. If it needs a long explanation, it's too complicated.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">5</span><h3>Interpret: so what?</h3></div><div className="def"><p><b>Interpretation</b> is explaining what a result means for the business and recommending an action based on it.</p></div> <p>This is the step people care about most. Your manager rarely needs every number. They need to know what it means for them and what to do.</p> <p className="eg"><b>Example:</b> “Cash fell 15%” is only a fact. “Cash fell mainly because customers owe us more, so let's find out why before we spend” is an answer.</p> <p className="tip"><b>Tip:</b> always finish with a recommendation, not just a description.</p> </div> <div className="step"> <div className="step-head"><span className="n">6</span><h3>Prompt: can AI help me do this faster?</h3></div><div className="def"><p>A <b>prompt</b> is the set of instructions and data you give an AI tool to produce an answer. A good prompt states the role, the context, the task, the data, the format you want, and any rules to follow.</p></div> <p>Once you understand the steps, AI tools can help you repeat them quickly on new data. But AI only gives good answers when you give it clear instructions and clean data.</p> <p className="eg"><b>Example:</b> tell the AI what the data is, what question you're asking, and ask it to show its working.</p> <p className="tip"><b>Tip:</b> check AI's math and its conclusions yourself, because it can sound sure and still be wrong. And never paste confidential company data into a public AI tool.</p> </div> </div> <p>One more thing: it's a loop. When you reach step 5, you'll often find a new question, and you start again from step 1.</p></div>

## Let's see it in action

## What this looks like at work

<div className="nbs-sec work"><p>In your first finance job, you'll use these six steps all the time, often without anyone calling them that. For example:</p> <ul> <li>Your manager asks why this month's costs came in higher than the <Term slug="budget" name="Budget" def="A budget is a plan for how money will be earned and spent over a set period, such as a month or a year. It sets limits for spending in different categories and shows how much can be saved.">budget</Term>.</li> <li>A team wants money for a new project, and you're asked whether it's worth it.</li> <li>Sales are up, but the bank balance is down, and someone wants to know why.</li> </ul> <p>Each time, the order is the same: check the data, pin down the question, do the math, show it simply, and say what to do.</p></div>

## How you'd say it to your manager

<div className="nbs-sec"><p>Your analysis is only useful if people understand it. Keep your message short: what happened, why, and what you suggest.</p> <div className="msg"> <p className="eyebrow">Example message</p> <p className="body">Hi Maya, I looked at why our cash fell $60k this year while sales grew 10%. The main reasons are $70k more owed to us by customers, which looks like slower payment (I'll confirm with the aging report), and $80k of one-off store refits. If we open the new store now, we'd have about $90k left, which is under three months of fixed running costs. I'd suggest we sort out any slow payers first, then look at whether the new store would pay for itself before we commit. Happy to walk you through the numbers.</p> </div> <p className="small">Notice it starts with the answer, gives the two biggest reasons, and ends with a clear next step.</p></div>

<div className="nbs-sec"><div className="remember"> <p className="eyebrow">Remember</p> <ul> <li>Numbers alone are not an answer. You need an order to think in.</li> <li>The six steps: Assess, Inquire, Analyze, Visualize, Interpret, Prompt.</li> <li>Don't skip steps 1 and 2. That's where a lot of mistakes start.</li> <li>A business can make a profit and still run low on cash.</li> <li>At work, lead with the answer, then the reasons, then your suggestion.</li> <li>Always end with what to do, not just what happened.</li> </ul> </div></div>
