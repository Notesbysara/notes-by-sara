From raw numbers to decisions

You open a spreadsheet. There are hundreds of numbers. Someone asks, “So what should we do?” and you don't know where to start.

That's normal. Numbers on their own don't tell you anything. What you need is a simple order to think in. This topic gives you six steps that take you from a pile of numbers to a clear decision.

The six steps​

Each step answers one question. Do them in this order.

1

Assess: what am I looking at?

Assessing data means checking what each column represents, where it came from, and whether it is complete, consistent and measured correctly, before you use it.

In practice, open the file and read it before you calculate anything: the column names, the first few rows, and any notes at the top or bottom.

Example: one report shows sales in thousands of dollars and another in dollars. If you mix them up, your answer is 1,000 times wrong.

Tip: always check the unit (dollars, thousands, millions) and the time period before anything else.

2

Inquire: what do I want to know?

A well-formed business question is a specific, measurable question about a defined number and time period, whose answer will inform a particular decision.

Pick one clear question. A vague question like “How are we doing?” has no clear answer. A specific one tells you exactly where to look.

Example: instead of “Is the business okay?”, ask “Why did our cash go down this year, even though sales went up?”

Tip: a good question names the number, the time period, and the decision it will help with.

3

Analyze: what do the numbers say?

Analysis is applying calculations or methods to data to answer a defined question.

Now do the math that answers your question. This could be comparing two years, working out a percentage, or adding up where money came from and where it went.

Example: to find out why cash fell, list the money that came in and the money that went out, and see which items were biggest.

Tip: your question decides the math, not the other way around.

4

Visualize: how do I show it?

Data visualization is the presentation of data in graphical form, such as charts, graphs and maps, so information is easier to understand and communicate.

The right chart depends on your message: a line for change over time, bars to compare things side by side, and a waterfall to show what added to a total or took away from it.

Example: a bar chart showing each reason cash went up or down is much clearer than a list of numbers.

Tip: one chart, one message. If it needs a long explanation, it's too complicated.

5

Interpret: so what?

Interpretation is explaining what a result means for the business and recommending an action based on it.

This is the step people care about most. Your manager rarely needs every number. They need to know what it means for them and what to do.

Example: “Cash fell 15%” is only a fact. “Cash fell mainly because customers owe us more, so let's find out why before we spend” is an answer.

Tip: always finish with a recommendation, not just a description.

6

Prompt: can AI help me do this faster?

A prompt is the set of instructions and data you give an AI tool to produce an answer. A good prompt states the role, the context, the task, the data, the format you want, and any rules to follow.

Once you understand the steps, AI tools can help you repeat them quickly on new data. But AI only gives good answers when you give it clear instructions and clean data.

Example: tell the AI what the data is, what question you're asking, and ask it to show its working.

Tip: check AI's math and its conclusions yourself, because it can sound sure and still be wrong. And never paste confidential company data into a public AI tool.

One more thing: it's a loop. When you reach step 5, you'll often find a new question, and you start again from step 1.

Let's see it in action​

Here's a small shop with six stores. The owner wants to open a seventh store, which would cost about $250,000. She looks at this year's results:

+10%Sales: $2.10m → $2.31m
−15%Cash: $400k → $340k

Sales went up, so the owner thinks she can afford the new store. Let's use the six steps to check.

Step 1 · Assess

We check the files. Sales are in dollars, the year runs January to December, and nothing is missing. The data looks fit to use.

Step 2 · Inquire

Our question: “Why did cash fall by $60,000 when sales went up, and can we afford a $250,000 store?”

Step 3 · Analyze

We list everything that moved the cash during the year:

A cash bridge lists every inflow and outflow of money that explains the change from a starting cash balance to an ending cash balance.

Cash at the start of the year$400k
Profit the shop made+ $140k
More money owed to us by customers (sold, not yet paid)− $70k
Extra stock bought for the shelves− $30k
Fixing up the stores and a new till system− $80k
Paying back part of a loan− $20k
Cash at the end of the year$340k

Check: 400 + 140 − 70 − 30 − 80 − 20 = 340. This is simplified; a full cash flow statement has a few more lines, which come later in the course.

The shop made a good profit. But customers now owe the shop $70k more than they did at the start of the year, so that money hasn't reached the bank yet. In most businesses' accounts, a sale counts the day it's made, even though the cash arrives later. That's how a business can be profitable and still have less cash.

Revenue (sales): income earned from selling goods or services in a period, whether or not the customer has paid yet.

Profit: revenue minus all expenses for the same period.

Cash: money that is immediately available to spend, including coins, banknotes and money in bank accounts that can be withdrawn at any time.

Accounts receivable: the money customers owe a business for goods or services they have received but not yet paid for.

The store fixes were a one-off. The $70k is different. Part of it comes from higher sales, but if customers are also taking longer to pay, it may keep happening. A report of who owes what, and for how long, would show which (it's called an aging report).

Step 4 · Visualize

We turn the table into a waterfall chart. Each bar starts where the last one ended, so the owner sees straight away where the money went.

Step 5 · Interpret

If she spends $250k on a new store, she's left with $90k. The shop's fixed running costs (rent, wages and bills, not counting the stock it buys) are about $40k a month, so $90k covers just over two months. A common rule of thumb is to keep enough cash for about three months of running costs, in case sales dip. Here that's $120k, so she'd be $30k short. And having enough cash isn't the whole answer: she'd still need to check whether the new store would make enough money to pay back its cost.

Step 6 · Prompt

Next year, she could give this same table to an AI tool and ask it to repeat the check, as long as she checks its math.

The decision

Don't commit to the new store yet. Find out why customers owe more and fix any late payments first. Then check whether the store would earn enough to be worth it.

This is an invented shop. All numbers are examples.

What this looks like at work​

In your first finance job, you'll use these six steps all the time, often without anyone calling them that. For example:

  • Your manager asks why this month's costs came in higher than the budget.
  • A team wants money for a new project, and you're asked whether it's worth it.
  • Sales are up, but the bank balance is down, and someone wants to know why.

Each time, the order is the same: check the data, pin down the question, do the math, show it simply, and say what to do.

How you'd say it to your manager​

Your analysis is only useful if people understand it. Keep your message short: what happened, why, and what you suggest.

Example message

Hi Maya, I looked at why our cash fell $60k this year while sales grew 10%. The main reasons are $70k more owed to us by customers, which looks like slower payment (I'll confirm with the aging report), and $80k of one-off store refits. If we open the new store now, we'd have about $90k left, which is under three months of fixed running costs. I'd suggest we sort out any slow payers first, then look at whether the new store would pay for itself before we commit. Happy to walk you through the numbers.

Notice it starts with the answer, gives the two biggest reasons, and ends with a clear next step.

Remember

  • Numbers alone are not an answer. You need an order to think in.
  • The six steps: Assess, Inquire, Analyze, Visualize, Interpret, Prompt.
  • Don't skip steps 1 and 2. That's where a lot of mistakes start.
  • A business can make a profit and still run low on cash.
  • At work, lead with the answer, then the reasons, then your suggestion.
  • Always end with what to do, not just what happened.
