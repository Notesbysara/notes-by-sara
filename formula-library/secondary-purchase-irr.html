Secondaries: Price Versus NAV and Buyer IRR

IntermediateAlternative InvestmentsAlso known as: secondaries and continuation funds, discount to NAV, LP stake sale, unfunded commitment
← SDR Basket ValuationSecurities Lending Income →

Finds: what a buyer earns on a fund stake bought at a discount.

When to use it: Use it to judge a secondary purchase: buying an existing stake in a private fund from a seller who wants cash. The buyer pays a discount to NAV and receives the fund's distributions from then on, so the investment is already partly mature and the J-curve is shorter. The buyer still takes on any unfunded commitment, and the NAV may itself be too high.

The formula

In words: Price paid = reported NAV x (1 − discount); Buyer cash flows = distributions received − further capital the buyer must fund (unfunded commitment); Buyer IRR solves NPV = 0; Buyer multiple = total distributions / (price + unfunded capital called)

Worked example

A buyer pays at a 12% discount to a NAV of 100, and expects distributions of 10, 15, 22, 28, 34 and 18 in years 1 to 6. A further 15 is called in year 2.

Price100 × (1 − 0.12)88
Total distributions127
Total invested (price + call)88 + 15103
Multiple127 ÷ 1031.23
Buyer cash flows by year−88, 10, 0, 22, 28, 34, 18
Buyer IRR6.1%
Cash flows if distributions are 10% lower−88, 9, −1.50, 19.80, 25.20, 30.60, 16.20
IRR in that case2.9%
The answer

The price is 88. The buyer receives 127 in total and funds 103 (price plus the call), a multiple of 1.23. The IRR is 6.1%. If the NAV is overstated by 10% (distributions fall by 10%), the IRR becomes 2.9%.
