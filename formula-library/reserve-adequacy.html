Reserve Adequacy Metrics

Intermediate

Also known as: exchange rate regimes, foreign exchange reserves, Greenspan-Guidotti, IMF ARA metric, import cover

Whether reserves are large enough for the exchange rate regime. Use it to judge whether a country can defend its exchange rate regime. A peg or tightly managed rate needs reserves to meet capital outflows and pay for imports; a floating rate needs less. Three common tests are months of imports covered, cover for short-term debt, and the IMF composite metric (a ratio of 100% to 150% of the metric is regarded as adequate). The weights shown are the fixed-regime weights, and the IMF publishes different ones for other regimes.

The formula

Import cover=12 RImports,RSTD≥1,ARA=0.05X+0.05M2+0.30 STD+0.15 OL\text{Import cover}=\frac{12\,R}{\text{Imports}},\quad \frac{R}{STD}\ge1,\quad ARA=0.05X+0.05M2+0.30\,STD+0.15\,OL

Import cover (months) = reserves / (annual imports / 12); Short-term debt cover = reserves / short-term external debt (Greenspan-Guidotti: at least 1); IMF reserve adequacy metric (fixed exchange rate) = 5% of exports + 5% of broad money + 30% of short-term debt + 15% of other liabilities (weights for a fixed regime; floating regimes use lower ones)

What each symbol means

Worked example

Reserves are 180 billion, imports 600, exports 400, broad money 700, short-term external debt 90 and other external liabilities 150 (billions).

  1. Import cover in months180 / (600 / 12)= 3.6
  2. Short-term debt cover180 / 90= 2.00
  3. IMF metric (fixed regime) = 5% X + 5% M2 + 30% STD + 15% OL0.05 x 400+0.05 x 700+0.30 x 90+0.15 x 150= 104.5
  4. Reserves / IMF metric180 / 104.5= 172%

Reserves cover 3.6 months of imports and 2.00 times short-term debt, which passes the rule of 1. The IMF metric comes to 104.5, so reserves are 172% of it, above the 100% level.
