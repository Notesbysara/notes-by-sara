Expected Shortfall (CVaR)

Intermediate

Also known as: CVaR, conditional value at risk, average tail loss, ES

The average loss beyond VaR. Use it to answer "how bad is it when VaR is breached?". VaR gives the threshold and ES gives the average loss beyond it. ES is always larger than VaR, and regulators now prefer it (Basel FRTB uses ES at 97.5%) because it captures the shape of the tail.

The formula

ES=V(σφ(zc)1−c−μ),EShist=−V×average of returns below the (1−c) percentileES=V\left(\sigma\frac{\varphi(z_{c})}{1-c}-\mu\right),\quad ES_{hist}=-V\times\text{average of returns below the }(1-c)\text{ percentile}

Parametric ES = V x (sd x pdf(z) / (1 - c) - mean); Historical ES = -V x the average of the returns that fall at or below the VaR cut-off

Worked example

A portfolio of 1,000,000 has daily returns with a mean of 0.03% and a standard deviation of 1.2%. For the historical sample used in the previous entry, what are the 90% VaR and expected shortfall? Compare with the 97.5% parametric figures.

  1. z-score= 1.960
  2. Parametric VaR = V x (z x sd - mu)1,000,000 x (1.95996 x sd-0.0003)= 23,219.57
  3. Parametric ES = V x (sd x pdf(z) / (1 - c) - mu)= 27,753.63
  4. ES / VaR27753.6 / var= 1.20
  5. Historical VaR = -V x percentile= 20,600.00
  6. Historical ES = -V x average of returns at or below the percentile= 28,500.00

The parametric 97.5% VaR is 23,219.57 and the expected shortfall is 27,753.63, 1.20 times larger. In the historical sample, the 90% VaR is 20,600.00 and the expected shortfall is 28,500.00.
