Expected Shortfall (CVaR)

IntermediateRisk ManagementAlso known as: CVaR, conditional value at risk, average tail loss, ES
← Expected Return of a PortfolioExpected Value →

Finds: the average loss beyond VaR.

When to use it: Use it to answer "how bad is it when VaR is breached?". VaR gives the threshold and ES gives the average loss beyond it. ES is always larger than VaR, and regulators now prefer it (Basel FRTB uses ES at 97.5%) because it captures the shape of the tail.

FormulaExampleCalculator

The formula

ES\displaystyle ES=V(σφ(zc)1−c−μ)\displaystyle =V\left(\sigma\frac{\varphi(z_{c})}{1-c}-\mu\right)EShist\displaystyle ES_{hist}=−V×average of returns below the (1−c) percentile\displaystyle =-V\times\text{average of returns below the }(1-c)\text{ percentile}

In words: Parametric ES = V x (sd x pdf(z) / (1 − c) − mean); Historical ES = -V x the average of the returns that fall at or below the VaR cut-off

What each symbol means

Worked example

A portfolio of 1,000,000 has daily returns with a mean of 0.03% and a standard deviation of 1.2%. For the historical sample used in the previous entry, what are the 90% VaR and expected shortfall? Compare with the 97.5% parametric figures.

z-score1.960
Parametric VaR1,000,000 × (1.95996 × sd − 0.0003)23,219.57
Parametric ES27,753.63
ES / VaR27,753.6 ÷ var1.20
Historical VaR20,600
Historical ES28,500
The answer

The parametric 97.5% VaR is 23,219.57 and the expected shortfall is 27,753.63, 1.20 times larger. In the historical sample, the 90% VaR is 20,600 and the expected shortfall is 28,500.
