Credit Valuation Adjustment (CVA)

IntermediateDerivativesAlso known as: counterparty credit risk, expected exposure, OTC derivatives credit charge, bilateral risk
← Credit Utilization RatioCross Rates and Triangular Arbitrage →

Finds: the expected loss from a derivative counterparty's default.

When to use it: Use it to price the risk that the other side of an over-the-counter derivative fails to pay. CVA is the expected loss from counterparty default, and banks deduct it from the value of their derivatives. Central clearing reduces this risk by guaranteeing the trades.

FormulaExampleCalculator

The formula

CVA\displaystyle CVA=(1−R)∑tEEt PDt DFt\displaystyle =(1-R)\sum_{t}EE_{t}\,PD_{t}\,DF_{t}

In words: CVA = (1 − Recovery) x Sum over each year of [Expected positive exposure x Probability of default in that year x Discount factor]

What each symbol means

RRecovery rate on the claim against the counterpartypercent
EE_tExpected positive exposure in year t: the average amount the counterparty would owe if it defaulted thenmoney
PD_tProbability the counterparty defaults in year t, after surviving earlier yearsper year
DF_tDiscount factor for year tnumber

Worked example

A bank's expected exposure to a counterparty over the next five years is 2, 3, 3.5, 3 and 2 million. The counterparty has a 1.5% annual default probability, a 40% recovery and the discount rate is 3%. What is the CVA?

Probability of default in each year0.0150, 0.0148, 0.0146, 0.0143, 0.0141
Discount factor for each year0.9709, 0.9426, 0.9151, 0.8885, 0.8626
Exposure x probability x discount factor for each year0.0291, 0.0418, 0.0466, 0.0382, 0.0244
CVA0.1081
The answer

The CVA is 0.1081 million. That is the amount by which the derivatives' value is reduced to allow for the chance that the counterparty does not pay.
