Credit Valuation Adjustment (CVA)

Intermediate

Also known as: counterparty credit risk, expected exposure, OTC derivatives credit charge, bilateral risk

The expected loss from a derivative counterparty's default. Use it to price the risk that the other side of an over-the-counter derivative fails to pay. CVA is the expected loss from counterparty default, and banks deduct it from the value of their derivatives. Central clearing reduces this risk by guaranteeing the trades.

The formula

CVA=(1−R)∑tEEt PDt DFtCVA=(1-R)\sum_{t}EE_{t}\,PD_{t}\,DF_{t}

CVA = (1 - Recovery) x Sum over each year of [Expected positive exposure x Probability of default in that year x Discount factor]

What each symbol means

SymbolMeaningUnit
RRecovery rate on the claim against the counterpartypercent
EE_tExpected positive exposure in year t: the average amount the counterparty would owe if it defaulted thenmoney
PD_tProbability the counterparty defaults in year t, after surviving earlier yearsper year
DF_tDiscount factor for year tnumber

Worked example

A bank's expected exposure to a counterparty over the next five years is 2, 3, 3.5, 3 and 2 million. The counterparty has a 1.5% annual default probability, a 40% recovery and the discount rate is 3%. What is the CVA?

  1. Probability of default in each year = PD x (1 - PD)^(t - 1)= 0.0150, 0.0148, 0.0146, 0.0143, 0.0141
  2. Discount factor for each year= 0.9709, 0.9426, 0.9151, 0.8885, 0.8626
  3. Exposure x probability x discount factor for each year= 0.0291, 0.0418, 0.0466, 0.0382, 0.0244
  4. CVA = (1 - R) x sum= 0.1081

The CVA is 0.1081 million. That is the amount by which the derivatives' value is reduced to allow for the chance that the counterparty does not pay.
