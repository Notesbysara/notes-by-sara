Stress Test Loss and Capital Buffer

BeginnerRisk ManagementAlso known as: scenario analysis, reverse stress test, shock scenario, stress testing
← Straight-Line DepreciationStudent Loan Interest Accrual and Capitalization →

Finds: the loss in a named scenario and how it compares with capital.

When to use it: Use it to see what happens in a specific bad scenario, such as an equity crash or a credit shock, without relying on a statistical model. A reverse stress test works the other way: it asks how large the shock must be to wipe out capital.

The formula

Loss\displaystyle \text{Loss}=V∑iwi si\displaystyle =V\sum_{i}w_{i}\,s_{i}Multiple to breach\displaystyle \text{Multiple to breach}=CapitalLoss\displaystyle =\frac{\text{Capital}}{\text{Loss}}

In words: Scenario loss = Portfolio value x sum of (Weight of asset i x Shock to asset i); Multiple of the shock that exhausts capital = Capital / Loss

What each symbol means

w_iShare of the portfolio in asset class ipercent
s_iPercentage price move assumed for asset class i in the scenariopercent
CapitalLoss-absorbing capital or risk budgetmoney

Worked example

A 100 million portfolio holds 40% in shares, 30% in corporate bonds, 20% in property and 10% in government bonds. A crisis scenario assumes moves of −30%, −10%, −15% and +4%. Capital is 12 million.

Portfolio return in the scenario−17.60%
Loss in money−100 × −0.17617.60
Capital as a share of the loss12 ÷ 17.668.2%
Loss as a multiple of capital17.6 ÷ 121.47
Shock multiple that uses up all capital12 ÷ 17.60.68
The answer

The scenario gives a portfolio return of −17.60%, a loss of 17.60 million. The capital buffer of 12 million covers only 68.2% of that loss, so the loss is 1.47 times the buffer. Capital would be exhausted by a shock about 0.68 times the size assumed, which here is less than one, so the buffer already fails.
