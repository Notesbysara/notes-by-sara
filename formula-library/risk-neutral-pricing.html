Risk-Neutral Probability and Pricing

IntermediateQuantitative FinanceAlso known as: risk neutral valuation, equivalent martingale measure, state prices, Q measure
← Risk PremiumRule of 40 →

Finds: an option price from risk-neutral probabilities.

When to use it: Use it to price a derivative without knowing investors' risk preferences. Pretend the world is risk-neutral, so the stock earns the risk-free rate, compute the expected payoff and discount at the risk-free rate. The real-world probability and expected return do not enter the price.

FormulaExampleCalculator

The formula

q\displaystyle q=S0erT−SdSu−Sd\displaystyle =\frac{S_{0}e^{rT}-S_{d}}{S_{u}-S_{d}}V0\displaystyle V_{0}=e−rT[q Vu+(1−q) Vd]\displaystyle =e^{-rT}\left[q\,V_{u}+(1-q)\,V_{d}\right]

In words: Risk-neutral probability of the up state = (Spot x e^(rT) − Down price) / (Up price − Down price); Value = e^(-rT) x [q x Payoff if up + (1 − q) x Payoff if down]

What each symbol means

qRisk-neutral probability: the probability that makes the stock grow at the risk-free rate on averagepercent
S_u, S_dStock price in the up and down statemoney
V_u, V_dOption payoff in each statemoney
rRisk-free rateper year

Worked example

A stock at 100 goes to 120 or 90 in a year, the risk-free rate is 5% and the real-world chance of an up move is 60%. Price a call with a strike of 100.

q(100 × exp(0.05 × 1) − sd) ÷ (120 − sd)50.42%
Real-world probability0.660%
Call payoff if upmax(120 − 100, 0)20
Call payoff if downmax(sd − 100, 0)0
Priceexp(−0.05 × 1) × (0.504237 × 20 + (1 − 0.504237) × 0)9.59
Using the real-world probability instead (wrong)exp(−0.05 × 1) × (0.6 × 20 + (1 − 0.6) × 0)11.41
The answer

The risk-neutral probability of an up move is 50.42%, lower than the real-world 60%. The call pays 20 or 0, so it is worth 9.59. Using the real probability instead would give 11.41, which is wrong because discounting at the risk-free rate does not reflect the risk.
