Expectations Theory with a Liquidity Premium

IntermediateFixed IncomeAlso known as: liquidity preference, segmented markets, pure expectations, term premium
← Expanded Accounting EquationExpected and Unexpected Credit Loss →

Finds: what the curve implies about expected rates and risk premium.

When to use it: Use it to see what the yield curve says about expected future rates. Under pure expectations the long rate is the average of expected short rates (L = 0). Liquidity preference adds a premium that makes the curve slope up even if rates are not expected to rise. Segmentation theory says each maturity has its own supply and demand, so no such formula links them.

FormulaExampleCalculator

The formula

(1+s2)2\displaystyle (1+s_{2})^{2}=(1+s1) (1+E[r2]+L)\displaystyle =(1+s_{1})\,(1+E[r_{2}]+L)

In words: (1 + two-year spot)^2 = (1 + one-year spot) x (1 + expected one-year rate next year + liquidity premium)

What each symbol means

s_1, s_2One-year and two-year spot ratesper year
E[r_2]Expected one-year rate in a year's timeper year
LLiquidity (term) premium investors need for tying money up for longerper year

Worked example

The one-year rate is 2.0% and investors expect the one-year rate to be 3.0% next year. They need a liquidity premium of 0.5% for the second year. What two-year rate results under each view?

Two-year rate, pure expectationssqrt((1 + 0.02) × (1 + 0.03)) − 12.499%
Two-year rate with premiumsqrt((1 + 0.02) × (1 + 0.03 + 0.005)) − 12.747%
Premium in the long rate0.0274726 − 0.02498780.248%
The answer

Pure expectations gives a two-year rate of 2.499%. With the liquidity premium it rises to 2.747%, so part of the upward slope is compensation for tying money up, not an expected rise in rates.
