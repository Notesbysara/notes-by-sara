Taylor Rule

IntermediateEconomics for FinanceAlso known as: monetary policy rule, policy rate, interest rate smoothing, neutral rate
← Tail Risk: Cornish-Fisher VaRTerminal Value (Exit Multiple Method) →

Finds: the policy rate a simple rule suggests.

When to use it: Use it as a benchmark for where policy might be. The rule raises rates when inflation is above target or the economy is running hot. Central banks do not follow it mechanically, and inputs such as r* and the gap are uncertain, so compare it with the actual rate to judge how tight or loose policy is. QE and QT are used when the rate is near zero or to unwind that.

The formula

i∗\displaystyle i^{*}=r∗+π+0.5(π−π∗)+0.5 gap\displaystyle =r^{*}+\pi+0.5(\pi-\pi^{*})+0.5\,\text{gap}it\displaystyle i_{t}=ρ it−1+(1−ρ) i∗\displaystyle =\rho\,i_{t-1}+(1-\rho)\,i^{*}

In words: Prescribed policy rate = neutral real rate + inflation + 0.5 x (inflation − target) + 0.5 x output gap; With smoothing, rate = rho x last rate + (1 − rho) x prescribed rate

What each symbol means

r*Neutral real interest rate (an estimate)percent
piCurrent inflationpercent
pi*Inflation targetpercent
gapOutput gap, as a percent of potential outputpercent
rhoSmoothing weight on the last ratenumber

Worked example

The neutral real rate is 1%, inflation 3.5% against a 2% target, the output gap −1%. The policy rate is 5% now.

Prescribed rate0.01 + 0.035 + 0.5 × (0.035 − 0.02) + 0.5 × −0.014.75%
Actual minus prescribed0.05 − 0.04750.25%
Policy stance (11 if 0.0025<0 else 00
Next rate with smoothing0.8 × 0.05 + (1 − 0.8) × 0.04754.95%
The answer

The rule prescribes 4.75%. The actual rate of 5% is 0.25% from it, so the stance flag is 0 (1 = looser than the rule, 0 = tighter or equal). With smoothing, the next rate would be 4.95%.
