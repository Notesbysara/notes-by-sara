Insurance Underwriting Profit and Float

Intermediate

Also known as: insurance companies, cost of float, combined ratio and investment income, Berkshire float

Whether an insurer's float is cheap. Use it to see how insurers make money. Premiums arrive before claims are paid, so the insurer invests the float. An underwriting loss (combined ratio above 100%) can still be profitable if the float is cheap, that is, the cost of float is below what the investments earn or the risk-free rate. A long tail of liabilities, as in liability insurance, makes the float larger and longer.

The formula

UW=P−Claims−Expenses,Cost of float=−UWFloat,Pretax profit=UW+y×FloatUW=P-Claims-Expenses,\quad \text{Cost of float}=\frac{-UW}{\text{Float}},\quad \text{Pretax profit}=UW+y\times\text{Float}

Underwriting profit = premiums - claims - expenses; Cost of float = underwriting loss / float (negative if underwriting is profitable); Pretax profit = underwriting profit + investment yield x float

What each symbol means

PPremiums earnedmoney

Worked example

Premiums earned are 1,000, claims 700 and expenses 330. The float is 1,500 and earns 5%. The risk-free rate is 4.5%.

  1. Combined ratio = (claims + expenses) / premiums(700+330) / 1000= 103.0%
  2. Underwriting result1000-700-330= -30.00
  3. Cost of float = -underwriting result / float--30 / 1500= 2.00%
  4. Investment income = yield x float0.05 x 1500= 75.00
  5. Pretax profit-30+75= 45.00
  6. Advantage of the float over the risk-free rate = rf - cost of float0.045-0.02= 2.50%

The combined ratio is 103.0%, an underwriting result of -30.00. The float costs 2.00% a year, below the risk-free rate of 4.5%, and earns 75.00, so pretax profit is 45.00.
