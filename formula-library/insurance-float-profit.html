Insurance Underwriting Profit and Float

IntermediateBanking & Financial InstitutionsAlso known as: insurance companies, cost of float, combined ratio and investment income, Berkshire float
← Insider Trading: Profit Gained and Maximum Civil PenaltyInsurtech: Underwriting Result after Automation →

Finds: whether an insurer's float is cheap.

When to use it: Use it to see how insurers make money. Premiums arrive before claims are paid, so the insurer invests the float. An underwriting loss (combined ratio above 100%) can still be profitable if the float is cheap, that is, the cost of float is below what the investments earn or the risk-free rate. A long tail of liabilities, as in liability insurance, makes the float larger and longer.

FormulaExampleCalculator

The formula

UW\displaystyle UW=P−Claims−Expenses\displaystyle =P-Claims-ExpensesCost of float\displaystyle \text{Cost of float}=−UWFloat\displaystyle =\frac{-UW}{\text{Float}}Pretax profit\displaystyle \text{Pretax profit}=UW+y×Float\displaystyle =UW+y\times\text{Float}

In words: Underwriting profit = premiums − claims − expenses; Cost of float = underwriting loss / float (negative if underwriting is profitable); Pretax profit = underwriting profit + investment yield x float

Worked example

Premiums earned are 1,000, claims 700 and expenses 330. The float is 1,500 and earns 5%. The risk-free rate is 4.5%.

Combined ratio(700 + 330) ÷ 1,000103%
Underwriting result1,000 − 700 − 330−30
Cost of float−−30 ÷ 1,5002%
Investment income0.05 × 1,50075
Pretax profit−30 + 7545
Advantage of the float over the risk-free rate0.045 − 0.022.50%
The answer

The combined ratio is 103.0%, an underwriting result of −30. The float costs 2% a year, below the risk-free rate of 4.5%, and earns 75, so pretax profit is 45.
