Carbon Markets: Abate or Buy Permits

IntermediateRegulation, Ethics & ESGAlso known as: carbon markets, cap and trade, marginal abatement cost, emissions trading, carbon price
← Capital Market Line (CML)Carhart Four-Factor Model Return →

Finds: the cheapest way to meet an emissions limit.

When to use it: Use it to see why a carbon price makes cuts happen where they are cheapest. A firm facing a permit price compares each abatement option with the price of simply buying permits. Trading lets the total cut be done at the lowest overall cost. This sketch takes the price as given and measures as separate, which real markets do not.

The formula

Abate if MACj<P\displaystyle \text{Abate if } MAC_{j}<PC\displaystyle C=∑abatedqjMACj+P×(Need−∑qj)\displaystyle =\sum_{abated}q_{j}MAC_{j}+P\times(\text{Need}-\sum q_{j})

In words: Shortfall = emissions − permits held; Abate each metric ton whose marginal abatement cost (MAC) is below the permit price, cheapest first, until the shortfall is closed; Cost of abatement = sum of (metric tons x MAC) over measures used; Permits to buy = shortfall not abated; Total cost = abatement cost + permits bought x price; Compare with buying permits for the whole shortfall

What each symbol means

MAC_jCost of cutting one more metric ton with measure jmoney per metric ton
PPrice of a permit (allowance) per metric tonmoney per metric ton
q_jMetric tons the measure can removemetric tons

Worked example

A firm emits 500,000 metric tons and holds permits for 400,000. Permits cost 85 a metric ton. It can abate 20,000 metric tons at 30, 30,000 at 55, 25,000 at 80, 15,000 at 110 and 10,000 at 150.

The answer

The shortfall is 100,000 metric tons. The firm uses the measures that cost less than the price and cuts 75,000 metric tons for 4,250,000, then buys permits for the remaining 25,000 metric tons at 2,125,000. The total is 6,375,000, against 8,500,000 if it bought permits for all 100,000 metric tons, a saving of 2,125,000.
