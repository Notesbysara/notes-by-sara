Reserve Requirement Wedge and Offshore Deposit Rates

IntermediateInternational FinanceAlso known as: eurodollar and offshore markets, euro market, reserve requirement tax, deposit insurance cost
← Reserve Adequacy MetricsResidual Income Valuation →

Finds: the cost advantage of an offshore bank.

When to use it: Use it to see why offshore markets such as the eurodollar market exist. Banks outside the home regulator's reach avoid reserve requirements and insurance charges, so they can pay depositors a bit more and charge borrowers a bit less than domestic banks. The gap is the reason depositors and borrowers use them. The wedge shrinks when reserve requirements are low or zero, as they are in many countries now.

The formula

cdom\displaystyle c_{dom}=i+f1−ρ\displaystyle =\frac{i+f}{1-\rho}coff\displaystyle c_{off}=i\displaystyle =iWedge\displaystyle \text{Wedge}=cdom−coff\displaystyle =c_{dom}-c_{off}

In words: Domestic bank's effective cost per dollar it can lend = (deposit rate + insurance fee) / (1 − reserve requirement); Offshore bank's cost = deposit rate (no reserve requirement or insurance); Wedge = domestic cost − offshore cost

What each symbol means

iInterest rate paid on depositspercent
rhoReserve requirement: share of deposits that cannot be lent and earns no interestpercent
fDeposit insurance premium and other regulatory chargespercent

Worked example

A domestic bank pays 4.5% on dollar deposits, must hold 10% in reserves and pays a 0.15% insurance charge.

Domestic effective cost(0.045 + 0.0015) ÷ (1 − 0.1)5.167%
Offshore cost0.0454.500%
Wedge0.0516667 − 0.0450.667%
Rate the offshore bank can add for depositors0.0516667 − 0.0450.667%
The answer

Each dollar the domestic bank can actually lend costs it 5.167%. An offshore bank with no reserve requirement or insurance costs the same 4.5% for a dollar it can lend. It can therefore offer depositors up to 5.167%, 0.667% more than the domestic deposit rate, and still break even.
