Martingale Check for Discounted Prices

IntermediateQuantitative FinanceAlso known as: martingale, fair game, discounted price process, no arbitrage test
← Market-Cap-Weighted IndexMaximum Drawdown →

Finds: whether discounted prices are fair games.

When to use it: Use it to test a price model. A martingale is a fair game: its expected future value equals its current value. Discounted asset prices are martingales under the risk-neutral measure, which is the reason risk-neutral pricing works. Under real-world probabilities the discounted price is usually not a martingale, because investors earn a risk premium.

FormulaExampleCalculator

The formula

EQ[e−rTST]\displaystyle E^{Q}\left[e^{-rT}S_{T}\right]=S0\displaystyle =S_{0}

In words: Under risk-neutral probabilities the expected discounted future price equals today's price: e^(-rT) x [q x Su + (1 − q) x Sd] = S0

What each symbol means

E^QExpected value under risk-neutral probabilitiesnumber
S_u, S_dUp and down pricesmoney
qRisk-neutral probabilitypercent
pReal-world probabilitypercent

Worked example

A stock at 100 goes to 120 or 90 in a year. The risk-free rate is 5% and the real-world probability of an up move is 60%. Test the discounted price under both sets of probabilities.

Risk-neutral probability of up(100 × exp(0.05 × 1) − sd) ÷ (120 − sd)50.42%
Expected discounted price, risk-neutralexp(−0.05 × 1) × (0.504237 × 120 + (1 − 0.504237) × sd)100
Expected discounted price, real-worldexp(−0.05 × 1) × (0.6 × 120 + (1 − 0.6) × sd)102.73
Real-world expected return (continuous)ln((0.6 × 120 + (1 − 0.6) × sd) ÷ 100) ÷ 17.70%
Risk premium over the risk-free rate0.076961 − 0.052.70%
The answer

Under the risk-neutral probability of 50.42%, the expected discounted price is 100, equal to today's 100, so it is a martingale. Under the real-world probability it is 102.73, above 100, because the stock is expected to earn more than the risk-free rate. That excess is the risk premium: 2.70% per year.
