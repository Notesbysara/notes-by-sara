Martingale Check for Discounted Prices

Intermediate

Also known as: martingale, fair game, discounted price process, no arbitrage test

Whether discounted prices are fair games. Use it to test a price model. A martingale is a fair game: its expected future value equals its current value. Discounted asset prices are martingales under the risk-neutral measure, which is the reason risk-neutral pricing works. Under real-world probabilities the discounted price is usually not a martingale, because investors earn a risk premium.

The formula

EQ[e−rTST]=S0E^{Q}\left[e^{-rT}S_{T}\right]=S_{0}

Under risk-neutral probabilities the expected discounted future price equals today's price: e^(-rT) x [q x Su + (1 - q) x Sd] = S0

What each symbol means

SymbolMeaningUnit
E^QExpected value under risk-neutral probabilitiesnumber
S_u, S_dUp and down pricesmoney
qRisk-neutral probabilitypercent
pReal-world probabilitypercent

Worked example

A stock at 100 goes to 120 or 90 in a year. The risk-free rate is 5% and the real-world probability of an up move is 60%. Test the discounted price under both sets of probabilities.

  1. Risk-neutral probability of up(100 x exp(0.05 x 1)-sd) / (120-sd)= 50.42%
  2. Expected discounted price, risk-neutralexp(-0.05 x 1) x (0.504237 x 120+(1-0.504237) x sd)= 100.00
  3. Expected discounted price, real-worldexp(-0.05 x 1) x (0.6 x 120+(1-0.6) x sd)= 102.73
  4. Real-world expected return (continuous)ln((0.6 x 120+(1-0.6) x sd) / 100) / 1= 7.70%
  5. Risk premium over the risk-free rate0.076961-0.05= 2.70%

Under the risk-neutral probability of 50.42%, the expected discounted price is 100.00, equal to today's 100, so it is a martingale. Under the real-world probability it is 102.73, above 100, because the stock is expected to earn more than the risk-free rate. That excess is the risk premium: 2.70% per year.
