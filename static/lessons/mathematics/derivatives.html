Derivatives as sensitivity

A derivative answers one question: if this moves a little, how much does that move? In finance, that question has many names, from marginal cost to duration to delta.

Calculus · Lesson 2 of 6 · 6 minutesBefore this: Rate of change

How much will the bond lose?

Example, with made-up numbers. You own a 10-year bond worth $100. It pays 5% a year, and market rates are 5%. Rates rise by 0.25 percentage points. Without working out the new price from scratch, can you say roughly how much the bond loses?

The idea

In the last lesson, you found the rate of change of a curve at one point. Do that at every point and you get a new function, the derivative. It tells you how sensitive the output is to the input, right there.

f′(x)  or  dy/dx

Both are read "the derivative of y with respect to x". The units are output per input: dollars per year, dollars per unit, dollars per percentage point.

Its everyday use is a quick estimate. For a small change in the input, the output changes by about the derivative times that change:

Change in y ≈ f′(x) × change in x

The bond's price (green) and its derivative used as a straight-line estimate (gold). Close to 5% they almost match. The further rates move, the more the estimate drifts.

Quick check

At today's price, a shop's profit has a derivative of +$2,000 per $1 rise in price. If it raises the price by $0.50, roughly how does profit change?

Worked example: the bond

For this bond, the derivative of price with respect to the rate is about −$7.72 per percentage point. The minus sign means the price falls when rates rise.

1. Sensitivity at 5%−$7.72 per point
2. Change in rates+0.25 points
3. Multiply−7.72 × 0.25
Estimated change in price−$1.93

Working out the new price in full gives a fall of $1.91, so the estimate is off by just 2 cents. Bond traders call this sensitivity duration. The small gap comes from the curve bending, which is the subject of a later lesson.

One idea, many names

Much of finance is derivatives with their own names:

Marginal costHow much total cost rises for one more unit made.
DurationHow much a bond's price moves when rates move.
DeltaHow much an option's price moves when the share price moves.

Example, with made-up numbers. A factory's cost of making q units is 5,000 + 20q + 0.01q2. At 1,000 units, the derivative is $40 per unit. Check by brute force: making unit 1,001 adds $40.01. The next lesson shows the shortcut rules for finding a derivative like this one.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Dropping the sign. A negative derivative means the output falls when the input rises. For bonds, higher rates mean lower prices.
  • Mixing units. A sensitivity per percentage point must be multiplied by points, not by a percent or by basis points. 0.25 points is 25 basis points.
  • Using it for big changes. The estimate is a straight line, and real curves bend. For a 2-point rise, it says −$15.44 when the true fall is $14.05.

Remember

  • The derivative is the rate of change at each point: how sensitive the output is to the input.
  • Change in output ≈ derivative × change in input, for small changes.
  • Marginal cost, duration and delta are all derivatives.

Check yourself

Three questions. Try them without scrolling up.

1. f′(10) = 4. What does that tell you?

2. A bond's sensitivity is −$7.72 per percentage point. Rates fall by 0.5 points. Roughly how does its price change?

3. When is "change in y ≈ derivative × change in x" least accurate?
