Your personal financial statements

The two statements that give a full picture of your finances: a net worth statement, which shows what you own and owe on one day, and a cash flow statement, which shows what came in and went out over a month. How they connect, and the few ratios worth checking.

Everyday money · Lesson 7 of 7 · 8 minutesBefore this: Personal taxes

In plain words

Add up everything you own, subtract everything you owe, and what is left is your net worth. It is a snapshot of one day. Separately, add up the money that came in over a month and the money that went out; the difference is what you saved. Saving is what makes net worth grow, so the two belong together.

The typical American family and the average one look very different:

Age matters even more. Families headed by someone 35 or younger had a median net worth of $33,000 in 2025, while those headed by someone 75 or older had about $505,000. Net worth is mostly built over decades, so the useful comparison is with your own figure a year ago, not with someone else's.

Source: Associated Press.

A net worth statement is a personal balance sheet. It lists your assets, everything you own that has value, and your liabilities, everything you owe. The difference is your net worth. It is the same equation as a company's balance sheet from the Accounting lessons, with net worth in place of shareholders' equity:

Net worth = Assets − Liabilities

Here is one for a person in their early thirties (illustrative figures):

Assets
Cash in bank accounts$12,000
Retirement account$38,000
Investment fund$9,000
Car, at what it would sell for today$14,000
Total assets$73,000
Liabilities
Student loan$21,000
Car loan$9,500
Credit card balance$2,500
Total liabilities$33,000
Net worth$40,000

This person uses $2,500 of their cash to pay off the credit card in full. What happens to their net worth?

Value each item at what it is worth today, not what you paid. A car bought for $25,000 might sell for $14,000 a few years later, and it keeps losing value. A home's value is an estimate until it is sold; recent sale prices of similar homes nearby are the usual guide. Retirement accounts can be listed at their balance, but remember that some of them will be taxed when you withdraw. Leave out everyday belongings such as furniture and clothes unless they are genuinely valuable, because they rarely sell for much. Liabilities are simpler: use the balance you would need to pay off each debt today.

Some assets can be spent quickly and some cannot. Cash and savings are liquid: you can use them this week. A home, a car or a retirement account is illiquid: turning it into cash takes time, costs money or triggers taxes and penalties. Two people with the same net worth can be in very different positions if one has it all in a house and the other has some in the bank.

Try your own. Rough figures are fine for a first look; the trend over time matters more than precision.

What households own differs a lot between countries. In Great Britain, property and private pensions make up three quarters of household wealth. In India, a 2017 Reserve Bank of India committee found that the average household held almost all its wealth in physical assets, mainly real estate and gold, and only 5% in financial assets such as deposits, shares and pensions:

In the US, 66% of families owned their home in 2025, and the median homeowner's home equity, the home's value minus the mortgage, was $230,000, more than the median family's entire net worth. For many households everywhere, the home is the largest item on the statement, and it is also one of the least liquid.

Source: Federal Reserve press release, 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances.

A cash flow statement covers a period, usually a month. It lists money in, mostly take-home pay, and money out, and the difference is what you saved, or what you overspent. A budget is a plan for the coming month; a cash flow statement records what actually happened, so comparing the two shows where the plan slipped. The same person's month (illustrative figures):

Take-home pay$4,200
Rent−$1,400
Debt payments: car loan, student loan, card−$650
Food−$600
Transportation: fuel, insurance, upkeep−$300
Utilities and phone−$250
Everything else−$600
Saved this month$400

The two statements connect. Money saved during the month adds to your assets, so it raises net worth. So does paying down the principal of a debt: cash goes out, but what you owe falls by the same amount, and only the interest part is a true cost. Net worth also moves when the value of what you own changes, as investments rise or fall and the car loses value:

Change in net worth= money saved+ debt principal repaid+ change in asset values

Over a year, saving $400 a month adds $4,800. If debt payments also cut the loans by $5,000, investments rise $3,000 and the car loses $2,000, net worth rises by $10,800 (illustrative figures). Saving and paying down debt are the parts you control; market values are not.

A few ratios turn the statements into a health check. Each compares two numbers you already have:

Savings rateMoney saved ÷ take-home pay. Here $400 ÷ $4,200 = 9.5%. Counting retirement contributions taken from pay before it arrives gives a fuller picture.
Emergency fund in monthsCash ÷ essential monthly spending. Here $12,000 ÷ $3,200 (rent, debts, food, transportation, utilities) = about 3.8 months, as in the emergency fund lesson.
Debt payments to incomeMonthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly pay. Lenders check this before approving a loan. In the US, the share of families whose debt payments were more than 40% of income rose from 6.5% in 2022 to 8.6% in 2025.
Debt to assetsTotal liabilities ÷ total assets. Here $33,000 ÷ $73,000 = 45%. It usually falls with age as loans are repaid and assets grow.

Source: Federal Reserve press release, 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances.

A negative net worth is common early in life and not a verdict. A graduate with a large student loan and few assets can start well below zero. What matters is the direction: a positive savings rate and debts shrinking on schedule mean net worth is rising. Updating the net worth statement once or twice a year and the cash flow statement monthly is enough for most people; a simple spreadsheet or an app that links to your accounts both work.

How professionals use it

Financial planners
Start every client relationship by building both statements, then set goals and track progress against them each year.
Lenders
Ask for income, assets and debts on every loan application and check ratios such as debt payments to income before approving.
Central banks and statisticians
Survey household balance sheets, as the Federal Reserve, the ONS and the RBI do, to judge how much debt and risk households carry.
Accountants
Prepare personal financial statements for clients applying for large loans, visas or court cases that require proof of assets.

Net worth shows where you stand; cash flow shows which way you are moving. Watch both, and compare with your own past.

The problems aren't labeled with a lesson, so part of the work is choosing the right idea. Work each one on paper, then open the answer. All figures are illustrative unless a country's actual rules are named.

  1. Monthly gross pay is $4,000. Income tax withheld is $360, social security and Medicare are 7.65% of gross pay, and 5% of gross pay goes into a retirement plan. What is net pay?

    Show answer
    Income and paychecks$4,000 − $360 − $306 − $200 = $3,134.

  2. Your salary rises 4% in a year when prices rise 6%. Roughly what happened to your real pay?

    Show answer
    Income and paychecksIt fell by about 2% (exactly 1.04 ÷ 1.06 − 1 = −1.9%). You can buy less than before, despite the raise.

  3. Take-home pay is $3,500 a month. How does the 50/30/20 rule split it?

    Show answer
    BudgetingUp to $1,750 for needs, up to $1,050 for wants, at least $700 for savings and paying down debt.

  4. You need $9,000 for a course that starts in 18 months, and you will keep the money in a savings account. Ignoring interest, how much should you set aside each month, and why is a savings account the right place?

    Show answer
    Saving and goals$9,000 ÷ 18 = $500 a month. Money needed within two years should be kept safe and easy to reach, not invested where it could fall in value just before you need it.

  5. Your essential spending is $2,400 a month. You are single, rent, and work in a field where job searches often take several months. What range should your emergency fund be in, and toward which end?

    Show answer
    Emergency fundThree to six months is $7,200 to $14,400. With no second income and long job searches, aim toward the upper end.

  6. In the US you have $180,000 in a savings account and $120,000 in a CD, both in your own name at the same FDIC-insured bank. How much is insured?

    Show answer
    Banking basics$250,000. The limit is per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, and both accounts are single-ownership deposits at one bank. Moving $50,000 to another insured bank would cover all $300,000.

  7. Using the UK's 2026/27 bands for England, a salary of £60,000 pays how much income tax? What are the average and marginal rates?

    Show answer
    Personal taxesTaxable income is £60,000 − £12,570 = £47,430. 20% on £37,700 is £7,540, plus 40% on £9,730 is £3,892: £11,432. Average rate £11,432 ÷ £60,000 = 19.1%; marginal rate 40%.

  8. Your marginal tax rate is 12%. Which saves more tax: a $2,000 deduction or a $500 credit?

    Show answer
    Personal taxesThe deduction saves 12% × $2,000 = $240. The credit saves $500, so it is worth more.

  9. You have $6,000 in cash, $25,000 in a retirement account and a car worth $10,000. You owe $15,000 on a student loan and $4,000 on the car. What is your net worth, and what is it after you use $3,000 of cash to pay down the student loan?

    Show answer
    Your personal financial statements$41,000 − $19,000 = $22,000, and still $22,000 after the payment, because cash and debt both fall by $3,000. Future interest costs fall, but so does the cash cushion.
