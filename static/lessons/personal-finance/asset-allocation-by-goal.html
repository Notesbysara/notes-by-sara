Asset allocation by goal

How to split money between shares, bonds and cash for each goal, based on when you need it and how much risk you can carry; what different mixes have done in the past; why spreading across countries matters; and how to keep the mix on track by rebalancing.

Investing · Lesson 3 of 5 · 9 minutesBefore this: Index funds and ETFs

In plain words

Asset allocation is how you divide your money between shares, bonds and cash. More shares means more growth over time but bigger drops along the way. Money you need soon should be mostly safe; money you will not need for decades can be mostly in shares. Each goal gets its own mix, and you nudge it back into line once in a while.

The mix decides both how much you are likely to earn and how bad a bad year can be. US shares and bonds since 1926:

60% shares, 40% bonds8.7%−26.6%

Every extra 20 percentage points in shares added roughly 0.8 to 0.9 of a point a year on average, and about 8 more points of loss in the worst year. No mix is best in general; the right one depends on what the money is for.

The three main building blocks behave differently.

SharesThe growth engine over long periods, with the deepest falls. Suited to money not needed for many years.
BondsLower returns and usually smaller falls; often, though not always, steadier when shares fall. In 2022 both fell together.
CashSavings and deposits. No fall in value, but usually little growth after inflation. For emergencies and near-term goals.

Start with when you need the money. The time horizon is the most important input, because a fall just before you need the money cannot be waited out. Many planners use rough bands like these:

Under about 3 yearsEmergency fund, a home deposit next year, a wedding. Keep it in cash or short-term deposits; shares could be down 30% when you need them.
About 3 to 10 yearsA child's university, a business, a home in several years. A mix, often weighted toward bonds, moving toward cash as the date nears.
10 years or moreRetirement, financial independence. Mostly shares, because there is time to recover from falls and inflation is the bigger risk.
No end dateMoney you may pass on. Can stay mostly in shares, set by how much fluctuation you can live with.

Then think about how much risk you can carry, and how much you can bear. Risk capacity is financial: a secure job, a solid emergency fund and other income mean a fall will not force you to sell. Risk tolerance is emotional: whether you could watch your investments drop a third without selling. The worst outcome is choosing an aggressive mix, panicking in a crash and selling near the bottom, which turns a temporary fall into a permanent loss. A slightly more cautious mix that you will hold through a bad year is better than an ideal one you abandon.

Spread across countries, not only across companies. Most investors hold mostly their own country's shares, known as home bias. But most countries are a small part of the world market. Share of the MSCI ACWI, a global index of large and medium companies, at the end of August 2026:

United States 63.6%Japan 5.1%Taiwan 3.3%United Kingdom 3.1%Canada 3.1%All other countries 21.9%

An investor in Taiwan holding only Taiwanese shares, or in India holding only Indian shares, has everything riding on a few percent of the world's companies, often concentrated in a few industries, and on the same economy that pays their salary. The Yearbook from the lesson on why invest found that investors in the vast majority of markets were better off investing globally than only at home. A global index fund, or a mix of a home fund and a world fund, spreads that risk.

Source: UBS Global Investment Returns Yearbook 2026.

Mixes drift, so rebalance. When shares rise faster than bonds, they become a bigger share of the portfolio, and your risk rises without your choosing it.

You set up a 60% shares, 40% bonds portfolio. In one year shares rise 30% and bonds are flat. What is your mix now?

Rebalancing means selling some of what has grown and buying what has lagged, to return to your target. Common approaches are once a year, or whenever an asset drifts more than about five percentage points from target. The easiest way is to direct new contributions to whatever is below target, which avoids selling and possible taxes. Rebalancing also builds in a useful habit: it makes you buy after falls and trim after rises, the opposite of what fear and excitement suggest.

Current mix

The mix should change as the goal gets closer. For a long-term goal, a common pattern is to hold mostly shares while the goal is far away and shift gradually toward bonds and cash in the last 10 to 15 years. Target-date funds, also called lifecycle funds, do this automatically: you pick the fund dated near your retirement year, and it moves from mostly shares to a more cautious mix over time. They are offered in many countries and used as the default in many US and UK workplace pension plans; check each fund's mix and fees, as they vary by provider.

Put it together goal by goal. An example for one person, with illustrative figures: an emergency fund of six months' expenses in a savings account; money for a home deposit in three years in short-term deposits; and retirement savings, 30 years away, in 80% global shares and 20% bonds, rebalanced each year and made more cautious from their fifties. Each pot is simple, and each matches when the money is needed.

How professionals use it

Financial planners
Assess each client's goals, time horizons, risk capacity and tolerance, then set and review a target mix for each goal.
Portfolio managers
Run balanced and target-date funds, rebalancing them to target and following a set glide path as dates approach.
Pension funds
Set strategic asset allocations for members' savings, the decision that drives most of a fund's long-run results.
Investment consultants
Model how different mixes might perform in good and bad markets to help institutions choose their allocation.

Match each goal's mix to when you need the money, spread it across the world, and rebalance once in a while.
