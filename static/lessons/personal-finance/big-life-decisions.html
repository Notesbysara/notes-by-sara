Big life decisions

A way to think through the money side of large, hard-to-reverse choices: buying or renting a home, combining finances with a partner, having children, changing jobs or countries, and taking a career break. With examples from the US, the UK, India and Taiwan.

Life goals · Lesson 2 of 3 · 11 minutesBefore this: Funding education

In plain words

Big decisions shape your finances for years. Compare the full cost of each option over time, not just the monthly payment. Ask how easy it would be to undo, keep a cash buffer for surprises, and make sure the choice fits what you actually want from life. Money should inform these choices, not make them for you.

Use the same four questions for any big decision.

1What does each option cost over time?Add up the full cost over the years it lasts, including what you give up, such as returns on money tied up or income not earned.
2How reversible is it?Renting can be undone in months; buying a home or having a child cannot. Harder-to-reverse choices deserve more care and a larger safety margin.
3Can you absorb a bad outcome?Check that your emergency fund, insurance and income could cope if costs run higher or income falls.
4Does it fit your life?Security, freedom, family and location all have value that numbers cannot fully capture. Write down what matters before comparing figures.

Buying versus renting is about unrecoverable costs, not "rent is wasted money". Rent buys a place to live. Owning has its own costs that you never get back: mortgage interest, property taxes, maintenance, insurance, buying and selling fees, and the return your deposit could have earned elsewhere. Only the part of the payment that repays the loan builds wealth. Compare rent with these costs, not with the whole mortgage payment.

Housing costs relative to income vary hugely. In Taiwan, the national house price-to-income ratio was 9.47 in early 2026, and mortgage payments took 41.46% of household income, in the ministry's "low affordability" range. Taipei's ratio was 15.41 in 2025. Where prices are that high compared with rents, renting and investing the difference can be the cheaper path, while ownership offers security against rent increases and eviction. In India, families often pool money across generations to buy, and in the UK many first-time buyers rely on help from parents.

Combining finances with a partner works best with an early, honest conversation. Share incomes, debts, credit histories and money habits before you commit. Couples commonly choose one of three setups:

Fully jointAll income goes into shared accounts. Simple and transparent, but needs close agreement on spending.
Yours, mine and oursA joint account for shared bills, often funded in proportion to income, plus personal accounts for each.
Fully separateEach pays agreed shares of costs. Independent, but needs care if one partner earns much less or stops work.

Agree how to protect the partner who earns less, especially if one cuts back work for children. Check how your country treats shared property and pensions on separation or death, and update beneficiaries and wills.

Children change both spending and earnings. Direct costs include childcare, food, housing space and education. The larger cost is often reduced earnings and pension contributions when a parent works less, which in most countries falls mainly on mothers. Before a child arrives, review the budget, build the emergency fund, check parental pay and benefits, and update life insurance and wills.

Judge a job offer on total pay, not salary alone.

Pay and bonusHow much of the bonus is guaranteed, and how shares or options vest.
Retirement contributionsAn employer match or pension contribution can be worth several percent of salary.
Insurance and leaveHealth, life and income protection cover, and paid leave, can be worth thousands a year.
Living costs and taxesA higher salary in a more expensive city or country can leave you worse off after rent and tax.

Moving abroad adds currency, tax and pension questions. Check whether your pension and social security contributions carry across, how both countries tax your income and assets, and whether you will need to send money home to family. Keep some savings in the currency of your likely future expenses.

Plan a career break or business start like a project. Estimate how long it might last and what you would live on. Build a larger cash buffer than usual, often six to twelve months of spending, keep health cover in place, and decide in advance at what point you would return to paid work.

How professionals use it

Financial planners
Model a client's choices side by side, such as buying now or in five years, and show the effect on every other goal.
Mortgage advisers
Assess affordability under higher rates and different deposit sizes before a client commits.
Family lawyers
Advise couples on how property, debts and pensions are treated on marriage, separation and death.
Compensation analysts
Value full pay packages, including equity, retirement contributions and benefits, when setting or comparing offers.

Compare full costs over time, respect how hard a choice is to undo, keep a buffer, and let the numbers serve what you value.
