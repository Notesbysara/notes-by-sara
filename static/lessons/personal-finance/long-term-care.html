Long-term care

What long-term care is, why health insurance usually does not pay for it, what it costs, who pays in the US, the UK, Taiwan and India, and the ways families plan for it: savings, home equity, insurance and public programs.

Protection · Lesson 3 of 3 · 10 minutesBefore this: How much insurance you need

In plain words

Long-term care is help with everyday tasks like washing, dressing and eating when age, illness or dementia make them hard. It can last years and cost a lot, and in many countries ordinary health insurance does not pay for it. Planning means knowing what your public system covers, and deciding how the rest would be paid: savings, your home, insurance or family.

Most people will need some care, and the most expensive kind is very expensive:

~70%chance that an American turning 65 will need some long-term care in their remaining years
vs
$129,575a year: the US median cost of a private nursing home room in 2025

Long-term care is mostly personal help, not medical treatment. It usually starts when someone can no longer manage some "activities of daily living" alone: bathing, dressing, eating, using the toilet, moving from bed to chair, and continence. Dementia, stroke, frailty and chronic illness are common causes. Because it is not treatment to cure an illness, health insurance usually excludes it. In the US, for example, Medicare does not cover long-term custodial care when that is the only care someone needs.

Care happens in many places, and costs rise with the level of support. US national medians from 2025 show the range:

Family at homeUnpaid, but often costly in lost earnings, pension savings and the carer's own health.
Paid help at home$35 an hour for a non-medical caregiver, about $80,080 a year at 44 hours a week.
Assisted living$6,200 a month, about $74,400 a year, for housing, meals and personal help.
Nursing home$10,798 a month for a private room, with round-the-clock nursing.

Source: CareScout. Costs vary widely by region and country.

The risk is very uneven, which is what makes it hard to plan for. According to US government figures, one-third of today's 65-year-olds may never need long-term care, but 20 percent will need it for longer than five years. Women need care longer on average (3.7 years) than men (2.2 years), partly because they live longer. Most people will need some help for a while, which savings can handle. A minority will need years of expensive care, which savings often cannot. That long tail is the part worth planning hardest for.

Source: US Administration for Community Living.

Who pays depends heavily on where you live:

United StatesMedicare pays only for short stays of skilled care, such as rehabilitation after a hospital stay. Medicaid pays for long-term care, but only once a person's income and savings are low, often after spending down their assets. Everyone else pays from savings, home equity or private insurance.
United KingdomCare is means-tested by local councils. In England, someone with assets above £23,250 pays the full cost of a care home; below £14,250, capital is ignored. These limits have been frozen since 2010, and a planned lifetime cap on care costs was scrapped. Scotland provides free personal care, and limits differ in Wales and Northern Ireland.
TaiwanTaiwan became a super-aged society in 2025, with people 65 and over above 20% of the population. The tax-funded Long-term Care 3.0 plan began in January 2026, expanding public services. Many families also hire live-in caregivers and pay for some care themselves.
IndiaFamilies provide most care at home, and there is almost no formal long-term care system. The share of people aged 60 and over is projected to rise from 10.1% in 2021 to 20.8% by 2050, while smaller households and migration leave fewer family carers available.

Sources: US HHS ASPE; GOV.UK; Which?; Taiwan Ministry of Health and Welfare; Taiwan National Immigration Agency; ETV Bharat, on UNFPA India Ageing Report 2023; Outlook India, on Transform Rural India.

Some countries insure everyone through a dedicated scheme. Japan, Germany and South Korea run public long-term care insurance funded by compulsory contributions; in Japan, everyone aged 40 and over pays in. These schemes pay for assessed care needs, though people still pay part of the cost themselves.

There are four main ways to pay for the part your public system does not.

Savings and incomePensions and investments pay for care as it comes. This works well for shorter needs and is the reason retirement plans often include a buffer for later-life care.
Home equitySelling or borrowing against a home can fund care, especially a move into a care home. This often means less to pass on to heirs.
Long-term care insurancePays a daily or monthly amount once you need help with a set number of daily activities. Mainly available in the US and some Asian markets; in the UK, buying an annuity at the point of needing care is more common.
Family careCommon everywhere and essential in many places, but it has a real cost, often borne by daughters and wives, in lost work, income and pension.

Read long-term care insurance closely. The details decide how much it really pays:

Benefit and limitThe daily or monthly amount and how long it pays, often two to five years. Compare it with local care costs.
Waiting periodOften 30 to 90 days of care you pay for yourself before benefits start.
Inflation protection and premium riskWithout inflation protection, a fixed benefit buys less each year. In the US, many older policies have had large premium increases, so check whether the insurer can raise rates.

Practical steps before care is needed. Most of these cost little and save a great deal of stress later:

Know your local rulesWhat public programs cover, the means-test limits, and how your home is treated.
Talk as a familyWho would provide care, where the person would like to live, and how costs would be shared.
Set up powers of attorneyLegal authority for someone you trust to manage your money and care decisions if you cannot. Covered in estate planning.
Keep a care bufferTreat later-life care as a likely cost in retirement planning rather than a remote risk.

How professionals use it

Financial planners
Build care costs into retirement plans and decide with clients whether to self-fund, insure, or rely on home equity.
Care fees advisers
In the UK, specialist advisers help families fund care home fees, including immediate-needs annuities and deferred payment agreements.
Elder law attorneys
Advise on powers of attorney, means tests and rules on giving away assets before care is needed.
Policy analysts
Model the cost of ageing populations and design public schemes, such as Japan's and Taiwan's.

Know what your public system pays, plan how you would cover a long stretch of care, and put powers of attorney in place before they are needed.

The problems aren't labeled with a lesson, so part of the work is choosing the right idea. Work each one on paper, then open the answer. All figures are illustrative unless a country's actual rules are named.

  1. Policy A costs $1,000 a year with a $250 deductible. Policy B costs $760 with a $1,500 deductible. You expect a claim in about 15% of years. Which has the lower expected yearly cost, and what is each one's worst year with one claim?

    Show answer
    Insurance for householdsA: 1,000 + 0.15 × 250 = $1,037.50. B: 760 + 0.15 × 1,500 = $985, about $52 a year cheaper. Worst years: A $1,250, B $2,260. B is the better choice only if you could pay $1,500 from savings comfortably.

  2. Sort these into "insure" or "usually self-insure": a cracked phone screen, your home burning down, being sued after injuring a cyclist, a broken toaster under extended warranty.

    Show answer
    Insurance for householdsInsure: the house fire and the lawsuit, both of which could be ruinous. Self-insure: the phone screen and the toaster, which an emergency fund covers more cheaply than insurance premiums.

  3. In India, an individual health policy had a base premium of ₹25,000. What did you pay with GST before 22 September 2025, and after, if the base premium stays the same? Would a group policy through your employer change?

    Show answer
    Insurance for householdsBefore: 25,000 × 1.18 = ₹29,500. After: ₹25,000, since individual policies are exempt. Group policies still carry 18% GST.

  4. A parent's family would need 30,000 a year for 12 years, has a mortgage of 180,000, and wants 40,000 for education. They have 60,000 in savings and employer life cover of twice their 50,000 salary. How much extra life cover would the needs method suggest?

    Show answer
    How much insurance you needNeed: 360,000 + 180,000 + 40,000 = 580,000. Existing: 60,000 + 100,000 = 160,000. Extra cover: 420,000, ideally on a term that ends when the children are independent.

  5. A house would cost 400,000 to rebuild but is insured for only 300,000, under a policy with an "average" rule. A storm causes 20,000 of damage. What does the insurer pay?

    Show answer
    How much insurance you needThe home is insured for 75% of its rebuild cost, so the insurer pays 75% of the claim: 15,000. Being underinsured cuts even small claims.

  6. In the UK in 2026/27, what is the weekly Statutory Sick Pay for someone earning £400 a week, and for someone earning £120 a week? What is the most anyone receives over the full 28 weeks?

    Show answer
    How much insurance you need£400 × 80% = £320, above the flat rate, so £123.25. £120 × 80% = £96. Maximum over 28 weeks: 123.25 × 28 = £3,451.

  7. Your employer pays full salary for 3 months of sickness, and your emergency fund covers another 3 months of essential spending. Which waiting period suits an income protection policy: 4 weeks, 26 weeks or 2 years? Why?

    Show answer
    How much insurance you needAbout 26 weeks. Sick pay and savings cover the first six months. A 4-week wait would pay for cover you already have, at a higher premium; a 2-year wait would leave a gap of a year and a half.

  8. In the US, assisted living costs $6,200 a month. A retiree expects 3 years of care and has $3,000 a month of pension and Social Security income to put toward it. How much must come from savings or home equity?

    Show answer
    Long-term careCost: 6,200 × 36 = $223,200. Income: 3,000 × 36 = $108,000. From savings: $115,200, before allowing for costs rising.

  9. In England, a care home resident has £60,000 in savings and no home. Roughly how much of their own savings will they spend before the council starts to contribute?

    Show answer
    Long-term careThey pay in full until their assets fall to the upper limit of £23,250: about £36,750. Between £23,250 and £14,250 they share the cost; below £14,250 their capital is ignored, though their income still counts.
