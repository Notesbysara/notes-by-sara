Insurance for households

How insurance works, which risks are worth insuring and which you can cover yourself, the main types a household needs, how deductibles and exclusions change what you pay and get, and how health cover works in the US, the UK, India and Taiwan.

Protection · Lesson 1 of 3 · 9 minutesBefore this: Retirement income

In plain words

Insurance lets many people each pay a small, certain amount so that the few who suffer a big loss get paid. It is worth buying for losses you could not afford, like a serious illness, a house fire or the death of a breadwinner. Small losses you can cover from savings are usually cheaper to pay yourself.

How households get covered differs hugely between countries, and so do the gaps:

26.7 millionpeople in the US, 7.9% of the population, had no health insurance for all of 2025
vs
3.7%of GDP spent on insurance premiums in India, about half the OECD average, despite a fast-growing market

Sources: US Census Bureau; Vision IAS, on Swiss Re data; OECD.

Insurance pools risk. No one knows who will have the fire, the accident or the illness this year, but an insurer covering thousands of households can predict roughly how many will. Each pays a premium; the insurer pays the claims, its costs and a profit from the pool. On average, policyholders get back less than they pay, which is the price of turning an uncertain, possibly ruinous loss into a small, certain cost.

So insure the big risks and keep the small ones. A useful test is the size of the possible loss compared with your savings:

Insure: would wreck your financesSerious illness or injury, losing your income, a breadwinner's death, your home burning down, being sued after an accident.
Usually self-insure: affordableA broken phone, a minor car scrape, a small repair. An emergency fund covers these more cheaply than insurance.
Be skeptical: low valueExtended warranties, phone and gadget cover, and add-ons sold at checkout often pay out little relative to their price.
Required anywaySome cover is compulsory, such as third-party car insurance in many countries, or required by a lender, such as buildings insurance on a mortgaged home.

The main types a household should know:

HealthPays medical costs. How much you need depends entirely on your country's public system.
Income protectionCalled disability insurance in the US. Replaces part of your pay if illness or injury stops you working. Often overlooked, though for many people their future earnings are their biggest asset.
LifePays a lump sum if you die, so dependents can cope. Covered in detail in the next lesson.
Property and liabilityHome or renters' insurance for your belongings and building, car insurance, and liability cover if you injure someone or damage their property.

Health cover depends most on where you live:

United StatesMost people are covered through an employer: 53.5% of the population in 2025, followed by Medicare for older people and Medicaid for lower incomes. Others buy plans directly, often through the government marketplace. Medical bills are a major source of financial hardship for the uninsured and underinsured.
United KingdomThe NHS provides care free at the point of use, funded by taxes. Private medical insurance is optional, mainly for faster treatment, and often offered by employers.
IndiaAyushman Bharat covers lower-income families. Many others rely on employer group cover or individual health policies; since 22 September 2025, individual health and life insurance premiums are free of GST, previously 18%.
TaiwanNational Health Insurance covers almost everyone, funded mainly by payroll-based premiums at a rate of 5.17%, unchanged since 2021. Many people add private cover for costs the public system does not pay: life insurers' health premium income reached NT$457.5 billion in 2024.

Sources: US Census Bureau; India Department of Financial Services; Care Health Insurance; Commonwealth Fund; Taiwan National Health Insurance Administration; Insurance Business, on Taiwan Insurance Institute data.

Read three parts of any policy before you buy.

The deductible or excessThe part of each claim you pay yourself. A higher one lowers the premium.
The limitsThe most the policy pays, per claim, per year or in total. Too low a limit can leave you exposed on exactly the big loss you insured against.
The exclusionsWhat is not covered, such as floods on some home policies or pre-existing conditions on some health plans.

Your car insurer offers a $500 deductible, or a $2,000 deductible for $300 a year less. You claim about once every 10 years and have $10,000 in an emergency fund. Which usually costs less over time? (illustrative figures)

Assumes at most one claim a year, each larger than both deductibles. Choose the higher deductible only if you could pay it from savings without hardship.

Be careful with policies that mix insurance and saving. Whole-life, endowment and investment-linked policies combine cover with a savings or investment element. They are sold widely in many countries, including Taiwan and India, often on commission. They tend to cost far more than pure protection, can lose much of their value if cancelled early, and their returns are often lower than a low-cost fund. A common approach is to buy cheap protection, such as term life cover, and invest separately. If you already have such a policy, check its surrender value and terms before cancelling.

Good habits when buying and claiming:

Disclose honestlyAnswer health and history questions fully. Mistakes or omissions can let an insurer refuse a claim later.
Check what you already haveEmployers, credit cards, bank accounts and public schemes often include some cover. Avoid paying twice.
Shop around at renewalPrices for the same cover vary a lot between insurers, and loyal customers are not always offered the best rate.
Review after life changesMarriage, children, a home purchase or a new job change what you need.

How professionals use it

Financial planners
Review a household's risks and existing cover, close dangerous gaps, and cut overlapping or low-value policies.
Actuaries
Estimate how often and how large losses will be, and set premiums so the pool can pay all claims.
Underwriters
Assess each applicant's risk and decide whether to offer cover, at what price and with what exclusions.
Insurance regulators
Check that insurers can pay claims, require clear disclosures, and police mis-selling of complex policies.

Insure what would ruin you, self-insure what would only annoy you, and read the deductible, limits and exclusions before you sign.
