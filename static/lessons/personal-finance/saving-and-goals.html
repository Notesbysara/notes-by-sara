Saving and goals

Why saving works better as a system than as willpower, how to turn wishes into goals with a number and a date, where to keep money depending on when you need it, and how to work out what to save each month.

Everyday money · Lesson 3 of 7 · 8 minutesBefore this: Budgeting

In plain words

Saving means keeping part of your income for later instead of spending it now. It works best when it happens automatically, before you see the money, and when it is aimed at something specific. Money for soon should be kept safe; money for many years from now can be invested.

Two famous experiments changed how employers in many countries help people save:

37% → 86%Share of new employees saving in a US company's retirement plan after it switched from opting in to automatic enrollment, with the right to opt out
and
3.5% → 13.6%Average saving rate of workers who agreed in advance to save part of each future pay raise, after just under four years

Sources: Center for Retirement Research at Boston College, on Madrian and Shea (2001); Chicago Booth Review, on Thaler and Benartzi's Save More Tomorrow.

Nothing about the plans' money changed in either case, only the default. People save far more when saving happens without a decision each month. That finding helped shape laws in the US that encourage automatic enrollment, and similar automatic schemes in other countries.

Pay yourself first. Instead of saving whatever is left at the end of the month, which is often nothing, move a fixed amount to savings on the day you are paid, before you can spend it. Set it up once as an automatic transfer and it keeps working. Two refinements copy the experiments above: start with an amount small enough not to hurt, and increase it each time your pay rises, so your take-home pay never actually falls.

A goal needs a number and a date. "Save more" is a wish; "$12,000 for a home deposit in four years" is a goal. A number and a date tell you how much to save each month, show whether you are on track, and make the goal harder to raid for something else. Most people have several goals at once, and it helps to keep each in its own account or labeled pot so progress is visible.

When you need the money decides where to keep it:

Within two yearsA holiday, a course, a car repair. Keep it in cash: a savings account or a short time deposit. The money must be there in full when you need it, so safety matters more than return.
Two to five yearsA home deposit, a wedding, further study. Mostly safe options such as time deposits or short-term bonds, which pay a little more for tying money up.
Five years or moreRetirement, a child's education in the distant future. Time to ride out ups and downs, so investing in shares, covered in the investing section, becomes sensible.

The reason is risk. Share markets can lose more than a third of their value in a year, as the US market did in 2008, and take years to recover. That is acceptable for money you will not need for decades and a real danger for money you need next year.

How much to save each month. If your savings earn interest, you need to put in a little less than the goal divided by the number of months, because interest does some of the work. The amount is the annuity payment from the Foundations lesson on annuities:

Monthly saving = Goal × r(1 + r)n − 1

r is the monthly interest rate (the yearly rate ÷ 12) and n is the number of months.

You want $10,000 in three years. Your savings account pays 4% a year, added monthly. About how much do you need to save each month?

Save a month

Interest helps more the longer the goal. Over a year or two it makes little difference, so the size of the monthly amount and starting on time matter most. Over decades, compounding does most of the work, which is the subject of the lesson on why to invest.

Countries save very different shares of their income, and the figures are harder to compare than they look. In OECD data for 2024, households in Sweden saved 16.0% of their disposable income, in France 12.8% and in Germany 11.2%, while households in New Zealand spent more than they earned, with a saving rate of −1.3%. Definitions matter too: in 2025 the US household saving rate was 4.6% by the US's own method but 10.7% under the harmonized method used by the OECD and Eurostat, which counts some items differently.

Sources: Business Today, on OECD data; Banque de France.

Your own saving rate is easier to measure: what you saved this year divided by your take-home pay. Tracking it once a year tells you more about your progress than any single balance.

How professionals use it

Financial planners
List a client's goals with amounts and dates, match each to a time horizon, then work out the monthly saving each one needs.
Employers and pension designers
Use automatic enrollment and automatic increases, because defaults change behavior more than information does.
Banks
Offer separate savings pots, round-ups and scheduled transfers to make saving automatic and visible.
Economists
Watch household saving rates as a sign of confidence: saving tends to rise when people are worried about the future.

Make saving automatic, give every goal a number and a date, and match where you keep money to when you need it.
