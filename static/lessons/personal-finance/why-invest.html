Why invest

Why money for long-term goals usually belongs in investments rather than a bank account, what more than a century of returns shows, why starting early matters so much, the risks you take on, and when you are ready to begin.

Investing · Lesson 1 of 5 · 8 minutesBefore this: Paying off debt

In plain words

Cash in the bank is safe, but over many years prices usually rise about as fast as its interest, or faster. Investing means owning things that grow, such as shares in companies, so your money can grow faster than prices. The trade-off is that investments can fall sharply in the short run, so they suit money you will not need for years.

What $1 invested in the US at the start of 1900 had become by the end of 2025, before inflation:

$124,854in shares (equities), with dividends reinvested
$284in long-term government bonds
$69in Treasury bills, the closest thing to cash

Prices rose about 38-fold over the same period, so $1 of 1900 buys what about $38 buys today. After inflation, shares grew roughly 3,300-fold, while bills barely stayed ahead.

Source: UBS Global Investment Returns Yearbook 2026; UBS Chief Investment Office.

Saving keeps money safe; investing makes it grow. Saving, as in the earlier lessons, means keeping money in deposits you can reach at any time, for emergencies and near-term goals. Investing means buying assets expected to earn more over time: shares, which give you part-ownership of companies and their profits; bonds, which are loans to governments or companies; property; or funds that hold many of these at once, as the Foundations lesson on financial assets described. The extra return is payment for accepting risk, the trade-off covered in the Foundations lesson on risk and return.

The pattern holds beyond the US. The same research, by Dimson, Marsh and Staunton, covers 35 markets since 1900. In all 21 countries with continuous histories, shares were the best-performing of the main asset classes. In real terms, after inflation, US shares returned about 6.6% a year from 1900 to 2025, against 1.6% for bonds.

Sources: Funds Society, on the Yearbook; WealthBriefing.

Inflation is the hidden cost of holding cash. Money that earns 2% while prices rise 3% loses about 1% of its buying power every year, even though the balance keeps rising. Over a few months that hardly matters. Over 30 years of saving for retirement, it decides whether your money keeps up with the cost of living. The Economics lesson on inflation explains why prices rise.

Time does most of the work, through compounding. Returns earn further returns, so growth speeds up the longer money stays invested, as the Foundations lesson on future value showed.

Two people each invest $300 a month until age 65, earning 5% a year after inflation (illustrative figures). One starts at 25, the other at 35. How much more does the early starter have at 65, in today's money?

Try your own numbers. Using a return after inflation shows the result in today's money, so you can judge what it would actually buy.

You put in
Invested · Kept in cash

Assumes the same return every year, which never happens: real returns vary a lot from year to year. The 5% default is below the long-run US average of about 6.6% after inflation, as no one can promise future returns will match the past.

The price of higher returns is that investments can fall, sometimes a long way. Shares and bonds have each, on several occasions since 1900, lost more than 70% of their value after inflation. World War I, World War II and the oil crisis of 1973 to 1974 caused three of the six worst falls in world share markets since 1900. A mix of 60% shares and 40% bonds never fell more than 50% from peak to trough over the same period, which is one reason investors spread their money, as the lesson on asset allocation will show.

Source: UBS; UBS Chief Investment Office.

Falls like that are bearable if you do not need to sell, because broad, diversified markets have usually recovered over time, though some recoveries took a decade or more, and a few national markets were wiped out entirely, one reason to spread money across countries. They are a disaster if you need the money next year. That is why the time until you need the money matters more than anything else in deciding whether to invest it. Money needed within a few years, such as a home deposit or school fees, usually belongs in savings; money for goals 10 or more years away, such as retirement, usually belongs mostly in investments.

More people invest than ever, in very different ways by country.

United States56% of families owned shares in 2025, directly or through funds and retirement accounts, down slightly from 58% in 2022.
IndiaDemat accounts, needed to hold shares and many funds, reached about 238 million in August 2026, though only about a fifth are actively used.
TaiwanTaiwan's oldest ETF, the Yuanta Taiwan Top 50 (0050), had about 2.96 million investors by May 2026, and over a million regular monthly investment plans.
United KingdomInvestments held in a stocks and shares ISA pay no tax on growth or income, within the £20,000 yearly ISA allowance covered in the taxes lesson.

Sources: Federal Reserve, 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances; Business Today; Asianet Newsable; CNA, via PR Times.

You are ready to invest when the basics are in place. A common order, used by many planners:

1Emergency fundCash for three to six months of essentials, so a surprise never forces you to sell investments at a bad time.
2Expensive debt clearedPaying off a card at 20% or more is a guaranteed return that investing rarely beats.
3Invest regularlyStart with money for goals at least several years away, using low-cost, diversified funds, the subject of the next lesson.

One exception to the order: if an employer adds money to your retirement contributions, it is usually worth contributing enough to get it even while paying down debt, as the retirement lessons will explain. And you do not need much to start: many platforms now let you invest small amounts each month.

How professionals use it

Financial planners
Match each of a client's goals to a time horizon, then decide how much of each goal's money to invest and how much to keep in savings.
Pension fund managers
Invest workers' contributions for decades, relying on long-run returns to pay pensions that keep up with inflation.
Investment researchers
Study long-run return data, such as the Yearbook, to estimate what different assets may earn in the future.
Policymakers
Design tax-advantaged accounts, such as ISAs and retirement plans, to encourage households to invest for the long term.

Keep money you need soon in savings; invest money you will not need for years, and start as early as you can.
