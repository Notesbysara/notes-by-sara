Paying off debt

How to build a plan to get out of debt: listing what you owe, deciding which debts come first, the avalanche and snowball methods, ways to lower the interest rate, and where to get free help when payments are no longer manageable.

Borrowing · Lesson 4 of 4 · 8 minutesBefore this: Loans and mortgages

In plain words

Write down every debt with its balance, interest rate and minimum payment. Keep up the minimum on all of them, and put every extra dollar on one debt at a time. Paying the highest interest rate first costs the least; paying the smallest balance first gives quicker wins. Either works if you stick to it and stop borrowing more.

Debt is a normal part of household finances, but falling behind is becoming more common in the US:

$1.29 trillionUS credit card balances at the end of September 2026, a record, within $19.1 trillion of total household debt
and
5.0%of US household debt was in some stage of delinquency, up from 4.7% three months earlier

Source: ABA Banking Journal, on the New York Fed's Household Debt and Credit Report.

Start with a complete list. For each debt, write the balance, the interest rate (the APR), the minimum monthly payment and the due date. Include everything: cards, store cards, buy now, pay later plans, car loans, student loans, money owed to family. Then add up the minimums and compare them with your budget. The amount left after essentials and minimums is what you have to pay down debt faster.

Some debts come first, whatever their interest rate. Missing rent or mortgage payments can cost you your home, and unpaid energy bills, taxes or court fines can lead to disconnection or legal action. UK debt charities call these priority debts and advise dealing with them before credit cards and personal loans. The same order makes sense everywhere: protect your home, essential services and anything that can lead to legal action, then keep up at least the minimums on everything else, so you avoid late fees and damage to your credit record.

Source: StepChange.

Then choose one debt to attack. Pay the minimum on all the others and put every spare dollar on the target. When it is paid off, move its whole payment to the next one, so the amount you put toward debt keeps growing. There are two ways to choose the order:

AvalancheHighest interest rate first. Each extra dollar removes the most expensive debt, so it costs the least in total interest.
or
SnowballSmallest balance first. You clear whole debts sooner, which cuts the number of bills and keeps you motivated.

You owe a $1,500 personal loan at 9%, a $6,000 credit card at 24% and a $9,000 car loan at 7%, and can pay $800 a month in total. Which order pays the least interest? (illustrative figures)

The cheapest method is not always the one people finish. In a study of thousands of people in debt settlement programs, Kellogg School researchers David Gal and Blakeley McShane found that those who paid off more of their individual debts, the "small victories" approach, were more likely to clear their entire balance, even though paying the highest rates first would have cost less. If you know you will stick to the plan, use the avalanche. If motivation is the problem, the snowball can be worth the extra interest, which is often small.

Source: Kellogg School of Management.

Lowering the interest rate speeds up either method. A few common ways, each with a catch:

Ask your lenderCard issuers sometimes cut the rate for customers who ask, especially those with a good payment record. It costs nothing to try.
Balance transferSome cards offer 0% on transferred balances for a set period, usually for a one-off fee. It only helps if you clear the balance before the offer ends and stop using the old card.
Consolidation loanOne personal loan at a lower rate replaces several card balances. It works only if the cards are not then run up again.
Use savingsCash earning a few percent can clear debt costing 20% or more. Keep a small emergency fund, though, or the next surprise goes back on the card.

Avoid moving unsecured debt, like credit cards, into a loan secured on your home. The rate may be lower, but if you cannot pay, the home is now at risk.

If the minimums are already more than you can pay, get help early. Lenders are usually more flexible before you fall far behind, and free, independent advice exists in most countries:

United KingdomFree debt advice is available from charities such as StepChange, National Debtline and Citizens Advice, and through the government-backed MoneyHelper service.
United StatesNonprofit credit counseling agencies can review your budget and may set up a debt management plan with lower rates. The FTC also suggests contacting card companies directly to negotiate a lower rate or a realistic payment plan.
TaiwanUnder the Consumer Debt Clearance Act, people struggling with unsecured bank debt can apply for pre-negotiation with their largest bank creditor, which can lead to a longer repayment plan at a lower rate. Court-supervised rehabilitation or liquidation is available if that fails.
IndiaTalk to the lender first about restructuring or a revised schedule, and use the bank's grievance process or the RBI's ombudsman scheme if you are treated unfairly.

Sources: Shelter England; ABC7, on FTC guidance; Central Bank of the Republic of China (Taiwan); Taishin Bank.

Be careful with companies that charge to "settle" or "fix" your debts. Paid debt settlement often tells you to stop paying, which brings fees, interest and damage to your credit record while the company negotiates, and taxes may be due on forgiven debt in some countries. Taiwan's banks warn against unlicensed agents offering to arrange debt negotiation, and UK charities point out that free advice is available, so there is no need to pay for it.

Sources: Standard Chartered Taiwan; Independent Age.

Fix the cause, or the debt comes back. Paying off a card and then filling it again is common. While you pay down debt, take cards out of online shopping accounts, keep a small emergency fund for surprises, and check the budget each month. Once the debts are gone, redirect the same monthly payment to savings and investing, the subject of the next section.

How professionals use it

Debt advisers
List a client's debts by priority, build a budget, and negotiate payment plans or formal solutions with creditors.
Financial planners
Decide with clients when to pay down debt and when to save or invest instead, by comparing the debt's interest rate with expected returns.
Collections teams
Work with borrowers who have fallen behind to agree on affordable repayment plans before accounts are written off.
Regulators
Set rules on how lenders treat borrowers in difficulty and police paid debt services that charge high fees.

Protect the essentials, pay every minimum, then put every extra dollar on one debt at a time until all of them are gone.

8 mixed problems from lessons 8 to 11 · about 15 minutes

The problems aren't labeled with a lesson, so part of the work is choosing the right idea. Work each one on paper, then open the answer. All figures are illustrative unless a country's actual rules are named.

  1. You have two credit cards: one with a $4,000 limit and a $1,500 balance, the other with a $6,000 limit and a $1,000 balance. What is your credit utilization? What happens to it if you close the card with the $6,000 limit after paying it off?

    Show answer
    Credit scores$2,500 ÷ $10,000 = 25%. After paying off and closing the $6,000 card, the remaining $1,500 is on a $4,000 limit: 37.5%, higher, even though you owe less.

  2. In one month you check your own credit score three times and apply for two new store cards. Which of these can lower your score?

    Show answer
    Credit scoresOnly the two applications. Each usually adds a hard inquiry. Checking your own score is a soft inquiry and never affects it.

  3. You carry a $2,000 card balance at 24% a year. Roughly how much interest does one month add?

    Show answer
    Credit cards24% ÷ 12 = 2% a month, so about $40.

  4. Your statement shows $900. You pay $900 by the due date. On most cards, how much interest do you pay on those purchases? What if you had paid $850?

    Show answer
    Credit cardsNone, because the full statement balance was paid within the interest-free period. Paying $850 means interest starts on the unpaid amount, and on many cards you also lose the interest-free period on new purchases until you pay in full again.

  5. A $20,000 car loan is repaid monthly over 5 years at 6% a year. What is the monthly payment and the total interest?

    Show answer
    Loans and mortgagesPayment = 20,000 × 0.005 ÷ (1 − 1.005−60) ≈ $386.66. Total paid $23,199, so interest ≈ $3,199.

  6. You buy a $400,000 home with a $60,000 down payment. What is the loan-to-value ratio, and why might a $80,000 down payment get you a better rate?

    Show answer
    Loans and mortgages$340,000 ÷ $400,000 = 85% LTV. With $80,000 down it is 80%. Lenders often charge more, or require mortgage insurance, above about 80%, because there is less cushion if prices fall.

  7. A $200,000 variable-rate mortgage with 25 years left has its rate rise from 5% to 7%. Roughly how much does the monthly payment change?

    Show answer
    Loans and mortgagesFrom about $1,169 to about $1,414, a rise of about $244 a month, or 21%, for a 2-point rise in the rate.

  8. You owe a store card of $800 at 29%, a credit card of $4,000 at 22%, and a student loan of $12,000 at 5%. In what order do the avalanche and snowball methods pay them? Before either, what must you keep paying?

    Show answer
    Paying off debtHere both give the same order: store card, credit card, student loan, because the smallest balance also has the highest rate. Before either, keep up rent or mortgage, essential bills and the minimum on every debt.
