Index funds and ETFs

What a fund is, how index funds and ETFs work, why most actively managed funds trail the index after costs, how much fees cost over decades, and what to check before choosing a fund, with examples from the US, India, Taiwan and the UK.

Investing · Lesson 2 of 5 · 9 minutesBefore this: Why invest

In plain words

A fund pools money from many people and buys many investments at once, so even a small amount is spread widely. An index fund simply buys everything in a market index, such as the 500 large US companies in the S&P 500, instead of trying to pick winners. It costs very little, and over long periods it usually does better than funds that try to beat the market.

Professional fund managers try to beat the market. Most do not, especially over longer periods:

79%of actively managed US large-company funds trailed the S&P 500 in 2025
89%trailed it over the five years to the end of 2025
88%of Australian share funds trailed their index over the 15 years to the end of 2025

Sources: S&P Dow Jones Indices, SPIVA U.S.; SPIVA by the numbers; SPIVA Australia.

The results vary from year to year: in the first half of 2026, 67% of US large-company funds trailed, an improvement. But the long-run pattern is the same across the many countries S&P tracks. The main reason is cost: the market's return is shared by all investors before fees, so the average investor, after fees, must earn less than the market, and active funds charge more.

Source: Advisor Perspectives, on SPIVA Mid-Year 2026.

A fund is a shared basket of investments. Investors buy units or shares in the fund, and a manager uses the money to buy shares, bonds or both. Each investor owns a slice of everything in the basket, so one fund can spread money across hundreds or thousands of companies, the diversification described in the Foundations lesson. Funds come in two main forms:

Mutual fundBought from and sold back to the fund company, at one price set after each trading day. Called a unit trust or OEIC in the UK.
or
ETFAn exchange-traded fund, bought and sold on a stock exchange through a brokerage account during the trading day, like a share.

Either form can be active, with a manager choosing what to buy, or passive, simply tracking an index. Most ETFs are index funds, but some are active, and many mutual funds are index funds.

An index is a rule-based list of investments. The S&P 500 holds about 500 large US companies, India's Nifty 50 holds 50 large Indian companies, the FTSE 100 holds the 100 largest companies on the London Stock Exchange, and the FTSE All-World covers thousands of companies in dozens of countries. Most are weighted by market value, so bigger companies make up more of the index. An index fund buys the companies in the same proportions and changes only when the index does, which keeps trading and research costs low.

Costs are the one thing you control, and they compound. Every fund charges a yearly fee, the expense ratio, called the total expense ratio (TER) in India and the ongoing charges figure (OCF) in the UK. It is taken out of the fund's assets, so you never see a bill, but your return is lower by that amount every year. In the US in 2025, the average expense ratio paid on equity mutual funds was 0.40%, and on index equity ETFs 0.14%, while broad index funds can cost 0.05% or less.

Sources: Investment Company Institute; InvestmentNews.

Index investing has spread worldwide.

United StatesIndex mutual funds and ETFs held 52% of long-term fund assets at the end of 2025, up from 19% in 2010.
IndiaMost investors use systematic investment plans (SIPs), fixed monthly investments into mutual funds: ₹32,160 crore flowed in through nearly 10 crore SIP accounts in October 2026. Each fund offers a direct plan, bought from the fund company, with a lower expense ratio than the regular plan sold through a distributor.
TaiwanThe Yuanta Taiwan Top 50 ETF (0050) is the largest fund, tracking the 50 biggest listed companies. High-dividend ETFs, such as 00878 and 0056, are also widely held.
United KingdomIndex funds and ETFs are usually held through an investment platform, which charges its own fee, often a percentage of the account, so compare the platform's cost as well as the fund's.

Sources: ICI, Trends in the Expenses and Fees of Funds, 2025; AMFI monthly data, October 2026; Taiwan Stock Exchange; Taipei Times.

An index fund is only as diversified as its index. Market-value weighting means a fund can end up dominated by a few giants. In Taiwan's 0050, TSMC made up 58% of the fund in August 2026, compared with 18% when the fund launched in 2003, so its price moves largely with one company. Many large US index funds are similarly weighted toward a handful of technology companies. A fund that tracks the whole world spreads this risk much further, a point the next lesson on asset allocation picks up.

Sources: China Times Wantrich; WantGoo, 0050 holdings.

Not every ETF is a simple index fund. Some are built for short-term trading, and the name alone does not always make that clear:

Leveraged ETFsAim for two or three times an index's daily move. Over longer periods their returns can differ greatly from that multiple, and losses can be severe. They are designed for short-term traders.
Inverse ETFsAim to rise when an index falls, also on a daily basis. They are short-term hedging tools, not long-term holdings.
Narrow theme ETFsTrack one industry or trend. They concentrate risk and often charge more than broad funds.
High-dividend ETFsPick companies paying high dividends. That is a choice of style, not extra free return; dividends are part of the total return, and frequent payouts may be taxed.

Before choosing a fund, check five things. What index or strategy it follows and how broad it is. Its expense ratio, plus any platform or account fees. How closely it has tracked its index, its tracking difference. Its size and trading volume, since very small funds can close. And how it is taxed where you live, which the lesson on taxes and your investments covers. A single low-cost fund that tracks a broad market, held for many years, is enough for many investors.

How professionals use it

Financial planners
Build most client portfolios from a few low-cost index funds, and justify any active fund against its extra cost.
Index providers
Such as S&P, FTSE Russell and MSCI, design and maintain the indexes that funds track, and charge licence fees for their use.
ETF market makers
Create and redeem ETF shares in large blocks to keep the market price close to the value of the fund's holdings.
Pension trustees
Use index funds for large parts of retirement savings to keep costs low for millions of members.

Buy the whole market cheaply, hold it for years, and let low costs and compounding do the work.
