Common investing mistakes

The mistakes that cost ordinary investors the most, with the evidence on each: trying to time the market, chasing what has done well, putting too much in one place, paying too much, borrowing to invest, and falling for scams, plus the simple habits that prevent them.

Investing · Lesson 5 of 5 · 9 minutesBefore this: Taxes and your investments

In plain words

Most investing losses do not come from picking the wrong fund. They come from behavior: selling in a panic, buying after prices have already jumped, betting too much on one thing, paying high fees, or trusting someone who promises easy money. A simple plan, followed calmly for years, avoids nearly all of them.

Investors in US funds earned less than the funds themselves, simply because of when they bought and sold:

9.9%a year: what US mutual funds and ETFs returned over the 10 years to the end of 2025
vs
8.7%a year: what the average dollar invested in them actually earned, about 12% less over the decade

Source: Morningstar, Mind the Gap 2026; WealthManagement.com.

Morningstar calls the difference the investor return gap. The funds did their job; investors lost part of the return by adding money after rises and pulling it out after falls. The gap was widest in the most volatile funds, about 2.1 percentage points a year against 0.4 in the steadiest, and in the newest, most exciting products: from January 2024 to June 2026, the average dollar in US bitcoin ETFs lost about 5.8% a year while the ETFs themselves gained 8.5%. Some researchers estimate a smaller cost from timing alone, but the direction of the evidence is consistent.

Sources: Advisor Perspectives; Morningstar; Financial Analysts Journal.

Mistake 1: trying to time the market. Selling when news is bad and waiting to buy back when things feel safer sounds sensible, but the market's biggest gains often come right after its biggest falls, when fear is highest. Missing a handful of those days does lasting damage.

Over the last 20 years, staying fully invested in the S&P 500 earned about 11.3% a year. What happens to that return if you missed just the 10 best days?

Source: J.P. Morgan, via Chase.

Mistake 2: chasing what has already done well. Money pours into a fund, a sector or a country after it has risen strongly, often near the top, and out after it has fallen, often near the bottom. Last year's best fund is rarely next year's: S&P's persistence studies regularly find that most top-performing funds do not stay at the top. Buying a broad index fund and adding to it on a schedule, whatever the news, removes the temptation.

Source: S&P Dow Jones Indices, U.S. Persistence Scorecard.

Mistake 3: putting too much in one place. A single company can fall and never recover, which is why the Foundations lesson on diversification matters so much for individuals. The risk is doubled when that company is your employer: if it struggles, your job and your savings can suffer at the same time. The same applies, more gently, to holding only your home country's market, as the asset allocation lesson showed. Many planners suggest keeping any single company to a small part of your investments.

Mistake 4: paying too much. High fund fees, frequent trading costs, advice charges that are not clear, and products with high commissions all come straight out of your return, every year. As the index funds lesson showed, a 1% fee instead of 0.1% can leave a 30-year pot about a fifth smaller. Always ask what you pay in total, as a percentage and in money.

Mistake 5: borrowing to invest, or buying what you do not understand. Margin loans, leveraged ETFs, options and other complex products magnify losses as well as gains, and a forced sale after a fall can turn a temporary drop into a permanent loss. If you cannot explain how a product makes money and how it could lose money, it does not belong in a long-term portfolio.

Mistake 6: falling for a scam. Investment fraud is now the costliest form of consumer fraud in both the US and Taiwan, and it increasingly starts on social media and messaging apps:

$7.9 billionlost to investment scams reported to the US Federal Trade Commission in 2025, about half of all reported fraud losses
and
64%of Taiwan's fraud losses in August 2024 came from fake investment scams, NT$8.9 billion in one month

Sources: US Federal Trade Commission; PYMNTS; Taiwan Stock Exchange.

Taiwan's losses have since fallen as police, banks and the stock exchange stepped up their response, but the warning signs are the same everywhere:

Guaranteed high returnsReal investments that can earn a lot can also lose. "Guaranteed" profits of several percent a month do not exist.
Pressure and secrecyBeing told to act now, keep it quiet, or join a private chat group run by a "teacher" or "analyst".
An unfamiliar platform or appEspecially one that shows big paper gains but asks for "fees" or "taxes" before you can withdraw.
A contact you did not seekAn online friend, a celebrity advert or a stranger who turns the conversation to investing.

Before investing with anyone, check that the firm is authorized with your country's regulator, such as the SEC and FINRA's BrokerCheck in the US, the FCA register in the UK, SEBI in India or the Financial Supervisory Commission in Taiwan, and contact it through details you find yourself, not ones the seller gives you. In Taiwan, the 165 anti-fraud hotline can check a suspected scam.

Source: National Immigration Agency, on National Police Agency guidance.

Two quieter mistakes cost just as much over time.

Waiting for the right moment to startHolding long-term money in cash while waiting for a fall gives up years of compounding, as the lesson on why invest showed. Regular monthly investing removes the decision.
Investing money you may need soonWithout an emergency fund, a job loss or repair can force you to sell at a bad time. Keep near-term money in savings.
Checking too oftenDaily price checks make short-term swings feel bigger and push people to act. Reviewing once or twice a year is usually enough.
No written planA short note of your goals, mix and rules, written in calm times, is the best defense against decisions made in a panic.

The habits that prevent almost all of these are simple. Invest automatically every month in a few low-cost, broadly diversified funds. Match each goal's mix to when you need the money, and rebalance once a year. Use tax-advantaged accounts. Keep an emergency fund so you never have to sell in a crisis. And treat anyone promising fast, certain profits as a warning, not an opportunity.

How professionals use it

Financial planners
Spend much of their value on behavior: writing a plan with clients and talking them out of panic selling or chasing fads.
Behavioral finance researchers
Measure gaps between fund and investor returns and design defaults, like automatic investing, that reduce them.
Compliance and fraud teams
At banks and exchanges, spot suspicious transfers and warn customers who may be sending money to scammers.
Regulators
Keep registers of authorized firms, publish warning lists, and pursue investment fraud and misleading advertising.

Your behavior matters more than your fund choice: automate, diversify, keep costs low, ignore the noise, and check before you trust.

9 mixed problems from lessons 12 to 16 · about 15 minutes

The problems aren't labeled with a lesson, so part of the work is choosing the right idea. Work each one on paper, then open the answer. All figures are illustrative unless a country's actual rules are named.

  1. An investment returns 7% in a year when inflation is 3%. Roughly what is the real return, and what is it exactly?

    Show answer
    Why investRoughly 7% − 3% = 4%; exactly 1.07 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 3.9%.

  2. You invest $200 a month for 20 years at 6% a year, compounded monthly. How much do you have, and how much of it is growth?

    Show answer
    Why invest200 × (1.005240 − 1) ÷ 0.005 ≈ $92,400. You paid in $48,000, so about $44,400 is growth.

  3. Why must the average actively managed fund trail its index after costs, even if some managers are skilled?

    Show answer
    Index funds and ETFsAll investors together own the market, so before costs their average return is the market's return. Active funds charge more and trade more, so after costs the average active dollar must earn less than a cheap index fund.

  4. $50,000 is invested for 20 years at 6% a year before fees. Fund A charges almost nothing; fund B takes 1% a year. Roughly how much more does A end with?

    Show answer
    Index funds and ETFsA: 50,000 × 1.0620 ≈ $160,400. B: 50,000 × 1.0520 ≈ $132,700. A ends with about $27,700 more.

  5. You will need $30,000 for a home deposit in 18 months. Should it be in an all-share index fund?

    Show answer
    Asset allocation by goalNo. Shares can fall 30% or more in a year, with no time to recover before you need the money. Keep it in savings or short-term deposits.

  6. Your target is 70% shares, 30% bonds. You now hold $80,000 in shares and $20,000 in bonds. What should you do?

    Show answer
    Asset allocation by goalThe total is $100,000, so the target is $70,000 in shares. Move $10,000 from shares to bonds, or direct new money to bonds until the mix is back to 70/30.

  7. In the UK in 2026/27, a basic-rate taxpayer whose income and gains stay within the basic-rate band makes a £10,000 gain on shares outside an ISA. How much Capital Gains Tax is due?

    Show answer
    Taxes and your investments(£10,000 − £3,000 allowance) × 18% = £1,260. Inside an ISA it would be nothing.

  8. In India, you make a ₹3 lakh gain on an equity mutual fund. How much tax is due if you held it for 14 months, and if you held it for 10 months? (Ignore cess.)

    Show answer
    Taxes and your investments14 months: (₹3,00,000 − ₹1,25,000) × 12.5% = ₹21,875. 10 months: ₹3,00,000 × 20% = ₹60,000.

  9. A friend in an online chat group says a "teacher" on a new trading app guarantees 3% a month, and you can only withdraw after paying a "release fee". What should you do?

    Show answer
    Common investing mistakesTreat it as a scam: guaranteed high returns, a private group, an unfamiliar app and fees to withdraw are classic signs. Do not pay anything, check the firm with your regulator, and report it, for example to the 165 hotline in Taiwan or the FTC in the US.
