How much insurance you need

How to size life cover from what your dependents would actually need, how much income protection to buy and when it should start paying, why a home is insured for its rebuild cost rather than its price, and how to avoid buying too much as well as too little.

Protection · Lesson 2 of 3 · 9 minutesBefore this: Insurance for households

In plain words

The right amount of insurance is the gap between what a disaster would cost and what you could already cover. Add up what your family would need if you died or could not work, subtract savings and cover you already have, and insure the difference. Too little leaves your family exposed; too much wastes money you could save.

Many families are underinsured without knowing it:

38%of US adults, about 92 million, say they need life insurance or more of it
vs
3%of the money Indian households would need if a breadwinner died is covered by life insurance, in a 2023 Swiss Re estimate

Sources: LIMRA and Life Happens, 2026 Insurance Barometer Study; Reinsurance News, on Swiss Re Institute.

Start with who depends on your income. Life insurance exists to replace money other people rely on. A parent with young children, a partner sharing a mortgage, or someone supporting elderly parents usually needs it. A single person with no dependents and no shared debts usually does not, beyond perhaps enough to cover funeral costs. A retiree whose partner would be fine on savings and pensions may no longer need it either.

Size life cover from needs, not from a rule of thumb. "Ten times your income" is a common shortcut, but it ignores how long your family would need support, what debts you have and what you already own. The needs method works through it step by step:

1Income to replaceThe yearly amount your family would need, often less than your full pay, since your own spending stops, multiplied by the years until your youngest child is independent or your partner retires.
2Debts to clearThe mortgage and other loans, so the family keeps the home.
3Future goalsChildren's education and final expenses.
4Subtract what existsSavings and investments, cover through your employer, and any state survivor benefits.

Term cover is usually the cheapest way to fill the gap. A term policy pays only if you die within a set period, which is why it costs far less than whole-life cover for the same amount. Match the term to the need: until the mortgage is paid or the youngest child finishes education. The need usually shrinks over time as debts fall and savings grow, so some people layer two policies of different lengths, or choose decreasing cover tied to a repayment mortgage.

Income protection is sized differently. It replaces a share of your earnings while you cannot work, rather than paying a lump sum. Two choices matter most:

How muchInsurers usually cap the benefit at roughly half to two-thirds of your gross income, so you have a reason to return to work. Aim to cover your essential spending.
When it startsThe waiting or deferred period, often a few weeks to a year. A longer wait makes cover cheaper. Match it to your sick pay and emergency fund.
How long it paysUntil you recover, or up to a set age. Short-term policies paying for one or two years cost less but leave long illnesses uncovered.

Check what the state and your employer already provide. This decides how big a gap you have:

United StatesSocial Security can pay disability and survivor benefits to eligible workers and families, but disability claims have strict tests and long waits. Many employers offer group life and disability cover, often a multiple of salary.
United KingdomStatutory Sick Pay is the lower of £123.25 a week or 80% of average weekly earnings, for up to 28 weeks, from April 2026. Many employers pay more. Beyond that, state benefits are means-tested and modest.
IndiaWorkers covered by the Employees' State Insurance scheme get sickness benefits, but most workers are in informal jobs without them. Term life and personal accident cover fill much of the gap for those who buy them.
TaiwanLabor Insurance pays sickness, disability and death benefits to covered workers. Many households also hold private life and health policies, so check what you already have before adding more.

Sources: GOV.UK; Which?.

Underinsuring property can shrink every claim, not just big ones. Many home policies apply an "average" or co-insurance rule: if you insure for less than the required value, the insurer pays only the same proportion of any claim. Insure buildings at 60% of their rebuild cost and a $10,000 repair might pay only $6,000. Insure belongings at what it would cost to replace them new, and update the rebuild figure every few years as building costs rise.

Health cover depends on your public system. Where public care is broad, such as the UK or Taiwan, private cover mainly adds speed or covers co-payments and treatments outside the public scheme. Where it is thin, as for many households in India, or tied to a job, as in the US, the sum insured and the deductible matter much more. Choose a limit that could pay for a serious hospital stay where you live, and a deductible you could pay from savings.

Too much insurance is a real cost too. Every premium is money that is not going into your emergency fund, debt repayment or retirement savings. Common signs of over-insuring:

Life cover with no dependentsNobody relies on the income it would replace.
Overlapping policiesTwo policies paying for the same loss, when only one will pay or both pay little.
Very low deductiblesPaying extra premium every year to avoid small costs you could cover.
Cover that outlived its needA mortgage paid off and children grown, but the same life cover kept running.

Review cover when life changes. A new child, a home purchase, a pay rise, a divorce, or a paid-off mortgage all change the numbers. A short review every few years, or at each of these events, keeps cover matched to what your household actually needs.

How professionals use it

Financial planners
Run a needs analysis for each client, set life and income cover against existing assets and benefits, and review it at life events.
Insurance brokers and advisers
Compare policies across insurers and match term lengths, waiting periods and limits to a household's needs.
Underwriters
Check that the cover applied for is reasonable compared with the applicant's income and debts before accepting it.
Loss adjusters
Assess property claims and apply the policy's rules, including reductions when a home is underinsured.

Insurance should fill the gap between what a disaster would cost and what you already have, no more and no less.
