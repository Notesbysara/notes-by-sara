Credit scores

What a credit report and a credit score are, how scores work in the US, the UK, India and Taiwan, what raises and lowers them, what a better score is worth in interest, and how to build credit from nothing and fix errors.

Borrowing · Lesson 1 of 4 · 8 minutesBefore this: Your personal financial statements

In plain words

Lenders share records of how people repay what they borrow. A credit score turns your record into one number that predicts how likely you are to fall behind on a loan. A higher score usually means easier approval and a lower interest rate. Paying on time, every time, matters most.

The same $300,000, 30-year mortgage, offered to two borrowers whose scores put them in different price tiers (illustrative figures):

$1,896a month at 6.5% interest
vs
$2,098a month at 7.5%: about $200 more each month, and roughly $72,500 more over 30 years

Rates and the gap between tiers vary by country, lender and year, but the pattern holds wherever credit is scored: a lower score makes borrowing cost more, and sometimes means not being able to borrow at all. Landlords, mobile phone companies and some employers may also check credit records, depending on the country and the law.

The report is the record; the score is a summary of it. A credit report is kept by a credit bureau, called a credit reference agency in the UK. Lenders send it details of your loans and cards: limits, balances, and whether each payment was on time. A credit score is a number calculated from that report by a statistical model, built to predict how likely you are to fall seriously behind on payments, typically over the next year or two. Lenders combine it with your income, existing debts and their own rules.

Each country has its own system:

United StatesThree national bureaus: Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. The most used score is the FICO Score, from 300 to 850; FICO says 90% of top lenders use it. The average FICO Score was 714 in spring 2026.
United KingdomThree credit reference agencies: Experian, Equifax and TransUnion. There is no single score: each agency uses its own scale, such as 0 to 1,000 at Equifax and 0 to 710 at TransUnion, and lenders apply their own scoring to the data.
IndiaFour bureaus licensed by the Reserve Bank of India: TransUnion CIBIL, Experian, Equifax and CRIF High Mark. The CIBIL Score runs from 300 to 900, and most lenders treat 750 or above as good. Since 1 July 2026, lenders must update bureau records every week.
TaiwanOne central bureau, the Joint Credit Information Center (JCIC), which banks check before approving cards and loans. Its score runs from 200 to 800. People with too little history get no score, and a serious past default can bring a fixed score of 200.

Sources: myFICO; AAA, on FICO data; ClearScore; Royal London; Upstox; Sahi; Money101; Business Next.

Pay on timeMissed payments do the most damage, and the later and more recent they are, the worse. Automatic payment of at least the minimum prevents most of them.
Use little of your limitsCredit utilization is card balances divided by card limits. $1,500 owed on $5,000 of limits is 30%. Lower is better, and the balance reported on your statement is what counts, even if you pay it off in full.
Keep old accountsA longer history gives more evidence. Old accounts in good standing help, even if you rarely use them.
Apply only when neededEach application for credit usually leaves a hard inquiry on your report, and several in a short time can lower your score. Checking your own score is a soft inquiry and never affects it.

Sources: Experian; Experian UK.

Having no record can be as hard as having a bad one. A lender cannot score what it cannot see. In the US, about 12.7 million adults, 4.9%, had no credit score in early 2025. In Taiwan, a new graduate with no loans or cards typically gets "no score at this time" from the JCIC. The usual ways to start are the same in most countries: a basic credit card with a low limit, paid in full each month; a secured card backed by your own deposit; or a small loan repaid on schedule. In the UK, being on the electoral register also helps lenders confirm who you are. Some lenders in several countries now also accept rent, utility or phone payment records.

Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis; Business Next; Royal London.

Bad marks fade, but slowly. In the US, most accurate negative information can stay on a report for seven years, and bankruptcy for ten. Their effect on the score shrinks as they age and as newer, on-time payments pile up. The fastest recovery is simply a long run of payments made on time.

Source: US Federal Trade Commission.

Check your reports for free, and dispute errors. Mistakes happen: an account that is not yours, a payment marked late that was on time, a debt shown twice. They can cost you a loan or a better rate, and an unknown account can also be the first sign of identity theft.

United StatesFree reports from all three bureaus, once a week, at AnnualCreditReport.com, the only site authorized by federal law.
United KingdomA free statutory report from each of the three agencies, as often as you like.
IndiaOne free full report a year from each of the four bureaus. Disputes must be resolved within 30 days.
TaiwanYou can apply to the JCIC for your own report online, in person, or through the post office.

Sources: FTC; ClearScore; Upstox; TransUnion CIBIL; Taiwan government open data, on JCIC applications.

To dispute an error, contact both the bureau and the lender that reported it, explain what is wrong, and include copies of any proof. Be wary of "credit repair" companies that promise to wipe your record for a fee: as the US Federal Trade Commission puts it, no one can legally remove accurate negative information, and anything they can do about real errors you can do yourself for free.

Source: FTC, Fixing Your Credit.

How professionals use it

Loan underwriters
Combine the score with income, existing debts and the loan's size to approve or decline, and to set the interest rate.
Risk modelers
Build and test the scoring models, and check that scores still predict defaults as the economy changes.
Financial planners
Help clients raise their scores before a big loan, such as a mortgage, by cutting card balances and fixing errors.
Regulators
Set rules on what bureaus may record, how long negative information stays, and how fast errors must be fixed.

Pay on time, keep card balances low, borrow only when you need to, and check your reports once in a while.
