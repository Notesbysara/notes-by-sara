The risk and return trade-off

Why riskier investments have to offer higher expected returns, what the risk-free rate is, and why a risk premium is never a promise.

Risk and return · Lesson 1 of 5 · 5 minutesBefore this: Expected value, What sets interest rates

In plain words

People don't like uncertainty. To persuade them to put money into something that might lose value, it has to offer the chance of earning more than a safe alternative. That extra expected return is the reward for taking risk.

Over the 97 years from 1928 to 2024, US stocks returned about 9.9% a year and three-month Treasury bills about 3.3%. Compounded, $100 invested at the start would have grown to roughly:

≈ $982,000in US stocks, dividends reinvested
vs
≈ $2,350in Treasury bills

Returns: Aswath Damodaran, NYU Stern, via A Wealth of Common Sense. End values compounded from the average yearly returns.

If that were the whole story, nobody would hold bills. But stocks fell in 26 of those 97 years, and in 1931 they lost 43.8%. The higher long-run return is the payment for living through those years.

Risk means uncertainty about the outcome, not just the possibility of loss. An investment that might return 2% or 20% is risky even though it never loses money. The wider the range of possible results, the riskier it is.

Investment A pays $100 next year, guaranteed. Investment B pays $0 or $200 with equal chances. Both have an expected payoff of $100. Which sells for more today?

The starting point is the risk-free rate: the return on something with no doubt about the payment. In practice that means short-term government bills in the government's own currency. Every risky investment is priced against it:

Required return = risk-free rate + risk premium

With a 4% risk-free rate and a 5% premium for a stock's risk, investors require 9% (illustrative figures). That is the hurdle rate from earlier lessons: the riskier the cash flows, the higher the rate used to discount them, and the lower their value today.

Across asset classes, the long-run pattern lines up with risk. Average yearly returns, 1928 to 2024:

Small US stocks
11.7%
US stocks (S&P 500)
9.9%
Gold
5.1%
10-year Treasury bonds
4.5%
Real estate
4.2%
Treasury bills (cash)
3.3%

Inflation averaged about 3% a year over the same period, so cash barely kept pace. Source as above.

The ordering is not perfect, and gold and property behave in their own ways. But the broad pattern is the one theory predicts: the assets with the largest swings have paid the most over long periods.

A risk premium is expected, not promised. If riskier assets were certain to earn more, they would not be risky. There have been ten-year stretches when US stocks did worse than bills. The premium shows up over long horizons and on average, which is exactly why it has to be paid.

Not every risk earns a premium. Risk that can be removed cheaply by spreading money across many investments is not rewarded. That is the subject of Diversification.

How professionals use it

Asset allocators
Decide how much of a portfolio to put in stocks, bonds and cash, trading higher expected return against bigger swings.
Corporate finance teams
Set the hurdle rate for a project as the risk-free rate plus a premium that matches the project's risk.
Pension funds
Assume a long-run return on their assets, and must justify the risk premium built into it.
Equity analysts
Estimate the equity risk premium, the extra return the whole stock market is expected to pay over government bonds.

In everyday terms

Money you need in two years for a deposit on a house should not sit in stocks; a bad year could take a third of it. Money you won't touch for thirty years can afford the swings.

Match the risk you take to how long you can wait.

Higher returns are the price of bearing risk. They are expected, never promised.
