Annuities and loan payments

Valuing a stream of equal payments, and using the same formula to work out what a loan costs each month.

Time value of money · Lesson 3 of 8 · 6 minutesBefore this: Present value and discounting, Summing a geometric series

In plain words

An annuity is the same payment made at regular intervals, like rent or a pension. A loan works the same way in reverse: you get a lump sum now and pay it back in equal installments. Each installment pays that month's interest first, and the rest reduces what you owe.

The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate in the US hit a record low of 2.65% in January 2021. By October 2023 it was close to 8%. On the same $400,000 loan, that is the difference between:

$1,612 a monthat 2.65%, about $180,000 of interest over 30 years
vs
$2,935 a monthat 8%, about $657,000 of interest over 30 years

The house is the same. The rate nearly doubled the monthly payment and more than tripled the total interest. Both numbers come from one formula.

An annuity is a fixed payment repeated at regular intervals for a set period. Its value today is the present value of every payment, added up. Because each payment is discounted by one more factor of (1 + r), the sum is a geometric series, and it collapses into a single expression:

PV = C × 1 − (1 + r)−nr

C is the payment, r the rate per period and n the number of payments. The fraction is the annuity factor: the value today of $1 paid every period.

A loan is an annuity seen from the lender's side. The bank pays out the loan amount today in exchange for a stream of equal payments, so the loan amount is the present value. Solve the formula for C and you get the payment:

C = PV × r1 − (1 + r)−n

A $20,000 car loan at 6% a year over five years: r = 0.5% a month, n = 60, so C = $386.66 a month (illustrative figures).

Match the rate to the payment period. Monthly payments need the monthly rate (annual ÷ 12) and the number of months.

In the first year of that $400,000 mortgage at 8%, you pay $35,221. How much of it reduces the loan?

InterestRepaying the loan

Each year's payments on the $400,000 loan at 8%. The total stays at $35,221; the split shifts.

This is amortization. Each month's interest is charged on the balance still owed. Early on the balance is large, so interest takes most of the payment. As the balance falls, interest shrinks and more of each payment goes to the loan. The halfway point in time is far from halfway in debt repaid.

The same factor works for saving. Paying in $500 a month at 6% a year for 30 years builds about $502,000, from $180,000 of contributions. The future value of an annuity is C × [(1 + r)n − 1] ÷ r.

When payments come at the start of each period, it is an annuity due. Rent and leases usually work this way. Every payment arrives one period earlier, so the value is the ordinary annuity × (1 + r).

Monthly payment:

How professionals use it

Mortgage lenders
Set payments with this formula and produce the amortization schedule that shows every month's split.
Insurers
Price annuities sold to retirees: a lump sum today in exchange for income for life.
Bond analysts
Value a bond's coupons as an annuity, then add the present value of the final repayment.
Leasing companies
Work out lease payments as an annuity due, since payments are made in advance.

In everyday terms

Because early payments are mostly interest, paying a little extra early does a lot. Adding $200 a month to that $400,000 loan at 8% clears it about six years sooner and saves roughly $160,000 of interest (illustrative figures).

Compare loans by total cost, not just the monthly payment. A longer term lowers the payment and raises the total.

The payment stays the same. What changes is how much of it is interest.
