Present value and discounting

Converting future cash into today's money, and why the discount rate matters most for cash that is far away.

Time value of money · Lesson 2 of 8 · 6 minutesBefore this: Future value and compounding

In plain words

$100 next year is worth less than $100 today, because money you hold now can earn something while you wait. Present value is the amount you would need today to end up with that future sum. Finding it is called discounting.

In November 2022, one ticket in California won the largest Powerball jackpot ever. The winner was offered two ways to collect it:

$2.04 billion30 yearly payments over 29 years, rising 5% a year
or
$997.6 millionin cash, today

Source: Powerball, November 2022. Both amounts are before tax.

Powerball treats these as the same prize. The cash figure is what the 30 payments are worth today. The gap between the two is the time value of money.

A future dollar is worth less than a dollar now for three reasons:

Opportunity costMoney in hand can be invested while you wait.
InflationThe future dollar will buy less.
RiskA promise to pay later may not be kept.

The discount rate prices all three at once. It is the return you could earn elsewhere on something of similar risk.

PV = FV(1 + r)n

1/(1 + r)n is the discount factor, the price today of $1 delivered in n years.

Discounting is compounding run backwards. The formula is the future value formula from the last lesson, rearranged. There, $1,000 at 6% grew to $5,743 over 30 years. Reverse it: $5,743 due in 30 years, discounted at 6%, is worth $1,000 today.

Year 1 means the end of the first year. Cash received today is not discounted.

The further away the cash, the more the rate matters, because the rate is applied once for every year of waiting. Raise the rate from 5% to 7% and see what happens to $1,000:

Due inAt 5%At 7%Change
1 year$952$935−2%
30 years$231$131−43%

This is why long-term bonds and growth stocks, whose value sits mostly in distant cash flows, fall hardest when interest rates rise.

Several cash flows: discount each one, then add. Amounts from different years can only be added once they are moved to the same date.

Take an asset paying $500, $500 and $1,000 over three years, with 8% available elsewhere (illustrative figures):

The payments add up to $2,000, but they are worth $1,685 today. Paying $1,700 would mean accepting less than 8% when 8% is on offer.

Back to the lottery. Discount its 30 rising payments at about 4.4% a year and you get exactly the $997.6 million cash option. The $2.04 billion headline just adds up dollars from different years.

This sum is the core of all valuation. Bonds, shares and projects are all priced as the present value of the cash they will pay. The next lessons on annuities, perpetuities and NPV are shortcuts for the same sum when cash flows follow a pattern.

How professionals use it

Bond traders
Price every bond as the present value of its coupons and principal. When yields rise, they are raising the discount rate, so prices fall.
Equity analysts
Build discounted cash flow models: forecast a company's cash flows for years ahead, discount them, and compare the total with the share price.
Corporate finance teams
Approve investments only when the present value of the expected cash flows exceeds the cost.
Pension funds and insurers
Discount promises they must pay decades from now to see how much money they need to hold today.

In everyday terms

A new employer offers a $5,000 signing bonus now, or $5,500 if you stay two years (illustrative figures).

With savings earning 4%, the later bonus is worth about $5,085 today. Waiting gains only $85, and you carry the risk of leaving or the offer falling through.

Whenever money is offered at different dates, move it to the same date before you compare.

A sum of money means little until you know when it arrives.
