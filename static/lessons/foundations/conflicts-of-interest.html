Conflicts of interest and fiduciary duty

What happens when the person advising you gains more from one choice than another, the ways firms deal with it, and the legal duty to put the client first.

Ethics and trust · Lesson 2 of 3 · 6 minutesBefore this: Why trust holds the system together

In plain words

A conflict of interest exists when the person helping you would earn more if you made one choice rather than another. It doesn't mean they are dishonest, but it pulls their judgment. A fiduciary is someone legally required to put your interests ahead of their own.

In 2015, the White House Council of Economic Advisers estimated what conflicted advice costs Americans saving for retirement in individual accounts:

≈ 1 pointlower annual return for savers receiving conflicted advice
and
≈ $17 billiona year in losses across those savers

Source: White House fact sheet, February 2015. Industry reviewers disputed the size of the estimate.

One percentage point sounds small. Over 35 years it can cut savings by more than a quarter. The cost of a conflict rarely shows up as a single bad decision. It shows up as a steady drag that compounds.

A conflict of interest exists when a professional's own interest, or a third party's, could pull their judgment away from the client's. Common forms:

Different pay for different productsA higher commission on one fund than on a similar, cheaper one.
The firm's own productsRecommending in-house funds when better outside options exist.
Personal tradingBuying a security for yourself before placing a large client order that will push its price up.
Gifts and favorsHospitality or business from a company whose products you then recommend.

An adviser recommends one of two similar funds for a $100,000 retirement account, both earning 6% a year before costs (illustrative figures):

Fund AFund B
Upfront commission3.0%0.5%
Yearly fee1.2%0.2%
Adviser's commission on $100,000$3,000$500

After 20 years, roughly how much less does the client have in Fund A than in Fund B?

Firms deal with conflicts in three ways:

AvoidRemove the conflict: pay advisers the same whatever the client buys, or ban personal trading in client stocks.
DiscloseTell the client clearly, in advance, what the conflict is and how the adviser is paid.
ManagePut controls around it: separate teams, approval of staff trades, independent review of recommendations.

Disclosure alone is weak. Research suggests that clients rarely adjust enough for a disclosed conflict, and that advisers who have disclosed may feel freer to act on it. Disclosure works best alongside avoiding or managing the conflict, not instead of it.

Fiduciary duty is the legal answer to the trust problem. A fiduciary owes the client two things:

Duty of loyaltyPut the client's interests first. Avoid conflicts where possible, and fully disclose and manage any that remain.
Duty of careGive advice that is competent, well researched and suited to the client's circumstances.

Not everyone who gives financial advice is a fiduciary at all times. In the US, registered investment advisers owe a fiduciary duty; broker-dealers must act in the client's "best interest" when recommending, a related but different standard. Clients are entitled to ask which one applies.

Difference:

How professionals handle it

Compliance teams
Keep a register of the firm's conflicts and the controls around each one.
Fee-only advisers
Are paid only by the client, never by product providers, which removes the commission conflict.
Research departments
Are kept separate from deal-making teams, so recommendations are not shaped by the firm's banking clients.
Boards of directors
Include independent members to oversee decisions where managers' interests differ from shareholders'.

In everyday terms

Three questions for anyone advising you: How are you paid? Are you acting as a fiduciary for me at all times? Would you earn more if I chose something else?

A good adviser will answer all three plainly.

Ask how your adviser is paid before you ask what to buy.
