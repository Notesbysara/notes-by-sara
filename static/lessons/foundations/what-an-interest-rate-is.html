What an interest rate is

The price of money over time, seen from both sides, and the difference between what you earn in dollars and what you earn in buying power.

Money and markets · Lesson 4 of 5 · 6 minutesBefore this: Percentages and percentage change

In plain words

An interest rate is the price of using someone else's money for a while. If prices in the shops rise faster than your interest, your money grows in number but buys less. The rate that matters is the one left after inflation.

In June 2022, an American with money in an average savings account faced these two numbers:

0.07%national average interest rate on savings accounts
vs
9.1%rise in consumer prices over the previous 12 months

Sources: Fortune, NPR. Inflation measured by the US consumer price index.

The balance went up. What it could buy went down by more than 8% in a year. Both statements are true, and the gap between them is the difference between a nominal and a real interest rate.

An interest rate is the price of borrowing, quoted per year as a percentage of the amount borrowed. Borrow $1,000 at 6% for a year and you repay $1,060. The same number looks different from each side:

To the lender

A reward for giving up the use of the money, for the risk of not being repaid, and for the loss of buying power while waiting.

To the borrower

The cost of having money now instead of later. Worth paying when the money earns more than the rate, or matters more now.

The nominal rate is what you are paid in dollars. The real rate is what you gain in buying power. The nominal rate has to cover inflation first; only what is left is a real return:

Inflation 4%
Real 2%
Nominal rate 6%, split roughly

The Fisher equation makes this exact. Growth in dollars equals growth in buying power times growth in prices:

(1 + n) = (1 + r) × (1 + i)

Here n is the nominal rate, r the real rate and i inflation. Rearranged, r = (1 + n) / (1 + i) − 1. The shortcut r ≈ n − i is close when rates are low and drifts when they are high:

You held $10,000 in an average account from June 2021 to June 2022. Measured in June 2021 prices, what was it worth by the end?

Real rate:

Lenders set nominal rates using the inflation they expect. If inflation turns out higher, the real rate they actually earn is lower than they planned. So unexpected inflation quietly moves wealth from lenders to borrowers. Homeowners with fixed-rate mortgages gained in 2022 for exactly this reason: their payments stayed fixed while wages and prices rose.

A negative real rate is common. It means savers are paying, in buying power, for the safety of their deposit.

How professionals use it

Central bankers
Judge whether policy is tight or loose by the real rate, not the nominal one. A 5% rate with 6% inflation is still loose.
Bond investors
Compare ordinary government bonds with inflation-protected ones. The gap between their yields shows the inflation the market expects.
Lenders
Build expected inflation into every loan rate, and use variable rates when they cannot predict it.
Pension and endowment managers
Set their targets in real terms, because their promises have to be met in future prices.

In everyday terms

Before comparing savings accounts, compare the rate with inflation. A 4% account when prices rise 3% earns you about 1% in buying power.

On a loan, inflation works the other way. A fixed rate becomes cheaper in real terms if inflation rises after you borrow.

What counts is not how many dollars you earn, but what they will buy.
