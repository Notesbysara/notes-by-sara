Diversification

How spreading money across investments cuts risk without cutting expected return, why correlation decides how much it helps, and the risk that no amount of spreading removes.

Risk and return · Lesson 4 of 5 · 6 minutesBefore this: Correlation, Measuring risk

In plain words

If all your money is in one company and it fails, you lose everything. Spread it across many companies and one failure hurts only a little, because the others carry on. That is diversification. It works best when the investments don't all rise and fall together.

At the end of 2000, Enron's employee retirement plan held $2.1 billion. Its biggest holding was the employer itself:

Employees lost more than $1 billion of retirement savings, and many lost their jobs in the same month. Their savings and their income depended on a single company.

Diversification cuts risk because investments don't move in lockstep. When one falls, others may hold steady or rise, so the portfolio swings less than its parts. How much this helps depends on correlation. Two investments, each with 20% volatility, held half and half (illustrative figures):

Correlation between themPortfolio volatility

The expected return of the mix is just the average of the two. The risk is lower. That is why diversification is often called the only free lunch in finance: unless correlation is exactly +1, it reduces risk without giving up expected return.

You own one stock with 35% volatility. You spread the same money equally across 20 similar stocks. Roughly what happens to your volatility?

Illustrative: stocks with 35% volatility each and a typical correlation of 0.2 between them. Most of the benefit comes from the first 20 or so holdings.

Risk comes in two kinds. Specific risk belongs to one company: a failed product, a fraud, a lawsuit. It can be diversified away. Systematic risk hits almost everything at once: recessions, interest rate shocks, financial crises. No number of stocks removes it.

Only systematic risk earns a premium. Because specific risk can be removed cheaply by holding many investments, markets do not pay anyone for carrying it. An undiversified investor takes extra risk for no extra expected return.

Diversification is weakest when it is most wanted. In severe crises, correlations between risky assets tend to rise, and things that usually move independently fall together.

Back to Enron. The employees' problem was not that Enron was risky; many companies are. It was concentration. Their savings, their pay and their careers all rode on the same company, so one failure took all three at once.

How professionals use it

Portfolio managers
Estimate correlations between holdings and combine assets so the whole portfolio is less risky than its parts.
Pension and endowment funds
Spread money across stocks, bonds, property and other assets in many countries.
Insurers
Diversify by writing policies across many regions and types of risk, so no single disaster sinks them.
Index fund providers
Offer the whole market in one fund, which removes nearly all specific risk at very low cost.

In everyday terms

A low-cost fund that tracks a broad market index gives you hundreds of companies in one purchase. Adding bonds and other countries spreads the risk further.

Be most careful with your employer's shares. Your job already depends on that company.

Diversification removes the risk you are not paid to take.
