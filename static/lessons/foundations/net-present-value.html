Net present value

The standard test for whether an investment is worth making: value everything it will pay back in today's money and subtract what it costs.

Time value of money · Lesson 7 of 8 · 6 minutesBefore this: Uneven and growing cash flows

In plain words

Net present value asks one question: is what you get back, counted in today's money, worth more than what you put in? If yes, the investment makes you richer. If no, you would do better putting the money somewhere else.

The Channel Tunnel between Britain and France opened in 1994. Measured against the forecasts it was approved on:

+80%construction cost over budget, in real terms; financing costs 140% over
and
½revenues roughly half of what was forecast

Sources: Flyvbjerg, "Survival of the Unfittest"; Anguera (2006).

A later study concluded that the British economy would have been better off had the tunnel never been built. In the language of this lesson, it had a negative net present value. The tunnel works well. It simply cost more, in today's money, than it returned.

NPV is the present value of every future cash flow, minus the cost today:

NPV = −C0 + Σ CFt(1 + r)t

C0 is the upfront cost, CFt the cash flow in year t, and r the discount rate. The rule: invest when NPV is above zero. A positive NPV is the amount by which the project makes the investor richer, in today's dollars, after earning the required return.

The discount rate is the hurdle. It is the return the same money could earn elsewhere at similar risk, so riskier projects face higher rates. A project with NPV of zero earns exactly that hurdle: acceptable, but it adds nothing.

A café owner can buy a $10,000 oven that adds $3,000 of cash a year for five years. The hurdle is 10% (illustrative figures):

The oven's NPV at different discount rates. The higher the hurdle, the lower the NPV; above about 15.2% the project destroys value.

The oven's extra cash turns out to be $2,400 a year, not $3,000: a 20% shortfall. What happens to the NPV at 10%?

NPV is only as good as the cash flows that go into it. Four rules keep them honest:

Cash, not profitCount money when it actually moves. Accounting profit spreads costs over time; NPV does not.
Only what changesInclude cash flows that happen because of the project, and nothing that would happen anyway.
Ignore sunk costsMoney already spent cannot be recovered by any decision, so it does not belong in the calculation.
Count what you give upIf the project uses a building you could rent out, the lost rent is a cost.

NPV:

Back to the Channel Tunnel. Its approval rested on forecasts of cost and traffic. Both were wrong in the same direction, the direction research finds again and again in large projects. The method was sound. The inputs were optimistic.

How professionals use it

Corporate finance teams
Require an NPV analysis before any major investment, and test how it changes if sales or costs miss the forecast.
Private equity investors
Value a company purchase as the NPV of its future cash flows and sale price.
Government analysts
Run cost-benefit appraisals of roads and railways, using an official social discount rate.
Property developers
Compare building costs today with the present value of future rents or sales.

In everyday terms

Solar panels cost $12,000 and cut your power bill by $1,200 a year for 20 years. At 6%, their NPV is about +$1,760, so they pay off, but by less than the $24,000 of total savings suggests (illustrative figures).

Ask what the money could earn elsewhere, then check whether the investment beats it in today's dollars.

Invest when what comes back, valued today, exceeds what goes out today.
