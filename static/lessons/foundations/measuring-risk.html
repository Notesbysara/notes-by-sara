Measuring risk

Volatility, drawdown and downside risk: three ways to put a number on how uncomfortable an investment can be.

Risk and return · Lesson 3 of 5 · 6 minutesBefore this: Variance and standard deviation, Measuring return

In plain words

Risk shows up in two ways: how much an investment's value jumps around, and how far it can fall before it recovers. The first is called volatility. The second is called drawdown. Both matter, because a fall you cannot sit through is a fall you end up locking in.

The Nasdaq Composite closed at a record 5,048.62 on March 10, 2000, at the height of the dot-com boom. What followed:

−78%fall to 1,114 by October 2002, in about 31 months
then
15 yearsuntil it closed above its 2000 peak, on April 23, 2015

Sources: Fortune, AFP via Phys.org.

An average return tells you nothing about a stretch like that. Measuring risk means describing the swings and the falls, not just the destination.

Volatility is the standard deviation of returns. It measures how widely returns scatter around their average. If returns are roughly bell-shaped, about two-thirds of years land within one standard deviation of the average. An investment averaging 10% with 20% volatility will, in most years, return somewhere between −10% and +30%.

Volatility scales with the square root of time. To turn monthly volatility into a yearly figure, multiply by √12; for daily, by √252, the number of trading days. A 4% monthly volatility is about 4% × 3.46 = 13.9% a year.

This assumes returns in different periods are independent of each other. Over long horizons that is only roughly true.

Drawdown is the fall from a previous peak to a later low. It answers the question investors actually feel: how much did I lose from the best point?

An illustrative price path. The shaded area is the time spent below the previous peak.

The Nasdaq fell 78% from its peak. How much did it then need to rise to get back there?

Losses and gains are not symmetric. After a loss, a smaller base has to grow by more to recover. The gain needed is 1 ÷ (1 − loss) − 1:

DrawdownGain needed to recover
−10%+11%
−20%+25%
−30%+43%
−50%+100%
−78%+355%

At a steady 10% a year, recovering from a 78% loss would take about 16 years. The Nasdaq, rising faster than that, still needed about 12½ years from its low in October 2002 to its new high in April 2015.

Volatility treats a surprise gain as risk too. Most investors only mind the downside, so two further measures look at losses alone. Downside deviation is volatility calculated only from returns below a target, such as zero. Value at risk states a loss that should not be exceeded with a given confidence: "a 95% one-month VaR of $2 million" means a loss larger than $2 million is expected in about one month in twenty.

How professionals use it

Risk managers
Set limits on volatility and value at risk for each desk or fund, and report them daily.
Portfolio managers
Size positions so the portfolio's overall volatility stays near a target.
Fund investors
Compare a manager's maximum drawdown with their returns. A strong record that came with a 60% fall is a different proposition.
Bank regulators
Require banks to hold capital based on how much their trading positions could lose in bad periods.

In everyday terms

Before investing, look at the worst fall the investment has had, not just its average return. Then ask whether you would have held on through it without selling.

The drawdown you can live through matters more than the return you hope for.

Volatility measures the bumps. Drawdown measures the fall.
