Perpetuities and growing perpetuities

How a stream of payments that never ends can still have a finite price, and what happens when those payments grow.

Time value of money · Lesson 4 of 8 · 5 minutesBefore this: Annuities and loan payments, Infinite series

In plain words

A perpetuity pays the same amount every year, forever. It still has a price, because payments far in the future are worth almost nothing today. The price is simply the yearly payment divided by the interest rate.

In 1648, a Dutch water board borrowed money to repair the dikes along the river Lek. It promised to pay interest every year, with no end date. Yale University now owns the bond:

A bond that pays forever is called a perpetuity. It raises an obvious question: what is an infinite stream of payments worth?

The answer is finite, and short:

PV = Cr

C is the yearly payment and r the discount rate. The quickest way to see why: put C ÷ r in an account paying r, and it earns exactly C a year forever without the balance ever falling. So C ÷ r today and C a year forever are worth the same. Formally, it is the sum of an infinite geometric series.

A preferred share paying $5 a year forever, at 8%, is worth $5 ÷ 0.08 = $62.50 (illustrative figures).

Value today of each $5 payment (left scale)Running total (right scale)

$5 a year forever at 8%. Payments after year 30 add only the last 10% of the value.

Infinity is cheap because distance shrinks value. The payment in year 60 is worth about 5 cents today. The total converges, and most of it comes from the first few decades.

A perpetuity pays $100 a year. Market rates fall from 5% to 4%. What happens to its value?

If the payment grows at a steady rate g, the formula becomes:

PV = C1r − g

C1 is next year's payment, not this year's. A share expected to pay a $3 dividend next year, growing 4% a year, discounted at 9%, is worth $3 ÷ 0.05 = $60 (illustrative figures).

The result is very sensitive to g as it approaches r. Keep r at 9% and C1 at $3:

The formula only works when g is below r. If payments grew as fast as the discount rate, every future payment would be worth as much as today's, and the sum would never stop.

Value today:

Back to the 1648 bond. Its payment is fixed, about €11 a year today, so inflation has eroded it for nearly four centuries. A fixed perpetuity loses buying power every year. One whose payments grow with inflation keeps it, which is exactly what the growing formula describes.

How professionals use it

Equity analysts
Value everything beyond their forecast years as a growing perpetuity, called the terminal value. It is often more than half of a company's estimated worth.
Property investors
Value a building as its yearly rent divided by a capitalization rate, a perpetuity in all but name.
Banks and insurers
Issue perpetual bonds and preferred shares, which count toward the capital regulators require them to hold.
Endowment managers
Set spending so the fund can pay out a growing amount forever without running down.

In everyday terms

A flat that earns $24,000 a year after costs, valued at a 5% rate, is worth about $24,000 ÷ 0.05 = $480,000 (illustrative figures).

Any time someone values an asset as "income divided by a rate," they are using a perpetuity. Small changes in that rate move the value a lot.

Forever has a price: the payment divided by the rate.
