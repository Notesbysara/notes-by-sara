R-squared and residuals

A fitted line never passes through every dot. R-squared says how much of the movement the line explains. The residuals, what's left over, tell you whether a straight line was the right choice at all.

Relationships · Lesson 3 of 3 · 5 minutesBefore this: Linear regression

How much of a stock's movement is just the market?

Example, with made-up numbers. In the last lesson, the stock's line was stock = −0.4 + 1.4 × market. The market clearly matters. But in some months the stock did its own thing. How much of its ups and downs does the market explain, and how much is the company itself?

The idea

Measure how much the stock moves in total: the sum of its squared gaps from its average. Part of that the line explains. The rest is in the residuals, each month's actual return minus the line's prediction. R-squared, written R², is the share the line explains:

Top: what the line missed each month. Bottom: the stock's total movement, split into what the line explains and what's left.

R² = 1 − (sum of squared residuals ÷ total sum of squared gaps)

R² runs from 0 (the line explains nothing) to 1 (every dot sits on the line). With a single x, it's simply the correlation squared.

Quick check

Regressing a stock on the market gives R² = 0.30. What does that say?

Worked example: same five months

Example, with made-up numbers. Market: 2, −1, 3, 0, 1. Stock: 3, −2, 4, 1, −1. Line: stock = −0.4 + 1.4 × market.

1. The line's prediction each month2.4, −1.8, 3.8, −0.4, 1.0
2. Residuals: actual − predicted0.6, −0.2, 0.2, 1.4, −2.0
3. Sum of squared residuals0.36 + 0.04 + 0.04 + 1.96 + 4 = 6.4
4. Total sum of squared gaps (from the correlation lesson)26
5. R²1 − 6.4 ÷ 26 = 0.754
Share explained by the market75%

Check: the correlation was 0.87, and 0.87² ≈ 0.75. The residuals add up to zero, as they always do for a least-squares line. In Excel: =RSQ(stock range, market range).

Why finance cares

For a stock regressed on the market, R² splits its risk in two. The explained part is market risk: you can't diversify it away. The residual part is company-specific risk, which shrinks as you hold more stocks. Here that's 75% market and 25% company. For funds, R² against a benchmark shows how closely the fund follows it. A fund with R² near 1 and high fees is charging active fees for something close to an index fund.

Reading the residuals

Good residuals look like random noise around zero. Three patterns mean the line is the wrong tool:

A curve (U or arch shape)The relationship isn't straight
A fan that widensThe spread grows with x, so predictions are less reliable at the high end
One huge residualAn outlier may be pulling the whole line

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Using prices instead of returns. Two prices that both drift upward over time can show a high R² with no real link. Regress returns.
  • Reading a high R² as proof. It says the line fits, not that x causes y, and not that it will keep fitting.
  • Dismissing a low R². A stock's beta can be useful even when the market explains only 30% of its moves.

Remember

  • Residual = actual − predicted. A good line leaves residuals with no pattern.
  • R² = 1 − squared residuals ÷ total squared gaps: the share of movement the line explains. With one x, it's the correlation squared.
  • For a stock against the market, R² is the share of market risk. The rest is company-specific.
