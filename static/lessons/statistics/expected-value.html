Expected value

When the outcome is uncertain, what is a bet, a loan or an investment worth? Expected value weighs each outcome by its chance, and it's the starting point for pricing almost anything in finance.

Probability · Lesson 4 of 7 · 5 minutesBefore this: Weighted averages, Probability basics

Worth $10,000?

Example, with made-up numbers. You can put $10,000 into a start-up. There's a 70% chance you lose it all, a 25% chance you get back $20,000, and a 5% chance you get back $200,000. On average, how much would you get back?

The idea

Multiply each outcome by its probability and add them up. It's a weighted average, with the probabilities as the weights:

Expected value = sum of (probability × outcome)

The rare $200,000 outcome adds the most to the average: $10,000 of the $15,000.

Expected value is the average you'd get if you could make the same bet many, many times. On one single bet, you'll almost never get exactly that amount.

Quick check

A coin flip: heads you win $10, tails you lose $5. What is the expected value?

Worked example: the start-up

OutcomeProbabilityYou get backProbability × outcome
Fails70%$0$0
Does OK25%$20,000$5,000
Big success5%$200,000$10,000
Total100%$15,000
Expected gain on $10,000+$5,000

A 50% expected gain, yet you lose everything 7 times in 10. Expected value says what a bet is worth on average. It says nothing about how risky it is. That's why investors also look at the spread, and why venture funds spread their money across many start-ups.

Fair bets, insurance and expected returns

A bet with an expected gain of zero is called fair. Insurance is deliberately unfair to the buyer. Example, with made-up numbers. A $1,000 phone has a 5% chance of being lost in a year, so the expected loss is $50. If cover costs $80, people still buy it, to swap a small, certain cost for protection against a big loss.

Analysts use the same idea for expected returns. If a share returns +30% in a boom (25% chance), +10% in a normal year (50%) and −20% in a bust (25%), its expected return is 0.25 × 30 + 0.5 × 10 + 0.25 × (−20) = 7.5%. Using the same weights, its standard deviation works out at about 17.9%.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Thinking the expected value is the likely outcome. In the start-up, $15,000 isn't even one of the possible results.
  • Judging by expected value alone. Two bets with the same expected value can carry very different risks.
  • Probabilities that don't add to 100%. List every outcome, including "nothing happens".

Remember

  • Expected value = sum of probability × outcome: the long-run average.
  • It's not the most likely result, and it doesn't measure risk.
  • A fair bet has an expected gain of zero. Insurance costs more than the expected loss, and buyers pay for the protection.

Check yourself

Three questions. Try them without scrolling up.

1. A trade has a 50% chance of making $100 and a 50% chance of losing $60. What is its expected value?

2. You lend $1,000 for a year at 10% interest. There's a 5% chance the borrower defaults, and you'd then recover $400. What do you expect to get back?

3. Insurance costs more than the expected loss. Why do sensible people still buy it?
