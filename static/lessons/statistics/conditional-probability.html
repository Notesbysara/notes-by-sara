Conditional probability

New information changes the odds. Conditional probability asks: how likely is this, given that we already know that? It's how lenders, insurers and analysts update their view.

Probability · Lesson 2 of 7 · 6 minutesBefore this: Probability basics

One missed payment

Example, with made-up numbers. A lender has 1,000 borrowers, and over a year 5% of them default. One borrower has just missed a payment. Is their chance of defaulting still 5%?

The idea

Follow all 1,000 borrowers. Split them first by whether they missed a payment, then by whether they defaulted:

50 borrowers default in all: 30 who had missed a payment and 20 who hadn't.

Once you know a borrower missed a payment, you only look at the 200 in that branch. Of them, 30 default: 30 ÷ 200 = 15%. Knowing about the missed payment has tripled the odds. That's a conditional probability, written with a bar that reads "given":

P(A | B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B)

P(default | missed) = 0.03 ÷ 0.20 = 0.15. "Given B" means: shrink the group to those where B happened, then count A inside it.

Quick check

Of 400 shares, 100 are tech shares, and 30 of those tech shares fell more than 10% this year. What is P(fell more than 10% | tech)?

Worked example: the full table

DefaultNo defaultTotal
Missed a payment30170200
No missed payment20780800
Total509501,000
P(default), knowing nothing50 ÷ 1,000 = 5%
P(default | missed)30 ÷ 200 = 15%
P(default | no missed payment)20 ÷ 800 = 2.5%
P(missed | default)30 ÷ 50 = 60%
Odds of default after a missed payment15%, not 5%

Two lessons from one table. First, the two events aren't independent: if they were, P(default | missed) would equal P(default). Second, the order matters. 60% of defaulters had missed a payment, but only 15% of those who missed one went on to default.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Flipping the condition. P(missed | default) and P(default | missed) answer different questions and are often far apart: 60% and 15% here.
  • Dividing by the wrong total. "Given B" means divide by the number where B happened, not by everyone.
  • Assuming independence. If knowing B changes the chance of A, the simple "multiply" rule from the last lesson doesn't apply.

Remember

  • P(A | B) is the chance of A once you know B happened: P(A and B) ÷ P(B).
  • If P(A | B) = P(A), the events are independent. If not, B tells you something about A.
  • P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different. Don't swap them.

Check yourself

Three questions. Try them without scrolling up.

1. P(A and B) = 0.06 and P(B) = 0.20. What is P(A | B)?

2. P(default) is 5%, and P(default | missed payment) is 15%. Are default and a missed payment independent?

3. A report says "80% of fraud cases involved a transaction abroad". What does that tell you?
