The lognormal distribution

A price can't fall below zero, but it can keep rising. The lognormal shape builds that in, and it's the standard model for stock prices.

Probability · Lesson 7 of 7 · 5 minutesBefore this: The normal distribution, The number e and continuous growth

Can you lose more than everything?

Example, with made-up numbers. A volatile stock's yearly return averages 10% with a standard deviation of 60%. Put that into the bell curve and ask for the chance of a return below −100%. The answer is about 3%: the curve says that 1 year in 30 the share ends up worth less than nothing. That can't happen. Shareholders can lose at most what they put in.

The idea

Prices move in proportions. A $10 share and a $1,000 share can both rise 5% in a day. So instead of treating the price change as normal, model the log return, ln(price ÷ today's price), as normal. Then the price itself is today's price × elog return, and e raised to any power is above zero. That price follows a lognormal distribution:

A wall at $0 on the left, a long tail on the right. The mean sits right of the median.

The shape is skewed to the right. Losses are limited to 100%, gains have no ceiling. That pulls the mean above the median: a few big winners lift the average, while the typical outcome stays lower.

Quick check

A share's price in a year is lognormal. Which is higher?

Worked example: a $100 share

Example, with made-up numbers. A share costs $100. Its log return over the next year is normal with mean 5% and standard deviation 30%. Two formulas do most of the work:

Median = today × emean  ·  Mean = today × emean + sd²/2
1. Median price100 × e0.05 = $105.13
2. Mean price100 × e0.05 + 0.045 = $109.97
3. 95% range: log return 5% ± 2 × 30%100 × e−0.55 to 100 × e0.65
4. Chance of ending below $100=NORM.DIST(0, 0.05, 0.30, TRUE)
95% range$58 to $192

The range isn't even: $42 down but $92 up. The chance of ending below $100 is 43%, even though the average price is about $110. Excel can also do step 4 in one go: =LOGNORM.DIST(100, LN(100) + 0.05, 0.30, TRUE).

Why finance uses it

Log returns add up over time, as you saw with continuous compounding. Add many small normal log returns and you get a normal total, so the price stays lognormal at any horizon: over t years the mean grows with t and the standard deviation with √t. This is the price model behind Black-Scholes and most option pricing. It still has the bell curve's weakness, though: daily log returns have fat tails too.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Treating the mean as the typical outcome. With a lognormal price, most paths end below the mean. The median is the typical outcome.
  • Mixing log and simple returns. A 30% log return is a 35% simple return (e0.30 − 1). Check which one a model wants.
  • Thinking it fixes fat tails. Lognormal fixes the floor at zero. It still underestimates crashes.

Remember

  • If log returns are normal, prices are lognormal: never below zero, with a long right tail.
  • Median = today × emean. Mean = today × emean + sd²/2, always higher.
  • It's the standard price model in option pricing, but it still misses fat tails.
