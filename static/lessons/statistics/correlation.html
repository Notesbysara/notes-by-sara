Correlation

Do two things move together? A scatter plot shows it, and one number from −1 to +1 measures it. It's the reason diversification works.

Relationships · Lesson 1 of 3 · 6 minutesBefore this: Variance and standard deviation

Does a second fund really spread the risk?

Example, with made-up numbers. You hold a stock fund and want to add a second one. Both swing by about 20% a year. If they rise and fall together, the second fund adds nothing. If they don't, your total risk drops. You need a way to measure how much two things move together.

The idea

Plot each month as a dot: one fund's return across, the other's up. That's a scatter plot. If the dots rise from left to right, the two tend to move together. The correlation, written r, sums up how tightly the dots hug a straight line:

+1 is a perfect rising line, −1 a perfect falling line, 0 no straight-line link at all.

Correlation ignores scale. It doesn't care whether returns are in percent or dollars, or how steep the line is. It only asks how consistently the two move together.

Quick check

Two series have a correlation of 0. Does that mean they're unrelated?

Worked example: a stock and the market

Example, with made-up numbers. Five months of returns, in %. Market: 2, −1, 3, 0, 1. Stock: 3, −2, 4, 1, −1. Both average 1%.

r = sum of (x gap × y gap) ÷ √(sum of x gaps² × sum of y gaps²)
1. Gaps from the mean, market1, −2, 2, −1, 0
2. Gaps from the mean, stock2, −3, 3, 0, −2
3. Multiply each pair and add2 + 6 + 6 + 0 + 0 = 14
4. Sum of squared gapsmarket 10, stock 26
5. Divide14 ÷ √(10 × 26) = 14 ÷ 16.12
Correlation+0.87

A strong positive link: the stock tended to be above its average in the same months as the market. In Excel: =CORREL(market range, stock range). Step 3 divided by 4 (one less than the number of months) is the covariance, 3.5. Correlation is covariance rescaled to sit between −1 and +1.

Why it matters: diversification

Example, with made-up numbers. Split your money 50/50 between two funds, each with a 20% standard deviation. The portfolio's risk depends on their correlation:

Correlation +1 (they move as one)20.0%
Correlation +0.517.3%
Correlation 014.1%
Correlation −1 (perfect opposites)0%

Same returns on average, less risk, as long as the correlation is below 1. One catch: in a crisis, correlations tend to jump toward 1, just when you need the protection most.

Correlation isn't causation

Two things can move together because a third thing drives both, or by pure chance. Ice cream sales and swimming accidents rise together because of hot weather, not because one causes the other. In finance, two stocks can be correlated simply because both depend on interest rates.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Reading it as causation. A high correlation says the two move together, not why.
  • Trusting it in a crisis. Correlations measured in calm years often rise sharply when markets fall.
  • Ignoring outliers. One extreme month can create or hide a correlation. Always look at the scatter plot too.

Remember

  • Correlation runs from −1 to +1 and measures how tightly two series follow a straight line.
  • Correlation = covariance ÷ (standard deviation of x × standard deviation of y). In Excel, =CORREL.
  • Lower correlation means more diversification, but correlations can jump in a crisis, and they never prove cause.
