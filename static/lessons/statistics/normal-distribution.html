The normal distribution

The bell curve is the most used shape in finance. Know a mean and a standard deviation, and it gives the chance of any range of outcomes. Just remember where it fails.

Probability · Lesson 6 of 7 · 6 minutesBefore this: Variance and standard deviation, Fat tails

How often will you lose money?

Example, with made-up numbers. A portfolio's yearly return averages 8% with a standard deviation of 15%. If returns roughly follow a bell curve, how often should you expect a losing year?

The idea

The normal distribution is a smooth, symmetric bell curve. Its whole shape is set by two numbers: the mean (where the middle is) and the standard deviation (how wide it spreads). The chance of any range is the area under the curve over that range:

The shaded area left of 0% is the chance of a losing year: about 30%.

Within 1 standard deviation of the meanabout 68%
Within 2 standard deviationsabout 95%
Within 3 standard deviationsabout 99.7%

This is where the "2 years in 3" rule of thumb from the standard deviation lesson comes from.

Quick check

For normal data, what share lies more than 2 standard deviations below the mean?

Worked example: a losing year

To use the curve for any value, first ask how many standard deviations it sits from the mean. That's its z-score:

z = (value − mean) ÷ standard deviation
1. How far is 0% from the 8% mean?0 − 8 = −8 points
2. In standard deviationsz = −8 ÷ 15 = −0.53
3. Area left of z = −0.53=NORM.DIST(0, 8, 15, TRUE)
Chance of a losing year29.7%

About 3 years in 10. A year worse than −22% (2 standard deviations down) should come about once in 44 years. Real markets do worse than that, because of the fat tails you met earlier. Use the bell curve for ordinary years, not for crashes.

Why it shows up so often

When an outcome is the sum of many small, independent effects, it tends toward a bell curve. That's why averages, measurement errors and long-run returns often look normal, and why the curve is a sensible starting point. It works less well for single daily returns (fat tails), for incomes (skewed) and for prices, which can't fall below zero. The next lesson deals with that last one.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Using it for extremes. For crashes and daily moves, the bell curve badly underestimates how often extremes happen.
  • Mixing one tail and two. "95% within 2 standard deviations" leaves 2.5% in each tail, not 5% in one.
  • Forgetting to convert to a z-score. The 68–95–99.7 rule is in standard deviations, so first work out how many standard deviations away a value is.

Remember

  • The normal distribution is set by its mean and standard deviation. Area under the curve is probability.
  • 68% within 1 standard deviation, 95% within 2, 99.7% within 3.
  • z = (value − mean) ÷ standard deviation. Fine for ordinary outcomes, risky for extremes.
