Misleading charts

A chart can be accurate in every number and still give the wrong impression. A few simple checks catch most tricks, whether they're on purpose or by accident.

Describing data · Lesson 8 of 8 · 5 minutesBefore this: Histograms and box plots

Did revenue soar?

Example, with made-up numbers. A company's annual report shows two bars: revenue of $100 million last year and $102 million this year. In the chart, this year's bar is three times as tall as last year's. Did revenue really jump?

The idea

Revenue grew 2%. The bar looks three times taller because the chart's axis starts at $99 million instead of zero, so only the top sliver of each bar shows:

Same two numbers. On the left the axis starts at zero, and on the right it starts at $99 million.

Cutting the axis is only one trick. Before you trust a chart, run through these five checks:

  1. Where does the axis start? Bars must start at zero, because their length is the message. Line charts can start higher, but check the scale.
  2. Why this time period? A start date picked at a low point makes any recovery look like strong growth.
  3. Is everything on the same scale? With two vertical axes, almost any two lines can be made to look like they move together.
  4. What's left out? A chart of "funds that beat the market" may only include funds that survived. This is called survivorship bias.
  5. Do the sizes match the numbers? Pictures and 3D shapes that grow in two directions make a doubling look like four times as much.

Quick check

Which kind of chart must always start its axis at zero?

Worked example: the chosen start date

Example, with made-up numbers. A fund's value over ten years was 100, 120, 135, 90, 95, 105, 118, 125, 132 and 140. Its advert says "up 56%!", measured from the low in year 4.

From the year-4 low: 90 → 140, over 6 years+56% · 7.6% a year
Over the full ten years: 100 → 140, over 9 years+40% · 3.8% a year
Yearly return, depending on the start date7.6% vs 3.8%

Both numbers are true. The advert's start date doubles the yearly return. Always ask: why does the chart start there, and what happened just before?

Try it yourself
Move the axis start and watch the bars change.

Common mistakes
Three slips readers make.
  • Comparing bar heights without checking the axis. Read the numbers on the axis before trusting what the shapes say.
  • Assuming bad intent. Most misleading charts are made by software defaults or in a hurry. Check the chart, then judge the maker.
  • Forgetting what isn't shown. Funds that closed, companies that went bust and years outside the window can change the story.

Remember

  • Bars must start at zero. A cut axis makes small changes look huge.
  • Ask why a chart starts where it does. A well-chosen start date can double a return.
  • Check scales, missing data and survivorship before trusting the picture.

Check yourself

Three questions. Try them without scrolling up.

1. A bar chart shows values of 92 and 96 with the axis starting at 90. How much taller does the second bar look, and how much bigger is it really?

2. An advert says a fund is "up 60% since March 2020". What's the first question to ask?

3. A chart shows that the average fund still running today beat the market over 20 years. What might be missing?

  1. Five salaries, in $ thousands: 3, 5, 5, 6, 21. What are the mean and the median, and which better describes a typical salary?

    Show answer
    Mean, median and modeMean 40 ÷ 5 = 8, median 5. The 21 pulls the mean up, so the median describes a typical salary better.

  2. A fund returns +25%, then −20%. What are the simple average and the geometric mean return?

    Show answer
    Averaging returnsSimple average 2.5%. Geometric: (1.25 × 0.80)1/2 − 1 = 0%. You ended exactly where you started.

  3. $10,000 grows to $14,641 over 4 years. What is the yearly growth rate?

    Show answer
    Averaging returns1.46411/4 − 1 = 10% a year.

  4. For 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 40, find the range and the interquartile range.

    Show answer
    Range and percentilesRange 40 − 2 = 38. 25th percentile: the median of 2, 4, 6, 8 is 5. 75th: the median of 10, 12, 14, 40 is 13. Interquartile range 8.

  5. Is the 40 in the last problem an outlier on a box plot?

    Show answer
    Histograms and box plotsUpper fence: 13 + 1.5 × 8 = 25. 40 is beyond it, so yes.

  6. What is the standard deviation of 4, 8, 12, treated as a sample?

    Show answer
    Variance and standard deviationMean 8. Squared distances 16, 0, 16 sum to 32. Variance 32 ÷ 2 = 16. Standard deviation 4.

  7. A fund averages 7% a year with a standard deviation of 12%. What's a typical year's range?

    Show answer
    Variance and standard deviation7 ± 12: about −5% to +19%, where roughly 2 years in 3 should land.

  8. Fund A: mean 5%, standard deviation 10%. Fund B: mean 10%, standard deviation 15%. Which has less risk per unit of return?

    Show answer
    Variance and standard deviationA: 10 ÷ 5 = 2. B: 15 ÷ 10 = 1.5. Fund B, despite its bigger swings.

  9. A strategy's mean monthly return is −0.4% and its median is +0.8%. Its excess kurtosis is 7. Describe its shape.

    Show answer
    Skewness and fat tailsMean below median: negative skew, rare big losses. Kurtosis of 7: fat tails, extreme months far more often than the bell curve expects.

  10. Profits of $50m and $55m are drawn as bars, with the axis starting at $45m. How many times taller does the second bar look, and how much bigger is it?

    Show answer
    Misleading chartsAbove the axis they're 5 and 10 tall, so it looks twice as big. Really 55 ÷ 50 = 1.10: 10% bigger.
