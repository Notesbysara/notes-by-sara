Fat tails

The bell curve says extreme days almost never happen. Markets disagree. Data with fat tails has far more extreme values than the bell curve expects, and finance is full of it.

Describing data · Lesson 6 of 8 · 5 minutesBefore this: Variance and standard deviation, Skewness

A once-in-14,000-years day

If daily stock returns followed the bell curve, a fall of 5 standard deviations in one day would come about once every 14,000 years of trading. Yet on October 19, 1987, the US stock market fell 22.6% in a single day, a move of far more than 5 standard deviations by the measures of the time. Was 1987 just very bad luck, or is the bell curve wrong for markets?

The idea

The bell curve (statisticians call it the normal distribution) has thin tails: values far from the average are extremely rare. Real market returns have fat tails. With the same standard deviation, they have more quiet days in the middle and more extreme days at the edges:

Left: same standard deviation, different shape. Right: zooming into the edge, the bell curve's tail all but vanishes, while the fat tail stays.

The measure is called kurtosis. A spreadsheet's =KURT() gives the excess kurtosis: 0 for the bell curve, above 0 for fat tails. Daily stock returns typically score well above 0.

The fat-tailed example is a statistician's Student's t curve, chosen to show the effect. Real markets vary, but the lesson holds: a model that assumes the bell curve says disasters are far rarer than they are.

Why it matters

Many risk tools, like a simple Value at Risk, start from the bell curve. With fat tails, they understate how bad the bad days get, which is part of why so many models were caught out in 2008. The rule of thumb from Variance and standard deviation, about 19 years in 20 within 2 standard deviations, is fine for the middle of the data. For the extremes, don't trust it.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Thinking fat tails just means a high standard deviation. Two data sets can share a standard deviation and have very different tails.
  • Mixing up kurtosis and skewness. Skewness is the direction. Kurtosis is the weight of both tails.
  • Trusting the bell curve for disasters. It works for ordinary days and badly underestimates extreme ones.

Remember

  • Fat tails: more extreme values than the bell curve expects, with the same standard deviation.
  • Kurtosis measures it. Excess kurtosis is 0 for the bell curve and above 0 for fat tails.
  • Market returns have fat tails, so bell-curve risk models understate crashes.
