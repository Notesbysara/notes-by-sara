Samples and standard error

Every average you calculate from data is an estimate. The standard error says how far off it could easily be, and it's often much further than people expect.

Inference · Lesson 1 of 4 · 6 minutesBefore this: Variance and standard deviation, The normal distribution

Skill or luck?

Example, with made-up numbers. A fund manager has averaged 12% a year over 5 years. The market averaged 8%. Her returns swing with a standard deviation of 20% a year. Is that 4-point lead good evidence of skill, or could a manager with no skill easily do the same?

The idea

Five years of returns are a sample: a few draws from all the returns the manager could have had. Another five years would give a different average. If you could repeat the five years many times, the averages would spread out around the true value. The standard deviation of that spread is the standard error:

Standard error = standard deviation ÷ √n

Averages spread less than single years, and less again the more years you have. Here the true mean is 8%.

Averages spread less because highs and lows partly cancel out. And whatever shape single returns have, averages of many tend toward a bell curve. That's the central limit theorem, and it's why the normal curve keeps showing up in statistics.

Quick check

Values have a standard deviation of 30. What's the standard error of an average of 100 of them?

Worked example: the manager's record

1. Standard error of a 5-year average20 ÷ √5 = 8.9%
2. Rough range for the true average: ± 2 standard errors12 ± 17.9 = −5.9% to 29.9%
3. The lead over the market, in standard errors4 ÷ 8.9 = 0.45
4. Chance a no-skill manager (true average 8%) averages 12% or more=1 − NORM.DIST(12, 8, 8.9, TRUE)
No-skill managers who'd look this goodabout 1 in 3

The 4-point lead is less than half a standard error, so it's weak evidence of skill. Even with 25 years, the standard error is still 20 ÷ √25 = 4%, and about 1 in 6 no-skill managers would average 12% or more.

Why it matters in finance

Average returns are among the hardest things in finance to pin down. Volatility is large compared with the average, so the standard error stays big for decades. That's why short track records, a great backtest or a "best fund of the year" list tell you less than they seem to. And because of the √n, halving the standard error takes four times as much data.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Mixing up standard deviation and standard error. Standard deviation is the spread of single values. Standard error is the spread of the average.
  • Thinking daily data fixes it. Slicing the same 5 years into days barely helps estimate the average return. What counts is how many years you have.
  • Forgetting the sample can be biased. A standard error assumes a fair sample. Looking only at funds that survived isn't one (survivorship bias).

Remember

  • An average from a sample is an estimate. Its standard error = standard deviation ÷ √n.
  • Roughly 95% of sample averages land within 2 standard errors of the true value.
  • Average returns are noisy: short track records prove little, and halving the error takes 4 times the data.
