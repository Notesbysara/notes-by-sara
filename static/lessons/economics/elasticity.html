Elasticity

How strongly buyers react to a change in price, what makes demand sensitive or insensitive, and why it decides whether a price rise brings in more money or less.

Markets and prices · Lesson 2 of 3 · 6 minutesBefore this: Supply and demand, Percentages and percentage change

In plain words

Some things people keep buying even when the price goes up, like medicine or gas for the commute. Other things they drop quickly, like one brand of cereal when another is cheaper. Elasticity measures how much buying changes when the price changes.

Governments tax cigarettes heavily, and decades of research show how smokers respond to higher prices:

+10%rise in the price of cigarettes
→
−3% to −5%fall in overall cigarette consumption, according to most studies

Source: US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

Consumption falls, but by less than the price rises. That is why a cigarette tax can cut smoking and raise more tax revenue at the same time. Whether a price rise brings in more money or less depends entirely on how sensitive buyers are.

Price elasticity of demand compares the two percentage changes:

Elasticity = % change in quantity% change in price

For cigarettes, −4% ÷ 10% = −0.4. The sign is almost always negative, so economists usually talk about its size:

Inelastic: below 1Buying changes less than price. Cigarettes, gasoline in the short run, essential medicines.
Unit elastic: exactly 1Buying changes by the same percentage as price.
Elastic: above 1Buying changes more than price. One brand among many close rivals, holidays, restaurant meals.

Four things make demand more elastic:

Close substitutesIf an almost identical alternative exists, buyers switch easily.
Luxury, not necessityWhat people can do without, they drop when it gets dearer.
A big share of the budgetA 10% rise on a car is noticed; on a box of matches, not.
TimeGiven time, people find alternatives, so demand becomes more elastic in the long run.

Time matters a great deal. For cigarettes, researchers estimate that the long-run effect of a lasting price rise is about double the short-run effect, as fewer people take up smoking and more quit over the years.

Source: US National Cancer Institute, Monograph 14.

A tax raises the price of cigarettes by 10%, and consumption falls 4%. What happens to the total amount spent on cigarettes?

Elasticity decides what a price rise does to revenue. Revenue is price times quantity, so it rises when demand is inelastic and falls when demand is elastic. Two products, both raising prices by 10% (illustrative figures):

Essential medicine−0.4−4%+5.6%
One brand of soft drink−2.0−20%−12.0%

Elasticity works for small changes. For large price moves, buyers' responses usually change along the way, so treat the result as a rough guide.

This is what investors mean by pricing power. A company whose customers barely respond to price can pass on rising costs and protect its margins. One whose customers leave at the first increase cannot. During inflation, that difference shows up quickly in profits.

How professionals use it

Equity analysts
Judge a company's pricing power by how its sales volumes respond when it raises prices.
Governments
Forecast tax revenue, and choose to tax goods with inelastic demand when the aim is revenue.
Airlines and hotels
Charge business travelers, whose demand is inelastic, more than leisure travelers who will shop around.
Pricing teams
Test price changes on small groups of customers to measure elasticity before changing prices everywhere.

In everyday terms

Last-minute flights cost more because people who must travel tomorrow have few alternatives. Booking ahead puts you in the elastic group, where sellers compete for you.

The more alternatives you have, the less a seller can charge you.

Elasticity shows who has the power to set the price.
