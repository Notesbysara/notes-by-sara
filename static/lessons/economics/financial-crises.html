Financial crises

Why banks are fragile, the four main kinds of crisis and what they have in common, how crises spread, what they cost, and the tools governments and central banks use to stop them.

Money and policy · Lesson 5 of 5 · 10 minutesBefore this: Exchange rates

In plain words

A financial crisis is when trust in the financial system breaks down suddenly. People rush to get their money out, lenders stop lending, and prices of assets or currencies collapse. Because everyone acts at once, a problem that could have been managed becomes much bigger.

Silicon Valley Bank, the 16th largest bank in the US, failed in March 2023 in less than two days:

$42 billionwithdrawn on Thursday, March 9, nearly a quarter of its deposits. The previous largest US bank run, at Washington Mutual in 2008, was $16.7 billion over ten days
and
About 90%of its deposits were above the $250,000 insurance limit, among the highest shares of any US bank, so its depositors had good reason to run

Sources: S&P Global Market Intelligence; Axios; AP, via CBC.

Regulators closed the bank the next morning. At the time it was the second largest bank failure in US history; First Republic Bank's failure that May was larger. Its story contains most of what this lesson is about: a mismatch between what a bank owes and what it owns, a loss of confidence, a run that fed on itself, and a government rescue that raised questions of its own.

Banks are built to be fragile. A bank takes deposits that customers can withdraw any day and uses them to make loans and buy bonds that last for years. This is called maturity mismatch, and it is useful: it turns savings into long-term lending. But it means no bank can repay all its depositors at once. A bank can be solvent, owning more than it owes, and still be illiquid, unable to turn its assets into cash fast enough.

A bank owes depositors $100 million. Its loans and bonds are worth $110 million if held to maturity, but would fetch only $80 million if sold this week. Depositors ask for $60 million back at once. What happens?

That is close to what happened at SVB. It had put much of its customers' deposits into long-term bonds while interest rates were low. As rates rose in 2022, those bonds fell in value, as bond prices do when rates rise. In March 2023 it sold $21 billion of them at a loss of nearly $2 billion and announced it needed to raise more capital. Depositors, many of them tech start-ups, read this as a warning. Prominent investors urged companies to pull their money, and much of it left online within hours.

Sources: Wikipedia, Collapse of Silicon Valley Bank; Fortune; Money Crashers.

Crises come in four main kinds, and they often arrive together:

Banking crisisRuns, bank failures and heavy losses across the banking system, so lending dries up. Examples: the US in 2008, Japan in the 1990s.
Currency crisisA sudden, large fall in a currency, often when a peg breaks. Example: Thailand and its neighbors in 1997.
Sovereign debt crisisA government cannot pay its debts on the original terms and defaults or restructures. Examples: Argentina in 2001, Greece in 2012.
Asset bubble burstingPrices of shares or property rise far above what their income can justify, then collapse, leaving lenders with bad loans. Example: Japan after 1989.

What they share is borrowing that can be pulled away quickly. Most crises build up in good times through a few familiar patterns: rapid growth in credit, rising asset prices that seem to justify more borrowing, heavy reliance on short-term funding, and borrowing in a foreign currency while earning in your own. The longer things go well, the more people believe the old risks no longer apply. The economists Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff named their history of eight centuries of crises after that belief: This Time Is Different.

Leverage turns losses into failures. A firm that funds itself mostly with borrowed money has little cushion. When Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy on September 15, 2008, it reported $639 billion in assets, the largest bankruptcy in US history. It had been running on roughly $30 of assets for every $1 of its own capital, so a fall of just over 3% in the value of its assets was enough to wipe out its capital.

Sources: Britannica; Wikipedia, Bankruptcy of Lehman Brothers.

Crises spread through connections and fear. Lehman owed money to banks, funds and companies around the world, and nobody knew who was exposed to whom. So lenders stopped lending to almost everyone. This spreading is called contagion. It also works through fire sales, where one firm's forced selling lowers prices for everyone holding the same assets, and through simple panic: when one bank fails, depositors at similar banks start to wonder.

The 1997 Asian crisis shows a currency crisis at work. Thailand had held the baht at about 25 to the US dollar, and its banks and companies had borrowed heavily in dollars. When investors began to doubt the peg, the central bank spent its reserves defending it until it could not, and on July 2, 1997, it let the baht float. By January 1998 the baht had lost 56% of its value against the dollar and Indonesia's rupiah 81%. Dollar debts suddenly cost twice as much or more to repay in local money. Thailand, Indonesia and South Korea turned to the IMF for emergency loans, and stock markets in Thailand, Malaysia and Indonesia lost more than three-quarters of their value by the end of 1997.

Sources: Goldman Sachs history; Bank of Thailand; CNBC.

Governments can be borrowers in trouble too. In March 2012 Greece restructured about €200 billion of bonds held by private investors, the largest sovereign default in recorded history. Bondholders accepted a cut of 53.5% in the face value of their bonds, reducing Greece's debt by about €107 billion. The previous record was Argentina's default beginning in late 2001, on about $80 billion of bonds.

Sources: European Stability Mechanism; Porzecanski, American University.

Bubbles can take decades to recover from. Japan's Nikkei stock index closed at 38,915.87 on December 29, 1989, at the height of a boom in shares and land. After the bubble burst, Japan's banks eventually wrote off about 100 trillion yen of bad loans. The index did not close above its 1989 record until February 22, 2024, 34 years later, a decade longer than Wall Street took to recover from the 1929 crash.

Sources: Nippon.com; AP, via The Boston Globe; Reuters, via Yahoo Finance.

The costs are large and long-lasting. IMF researchers Luc Laeven and Fabián Valencia have recorded 151 systemic banking crises around the world between 1970 and 2017. Comparing types, they find that sovereign debt crises tend to cost more lost output than banking crises, and banking crises more than currency crises. Output often stays below its old path for years, which is the gap between "recovery" and "catching up" from the business cycle lesson.

Sources: Laeven and Valencia, IMF, 2018; Laeven and Valencia, IMF Economic Review, 2013; Cecchetti, Kohler and Upper, NBER, 2009.

Policymakers have four main tools to stop a crisis:

Lender of last resortThe central bank lends to banks that are solvent but short of cash. The classic rule, from Walter Bagehot's 1873 book Lombard Street: lend freely, at a high rate, against good collateral.
Deposit insuranceA guarantee that small deposits are safe removes the reason to run. The US created it in 1933; it now covers $250,000 per depositor per bank, and no depositor has lost insured money since it began in 1934.
Rescues and restructuringGovernments inject capital into banks, guarantee debts, or wind failed firms down in an orderly way. Countries in trouble can borrow from the IMF, usually on strict conditions.
Rules that prevent the next oneAfter 2008, regulators worldwide required banks to hold more capital and more easy-to-sell assets, under rules known as Basel III.

Sources: FDIC; Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond, on Bagehot; Bank for International Settlements, Basel III.

Every rescue creates a dilemma, called moral hazard. If people expect to be rescued, they take more risk. When SVB failed, US authorities chose to protect all its depositors, including those above the $250,000 limit, to stop runs spreading to other banks. That likely prevented a wider panic, but it also told large depositors that the limit might not apply when it matters. Each crisis is partly a test of how to stop the panic now without encouraging the next one.

How professionals use it

Bank analysts and regulators
Check how much of a bank's funding could leave quickly, how much is uninsured, and how much its assets would lose if sold in a hurry.
Credit analysts
Watch for the build-up signs: fast credit growth, short-term or foreign-currency borrowing, and borrowers who can only pay if prices keep rising.
Portfolio managers
Plan for the fact that in a crisis, assets that normally move independently tend to fall together, and cash becomes scarce.
Sovereign debt investors
Track a country's debt, foreign reserves, and how much of its borrowing is short-term or in foreign currency.

Crises rarely start with something new. They start with familiar risks that people decided no longer applied.
