The business cycle

The pattern of expansions and recessions, who decides when a recession starts, why it is only known afterward, and how forecasters try to see turns coming.

The economy · Lesson 3 of 3 · 7 minutesBefore this: Unemployment

In plain words

The economy doesn't grow in a straight line. It grows for a while, reaches a high point, shrinks for a while, hits a low point, then grows again. That repeating pattern is the business cycle. The shrinking part is a recession.

The last US cycle held two records at once:

128 monthsThe expansion from June 2009 to February 2020, the longest in US records going back to 1854
then
2 monthsThe recession that ended it, from February to April 2020, the shortest on record

Source: NBER Business Cycle Dating Committee; NBER chronology.

Neither length is typical, but together they show the basic shape. Expansions usually last years; recessions usually last months. And the economy does not climb back to the same place: each cycle sits on a long-run trend that rises over time.

Every cycle has four phases. An expansion runs from a low point, the trough, up to a high point, the peak. A contraction, or recession, runs from the peak down to the next trough.

A stylized cycle. Real cycles are irregular: no two expansions or recessions have the same length or depth.

In the US, recessions are dated by a committee, not a formula. The Business Cycle Dating Committee of the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), a private research group, keeps the official record of peaks and troughs. Its definition: a recession is a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months. That gives three tests, depth, breadth and length, and a very severe decline on one test can make up for a weaker showing on another. The 2020 recession was extremely short, but the fall was so deep and so widespread that it qualified.

The committee looks at many monthly measures rather than one: payroll jobs, real income, real consumer spending, factory output and sales, along with GDP.

Other economies date their cycles in their own ways. The euro area has had a similar committee since 2003, run by the Centre for Economic Policy Research (CEPR). It dates turning points by quarter rather than by month, because Europe's quarterly data are more reliable. Many countries, Australia among them, simply use the two-quarter rule described below.

A common rule of thumb says a recession means GDP falls for two quarters in a row. When the 2007 to 2009 US recession began, how long did it take for that to happen?

The rule and a committee can give different answers. In 2012, when official figures showed euro area GDP falling for a second straight quarter, the CEPR committee concluded that the recession had in fact begun earlier, after a peak in the third quarter of 2011. Australia, which does go by the rule, went almost 29 years without a recession, the longest run in the developed world, until GDP fell 0.3% and then 7.0% in the first two quarters of 2020.

Sources: Bruegel, on the CEPR committee; Bloomberg; Economic Papers, 2021.

A recession is only dated after it has started, often long after. The committee waits until the data are clear enough that it won't need to revise them. The December 2007 peak was announced on December 1, 2008, twelve months later, by which point the recession was already a year old. So people who say "we're not in a recession" are often only saying it hasn't been declared yet.

Forecasters sort economic data by when it tends to turn, relative to the cycle:

LeadingTend to turn before the economy does. Examples: building permits, new claims for unemployment benefits, the gap between long- and short-term interest rates, stock prices. The Conference Board combines ten of these into its Leading Economic Index.
CoincidentMove with the economy and show where it is now. Examples: payroll jobs, personal income, manufacturing and trade sales, industrial production.
LaggingTurn after the economy does. The unemployment rate is the best known: it often keeps rising after the trough. In 2009, payroll jobs bottomed eight months after the recession ended.

Some governments turn the indicators into a single signal. Taiwan's National Development Council scores nine indicators each month, including exports, industrial production, jobs and stock prices, and shows the result as a traffic light:

BlueRecession
Yellow-blueSluggish
GreenSteady growth
Yellow-redSlight boom
RedBoom

The signal can swing quickly. It showed blue in October 2023, then red in late 2025 and early 2026, as firms gained from strong demand related to artificial intelligence.

Sources: NDC, October 2023; NDC, December 2025; Taipei Times.

Leading indicators can still be wrong. In late 2022, with the Leading Economic Index falling and the yield curve inverted, meaning short-term interest rates were above long-term ones, the Conference Board forecast a US recession starting around the end of 2022 and lasting through mid-2023. It did not come: the economy kept growing, and by early 2024 the Conference Board said its index no longer signaled a recession. A Wall Street saying captures the problem: the yield curve has predicted eight of the last five recessions.

Sources: The Conference Board, November 2022; Investing.com, quoting the Conference Board, February 2024; Marketplace, 2023.

Cycles have several causes, and most downturns involve more than one:

Demand shocksHouseholds and firms spend less, because confidence falls, credit tightens or interest rates rise. Output and inflation both tend to fall.
Supply shocksSomething cuts what the economy can produce, such as an oil price spike, a pandemic or a disaster. Output can fall while prices rise, as in the 1970s.
Credit cyclesBorrowing grows fast in good times and is cut back sharply in bad ones, deepening both the boom and the bust. The lesson on financial crises covers this.

Governments lean against the cycle with fiscal policy, meaning taxes and spending, alongside the central bank's interest rate. Part of it is automatic. In a downturn, people pay less income tax as their incomes fall and more of them qualify for unemployment benefits, so the government budget cushions the fall without any new law. These are called automatic stabilizers. Governments can also act on purpose, with tax cuts, payments to households or spending programs, as the US did with the CARES Act in 2020. Such measures can be large, but they take time to pass and to reach people, while automatic stabilizers start working at once.

Sources: US Government Accountability Office; Econofact; Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, on the 1970s.

How professionals use it

Investors
Shift between cyclical businesses, such as carmakers, airlines and builders, whose profits swing with the cycle, and defensive ones, such as food and utilities, whose sales hold up in a recession.
Lenders
Test whether a borrower could still pay if revenue fell as it did in a past recession, because defaults cluster in downturns.
Companies
Plan hiring, inventory and borrowing so that a downturn doesn't leave them with too much stock or too much debt.
Central banks
Lean against the cycle, cutting interest rates in a downturn and raising them when the economy overheats.

You can't time the cycle precisely, but you can plan for it, because a downturn always comes.
