Inflation

How inflation is measured, what causes it, what happens at the extremes of hyperinflation and deflation, and who gains and loses when prices rise.

Money and policy · Lesson 3 of 5 · 7 minutesBefore this: Central banks and interest rates

In plain words

Inflation is when prices in general go up, so the same amount of money buys less. A little inflation is normal. A lot of it eats into savings and wages, and makes planning hard. Falling prices, called deflation, cause their own problems.

In 2022, after the pandemic and Russia's invasion of Ukraine, inflation hit levels much of the world had not seen in four decades:

9.1%United States, June 2022, the highest since 1981
11.1%United Kingdom, October 2022, the highest in 41 years
10.6%Euro area, October 2022, a record since the euro began

Sources: US Bureau of Labor Statistics; Office for National Statistics, via Al Jazeera; Eurostat.

These numbers are averages. In the US that June, energy prices were up 41.6% on a year earlier and food 10.4%, so households that spent a large share of their budget on fuel and groceries felt far more than 9.1%.

Inflation is measured with a basket of goods and services. Statisticians price a fixed list of what typical households buy, from rent and groceries to haircuts and car insurance, and track its cost over time. In the US, the Bureau of Labor Statistics collects about 80,000 prices a month. Each item is weighted by how much households spend on it, so rent counts for far more than matches. The result is the consumer price index (CPI), and the inflation rate is its change over a year:

Inflation = CPI this year − CPI last yearCPI last year

Central banks also watch core inflation, which leaves out food and energy because their prices jump around with harvests and oil markets. In June 2022, US core inflation was 5.9%, well below the headline 9.1%. Core inflation is a better guide to where prices are heading; headline inflation is what people actually pay.

Different price indexes give different answers. The US Federal Reserve's 2% target, set in 2012, is measured with the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index rather than the CPI. The PCE index covers more spending, including health care paid for by employers and governments on households' behalf, and its weights update faster as people switch between products. CPI inflation tends to run a little higher: since 2000, by about 0.4 percentage points a year on average.

Sources: Federal Reserve; Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland.

Inflation compounds against you. Try it: see what a sum of money will buy in the future at a steady rate of inflation.

Buys what buys today

Your savings account pays 2% a year. Inflation is 5%. After a year, compared with today, can you buy more or less with your savings?

Inflation has three main causes, and they often act together:

Demand-pullPeople and firms try to buy more than the economy can produce, so sellers raise prices. Often follows easy credit or large government spending.
Cost-pushCosts of production jump, such as energy, raw materials or wages, and firms pass them on. A supply shock like an oil price spike works this way.
ExpectationsIf people expect high inflation, workers ask for bigger raises and firms raise prices in advance, which makes the expectation come true.

Underneath all three is money. Prices cannot keep rising for long unless the amount of money and credit grows to pay for them, which is why the central bank's interest rate is the main defense, as in the last lesson.

Because expectations matter so much, they are measured closely. Surveys ask households, businesses and economists what inflation they expect. Markets give a second reading: the difference between the yield on an ordinary government bond and an inflation-protected bond of the same maturity, called the breakeven inflation rate, is roughly the inflation investors are pricing in. In the US, the five-year breakeven averaged close to 2% in the years before COVID-19.

Inflation and unemployment have tended to move in opposite directions, a pattern called the Phillips curve, after the economist A. W. Phillips, who found it in British data from 1861 to 1957. When jobs are plentiful, wages and prices tend to rise faster. The 1970s showed that this is not a stable trade-off: once people expect high inflation, or an oil shock hits, inflation and unemployment can rise together. In recent decades the relationship has looked weaker, which economists call a flattening of the curve.

Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, FRED Blog; Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis; Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco.

At the extreme is hyperinflation. Zimbabwe's government financed itself by printing money, and by November 2008 prices were rising at an estimated 79.6 billion percent a month, doubling roughly every day. The central bank issued a 100 trillion dollar note. In the end people simply refused to use the Zimbabwe dollar, and in 2009 it was abandoned in favor of foreign currencies such as the US dollar.

Sources: Johns Hopkins Magazine, on Steve Hanke's estimate; Cato Institute; Hanke, "The Printing Press", Forbes, 2008; Hanke and Bostrom, Johns Hopkins.

The opposite problem is deflation. Japan's consumer prices fell slightly on average from 1998 to 2012, by about 0.3% a year. That sounds harmless, but it lasted 15 years. When prices are expected to fall, people put off purchases, debts become heavier in real terms, and a central bank cannot push interest rates much below zero to help. Japanese firms got so used to stable prices that leaving prices unchanged became the norm. In 2013 the Bank of Japan adopted a 2% inflation target to break the pattern.

Sources: Bank of Japan, speech by Governor Kuroda, 2014; Watanabe, University of Tokyo.

Inflation moves wealth from some people to others:

Lose: holders of cash and low-rate savingsTheir money buys less each year unless it earns more than inflation.
Gain: borrowers at fixed ratesThey repay with money that is worth less than when they borrowed it.
Lose: people on fixed incomesPensions and contracts not linked to prices lose value, as do bonds with fixed payments.
Protected, partly: indexed assetsInflation-linked bonds raise their payments with prices. Shares and property tend to keep up over long periods, but not reliably year to year.

How professionals use it

Bond investors
Demand yields above expected inflation, and compare ordinary and inflation-linked bonds to see what inflation the market expects.
Equity analysts
Ask which companies can raise prices as fast as their costs rise, the pricing power from the elasticity lesson.
Financial planners
Plan in real terms, so a retirement income target keeps its buying power over decades.
Wage and contract negotiators
Link pay, rents and long contracts to a price index so neither side loses to inflation.

What matters is not how much money you have, but what it will buy.
