Exchange rates

How to read a currency quote, what moves exchange rates in the short and long run, how forward rates and carry trades work, the choice between floating and pegged currencies, and what currency moves do to companies and investors.

Money and policy · Lesson 4 of 5 · 9 minutesBefore this: Inflation

In plain words

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. Like any price, it moves with supply and demand: when more people want a currency, it gets stronger. A stronger currency makes imports and foreign trips cheaper, but makes a country's exports dearer for foreign buyers.

In the first week of May 2025, two neighboring currencies showed two different ways of handling the same pressure:

+8%The Taiwan dollar against the US dollar in two days, its biggest surge since 1988
while
HK$7.75The limit of Hong Kong's peg. Its monetary authority bought a record US$7.8 billion in one day to stop its dollar rising further

Sources: Bloomberg; Reuters, via The Nation; TheStreet.

Taiwan's exporters, holding US dollars they had earned, rushed to convert them, and its life insurers, which hold large US dollar investments, scrambled to protect themselves against a falling dollar. Shares of exporters such as TSMC fell on worries about their earnings in Taiwan dollars. Hong Kong, whose currency is tied to the US dollar, absorbed the same wave by creating Hong Kong dollars on demand. Who wins and loses from a currency move depends on which currency your income, costs, assets and debts are in.

Reading a quote. An exchange rate is written as a pair. In USD/TWD = 30.145, the first currency, the base, is one unit, and the number is how many units of the second it buys: one US dollar buys 30.145 Taiwan dollars. Turn it around and one Taiwan dollar buys 1 ÷ 30.145 ≈ 0.0332 US dollars. If USD/TWD falls to 29, each US dollar buys fewer Taiwan dollars: the Taiwan dollar has appreciated (strengthened) and the US dollar has depreciated (weakened). Always check which currency is the base before deciding whether a rising number is good or bad news.

Two rates against a common currency give a third, called a cross rate. If one euro buys 1.10 US dollars and one US dollar buys 150 yen, then one euro buys 1.10 × 150 = 165 yen (illustrative figures). Many currency pairs are in fact traded through the US dollar this way, which is one reason it appears in most trades.

The currency market is the largest market in the world. In April 2025, trading averaged $9.6 trillion a day, and the US dollar was on one side of 89% of all trades. The euro came second, at 28.9%, and the yen third, at 16.8%. Because every trade involves two currencies, these shares add up to 200%. Trades are either spot, settled within about two days at today's rate, or for a later date at a rate agreed now, through forwards and swaps.

Source: Bank for International Settlements, Triennial Survey 2025.

In the short run, four forces move exchange rates:

TradeExporters sell foreign currency for their own, pushing their currency up. Importers do the opposite. A country with a large trade surplus has steady demand for its currency.
Investment flowsForeign investors must buy a currency to buy that country's shares, bonds or companies. When they sell and leave, the currency weakens.
Interest ratesHigher rates attract savers and investors, which tends to strengthen a currency. Changes in expected rates move currencies as soon as they are announced.
Risk and confidenceIn a panic, money tends to move into currencies seen as safe, traditionally the US dollar, the Swiss franc and the yen, and out of riskier ones.

In the long run, prices pull currencies toward purchasing power parity (PPP). The idea: once converted into the same currency, the same goods should cost about the same everywhere, or traders would buy where they are cheap and sell where they are dear. So a country with higher inflation should see its currency weaken over time. The Economist tests this with the price of a Big Mac. In January 2026 a Big Mac cost $6.12 in the US, the equivalent of $9.08 in Switzerland and $2.47 in Taiwan. By this measure the Swiss franc was 48% overvalued against the dollar and the Taiwan dollar 60% undervalued.

Sources: The Economist's Big Mac index, January 2026, via Mappr; Statbase.

PPP is a weak guide in the short run. A Big Mac includes local rent and wages, which cannot be traded across borders, so it is naturally cheaper in countries where those are cheap. Currencies can stay far from PPP for years. Analysts use it as a long-term anchor, not a trading signal.

Interest rates also set the forward rate. Suppose you will need US dollars in a year. You can fix the rate today with a forward contract. The forward rate is set so that nobody can earn a risk-free profit by borrowing in one currency and lending in the other, a rule called covered interest parity:

Forward = Spot × 1 + rate on second currency1 + rate on base currency

If USD/TWD is 30.00 today, US one-year rates are 4% and Taiwan's are 2%, the one-year forward is 30.00 × 1.02 ÷ 1.04 = 29.42 (illustrative figures). The currency with the higher interest rate trades at a lower rate forward. Its extra interest is exactly offset by the worse exchange rate, which is the law of one price from the Foundations lesson on arbitrage.

The carry trade bets that this offset won't happen in practice. Investors borrow in a low-rate currency, convert, and invest in a high-rate one, without fixing the exchange rate. It pays the difference in interest as long as the currencies stay put, and can lose much more if they don't. From mid-2023, US rates were around 5.3% while Japan's were −0.1%, so borrowing yen to invest in dollar assets became a popular trade. The yen had begun to rise in July 2024, and after the Bank of Japan raised rates on July 31 it kept climbing, up 14% against the dollar in less than a month. Investors rushed to repay their yen loans, and on August 5 Japan's Nikkei stock index fell 12.4%, its worst day since 1987.

Sources: BIS Bulletin 90; Reuters, via Fox Business; Fair Observer.

Each country chooses how much to control its exchange rate. The main options:

FloatingThe market sets the rate and the central bank rarely steps in. The US dollar, euro, yen and pound float.
Managed floatThe market sets the rate, but the central bank buys or sells currency to smooth sharp moves. Taiwan's central bank works this way.
Fixed or peggedThe central bank promises a set rate and trades whatever it takes to hold it. Hong Kong has held its dollar between 7.75 and 7.85 per US dollar since 2005, within a link to the US dollar that began in 1983.

A fourth option is to share a currency, as the countries of the euro area do, or to adopt another country's, as El Salvador did with the US dollar. Each choice is a trade-off. A peg gives businesses certainty, but the country gives up its own interest rate policy: to hold its peg, Hong Kong's interest rates have to follow US rates, whatever its own economy needs. A float lets the currency absorb shocks, but brings swings like Taiwan's in 2025.

Source: Hong Kong Monetary Authority.

A peg lasts only as long as the central bank can and will defend it. In 2011 Switzerland set a floor of 1.20 francs per euro to stop investors fleeing the euro crisis from pushing the franc too high, and bought foreign currency in huge amounts to defend it. On January 15, 2015, without warning, it gave up. The franc rose almost 30% against the euro within minutes, and Swiss shares fell sharply because their companies' foreign earnings were suddenly worth less in francs. Pegs that break suddenly are a recurring feature of financial crises, the subject of the next lesson.

Sources: Swissinfo; Swiss National Bank, via BIS; Reuters, via Daily FT.

For investors, a foreign investment carries two returns: the asset's own return, and the change in the currency it is priced in. They multiply:

Home return = (1 + asset return) × (1 + currency change) − 1

A Taiwanese investor buys US shares that rise 10% in US dollars over a year. Over the same year the US dollar falls 8% against the Taiwan dollar. What is the investor's return in Taiwan dollars?

Return in home currency:
A negative currency change means the foreign currency weakened against yours.

Companies are exposed in three ways. Exporters earn foreign currency but pay costs at home, so a stronger home currency squeezes them, while importers gain. Companies with foreign subsidiaries report those earnings in the home currency, so their reported profits move with exchange rates even if nothing changed in the business. And anyone who borrows in a foreign currency, while earning in their own, can see their debt jump if their currency falls. Many protect themselves by hedging: fixing future exchange rates with forwards, or matching foreign income with foreign costs and debts. Hedging costs money and removes gains as well as losses, which is why Taiwan's insurers had left much of their dollar holdings unhedged before May 2025.

How professionals use it

Corporate treasurers
Decide how much of the company's foreign income, costs and debt to hedge, and with which contracts.
Equity analysts
Separate a company's growth from currency effects, often reporting "constant-currency" growth alongside the reported figure.
Fund managers
Decide whether to hedge the currency risk of foreign holdings, since it can add to or wipe out the return on the assets themselves.
Central banks and economists
Track the exchange rate as one of the channels of monetary policy, since a weaker currency raises import prices and inflation.

Every international price, profit and investment is really two bets: one on the thing itself, one on the currency.
