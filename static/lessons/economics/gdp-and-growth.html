GDP and growth

What GDP measures, how it is added up, why real growth is the number that matters, and where long-run growth comes from.

The economy · Lesson 1 of 3 · 6 minutesBefore this: Percentages and percentage change

In plain words

GDP, gross domestic product, is the value of everything a country produces in a year. When it grows, the country is producing more and, on average, people are earning more. It's the single most watched number about an economy.

The US Bureau of Economic Analysis reports how much the American economy grew, after adjusting for inflation:

2.8%real GDP growth in 2024
then
2.1%real GDP growth in 2025

Source: US Bureau of Economic Analysis.

A difference of less than one percentage point sounds small. For an economy of about $29 trillion, it is roughly $200 billion of output in a single year. Small differences in growth, kept up for decades, separate rich countries from poor ones.

GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period. "Final" matters: the flour a bakery buys is not counted separately, because its value is already inside the price of the bread. Counting both would count the flour twice.

GDP can be added up three ways, and in principle they give the same answer. Every dollar spent on output is a dollar of someone's income:

SpendingWhat households, businesses, government and foreigners spend on the country's output.
IncomeWages, profits, rents and interest earned by producing it.
OutputThe value each industry adds, summed across the economy.

The spending view is the one most often quoted: GDP = consumption + investment + government spending + exports − imports. For the US in 2024:

Consumer spending is two-thirds of the US economy. That is why markets react so strongly to news about jobs, wages and household confidence.

Nominal GDP is measured in current prices; real GDP removes inflation. If prices rise 3% and nominal GDP rises 5%, the economy produced only about 2% more. The difference can be large: in the last quarter of 2025, US GDP in current dollars grew at an annual rate of 4.2%, while real GDP grew just 0.5%. Real GDP is the number reported as "growth."

GDP is an estimate, and it gets revised. That same quarter was first reported at 1.4% real growth, then cut to 0.7%, then to 0.5% as more data arrived. Source: BEA, third estimate.

To compare living standards, divide by population. GDP per person tells you how much is produced for each resident. A large country can have a large GDP and still be poor per person.

Two countries start equally rich. One grows 3% a year per person, the other 2%. After 35 years, how much richer is the first?

Over the long run, growth comes from three sources: more workers, more capital for each worker (machines, buildings, software), and producing more from the same workers and capital. That last source, productivity, reflects better technology, skills and organization. It is the only one that can raise living standards indefinitely, because the other two run into limits.

GDP leaves things out. Unpaid work at home, leisure, the depletion of natural resources and how income is shared are not in it. A country can grow while most of its people see little gain. GDP measures production, not wellbeing, and it is best read alongside other measures.

For finance, GDP is the backdrop to almost everything. Over long periods, company profits cannot grow much faster than the economy they sell into. Faster expected growth tends to push interest rates up; a shrinking economy usually means falling profits, rising defaults and central bank rate cuts. That is why analysts treat each GDP release as a check on their forecasts.

Small differences in growth, compounded, separate rich countries from poor ones.
