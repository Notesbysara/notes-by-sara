Central banks and interest rates

What central banks are trying to achieve, how one short-term interest rate reaches the whole economy, why it takes so long, and why independence matters.

Money and policy · Lesson 2 of 5 · 7 minutesBefore this: What money is

In plain words

A central bank manages a country's money. Its main tool is one interest rate. Raising it makes borrowing more expensive, so people and companies spend less and prices rise more slowly. Cutting it does the opposite. The aim is to keep inflation low and steady without causing a recession.

When inflation surged in 2021 and 2022, two central banks made opposite choices:

0% → 5.25–5.5%US Federal Reserve: eleven rate rises from near zero between March 2022 and July 2023, its fastest since the early 1980s
vs
85.5%Turkey's inflation in October 2022, after its central bank kept cutting rates under pressure from the president

Sources: CNBC; Axios; Reuters, via RTÉ.

US inflation, which had peaked at around 9%, was back to about 3% by mid-2023. Turkey's lasted far longer. How a central bank sets its interest rate, and whether people trust it to do so, makes a large difference to what money is worth.

Most central banks have one main goal: stable prices. Many, including those of the US, the euro area, the UK and Japan, aim for inflation of about 2% a year, low enough not to matter much in daily life but above zero. Some have extra goals: the US Federal Reserve also has a mandate for maximum employment. Central banks are also the lender of last resort, lending to banks in a panic, which is covered in the lesson on financial crises.

The tool is a very short-term interest rate. A central bank sets the rate on overnight lending between banks, or on the reserves banks keep with it. In the US this is the federal funds rate; the UK calls it Bank Rate. Banks then price their own loans and deposits off it. In September 2026 the Fed raised its range by a quarter point to 3.75–4%, its first rise since 2023, and held it there in October.

Source: Federal Reserve, October 28, 2026.

One rate reaches the whole economy through several channels. A rise in the policy rate works roughly like this:

Policy rate risesThe central bank's overnight rate
↓
Borrowing costsLoan, mortgage and bond rates rise
Asset pricesShare and property prices tend to fall
Exchange rateThe currency tends to strengthen, making imports cheaper
ExpectationsPeople expect lower inflation and set prices and wages accordingly
↓
Spending slowsHouseholds and firms borrow and spend less
↓
Inflation easesWith less demand, prices rise more slowly

It takes time. In the Bank of England's model, a change in its rate has its fullest effect on output after about one year and on inflation after about two. So central banks have to act on forecasts, before they can see the results of what they have done. Raising too slowly lets inflation settle in; raising too far can cause a recession.

Source: Bank of England, "The transmission mechanism of monetary policy".

The central bank unexpectedly raises its rate by a full percentage point. What happens to the price of a bond you already own, which pays a fixed 3%?

Credibility is part of the tool. If people believe a central bank will keep inflation near its target, they set wages and prices on that basis, and inflation tends to stay put. That is why most central banks are independent of governments: politicians face elections and may prefer low rates now. Turkey shows what happens when that trust breaks. President Erdogan called high interest rates "the mother and father of all evil", and the central bank cut rates even as prices surged. After the 2023 elections a new economic team changed course, raising the rate from 8.5% to 50% between June 2023 and March 2024. Inflation peaked again at 75% in May 2024 before falling, and the first cut, to 47.5%, came only in December 2024.

Sources: AFP, via Dawn; AP, via ABC News; AFP, via Fortune.

When rates reach zero, central banks have other tools. They can buy large amounts of bonds to push long-term rates down, known as quantitative easing. A few went further and set negative rates. The Bank of Japan charged −0.1% on some of the reserves banks held with it from 2016, and only in March 2024 did it raise its rate, to a range of 0 to 0.1%, its first increase in 17 years and the end of the world's last negative policy rate.

Sources: The Japan Times; Bloomberg, via Fortune.

The full toolkit has a few more parts:

Open market operationsBuying and selling government bonds and lending to banks against collateral, to keep the market interest rate close to the target.
Reserve requirementsA minimum share of deposits banks must hold as reserves. Some central banks still change this actively; the US Federal Reserve cut its requirement to zero in March 2020.
Forward guidanceTelling markets where rates are likely to go. Because long-term rates reflect expected short-term rates, words alone can move borrowing costs.
Quantitative tighteningThe reverse of quantitative easing: letting bonds mature without replacing them. The Fed's balance sheet peaked near $9 trillion in 2022; it shrank it by about $2.4 trillion before stopping on December 1, 2025.

Sources: Federal Reserve, reserve requirements; Federal Reserve, balance sheet; Brookings.

How professionals use it

Bond traders
Try to predict every rate decision, because expected short rates drive bond yields and so bond prices.
Equity analysts
Use interest rates in the discount rate for valuing companies. Higher rates mean future profits are worth less today.
Bank analysts
Track how rate changes affect what banks earn on loans compared with what they pay on deposits.
Company treasurers
Decide when to borrow, and whether to fix the rate or let it float, based on where they think rates are heading.

A central bank moves one short rate, and the rest of the economy reprices around it.
