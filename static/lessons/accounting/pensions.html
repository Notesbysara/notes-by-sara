Pensions

Some companies promise staff a set income in retirement, decades away. That promise is a debt, measured as a present value, and small changes in interest rates can move it by a lot.

Advanced topics · Lesson 5 of 5 · 6 minutesBefore this: Present value, Taylor approximations

A $500 million promise

Example, with made-up numbers. Brewline's older roasting business promised its staff a pension based on their final salary. Actuaries value everything promised so far at $500 million today. The fund set aside to pay it holds $420 million of investments. What goes on Brewline's balance sheet?

The idea

There are two kinds of pension plan:

Defined contributionThe company pays a set amount into each worker's account. The expense is the contribution, and that's the end of it. The worker carries the investment risk
Defined benefitThe company promises a set pension. It carries the risk that investments fall short, or that people live longer

For a defined benefit plan, two numbers matter:

Defined benefit obligationThe present value of all pensions earned so far, discounted at a high-quality corporate bond rate
Plan assetsThe investments held to pay them, at fair value
Net pension liability = obligation − plan assets

The gap between the promise and the fund is the deficit. A 1-point fall in the discount rate widens it sharply.

Quick check

A company pays 5% of each salary into workers' own retirement accounts. Does it carry a pension liability beyond those payments?

Worked example: Brewline's plan

1. Deficit: 500 − 420$80m net pension liability
2. Service cost: pensions earned by staff this year$20m
3. Net interest: 5% × 80$4m
4. Pension expense in profit (IFRS)20 + 4 = $24m
5. Discount rate falls 1 point. The obligation's duration is 15≈ 500 × 15 × 1% = +$75m
Deficit after the rate fallabout $155 million

Step 5 is the duration idea from the Taylor approximations lesson: the obligation behaves like a long bond, so falling rates make it bigger. Under IFRS, swings like this, and differences between actual and expected returns, go to other comprehensive income rather than profit. US GAAP's expense uses an expected return on plan assets and spreads gains and losses over several years.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Leaving the deficit out of debt. A pension deficit is money owed to former staff. Analysts often add it to debt when valuing a company.
  • Trusting the discount rate blindly. A slightly higher rate shrinks the obligation a lot. Compare the rates companies use.
  • Mixing up the plan types. Only defined benefit plans create this risk. Most new plans today are defined contribution.

Remember

  • Defined contribution: pay in and you're done. Defined benefit: the company carries the risk.
  • Net pension liability = present value of promised pensions − plan assets.
  • The obligation acts like a long bond: when discount rates fall, it rises by roughly duration × change in rate.
