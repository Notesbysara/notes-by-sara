Stock compensation and dilution

Many companies pay staff partly in shares. No cash leaves, but it's still a cost: it's expensed over time, and each new share gives existing owners a smaller slice.

Advanced topics · Lesson 4 of 5 · 6 minutesBefore this: The income statement

Paying with shares instead of cash

Example, with made-up numbers. To keep its managers, Brewline grants them 1 million restricted shares, worth $20 each, that they receive only if they stay 4 years. Earlier it also gave out 2 million options to buy shares at $15. Brewline's shares now trade at $25. What does this cost Brewline's shareholders?

The idea

Restricted stock units (RSUs)Shares given for free once the employee has stayed long enough. That waiting period is called vesting
Stock optionsThe right to buy shares at a fixed price, the strike. Worth something only if the share price rises above it
The expenseThe award's fair value on the grant date, spread evenly over the vesting period. It's a non-cash cost, so the cash flow statement adds it back
The dilutionNew shares mean each existing share owns less. Diluted EPS counts the shares these awards could create

Options don't add their full count to diluted shares. The treasury stock method assumes the company uses the cash employees pay on exercise to buy shares back at today's price. Only the difference counts.

The awards add 1.8 million shares, 1.8% more, so the same $195 million of profit is shared more thinly. The added shares are drawn larger than scale so they're visible.

Quick check

Why does the cash flow statement add stock compensation back to net income?

Worked example: Brewline's awards

1. RSU expense: 1m × $20, over 4 years$5m a year
2. Options: cash from exercise, 2m × $15$30m
3. Shares bought back with it at $2530 ÷ 25 = 1.2m
4. Net new shares from options2.0 − 1.2 = 0.8m
5. Diluted shares: 100 + 0.8 + 1.0101.8m
6. Diluted EPS: 195 ÷ 101.8$1.92
EPS, basic vs diluted$1.95 vs $1.92

Many companies report "adjusted" profit that leaves out stock compensation. Brewline's $5 million is small here, but at some technology companies it's a large share of costs. Treat it as a real expense when you compare them.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Accepting "adjusted" profit at face value. Leaving out stock compensation makes profit look higher. The cost is real, only paid in shares.
  • Forgetting buybacks cost cash. Companies often buy back shares to cancel out dilution. That spending is the cash cost of the stock pay.
  • Counting every option. Options with a strike above the share price wouldn't be exercised, so they're left out of diluted shares.

Remember

  • Stock compensation is expensed at grant-date fair value over the vesting period. No cash, but a real cost.
  • Treasury stock method: net new shares = options − (options × strike ÷ share price).
  • Diluted EPS = net income ÷ (basic shares + net new shares from awards).
