Financial Accounting

The accounting equation

Every company's books rest on one line: what it owns equals what it owes plus what belongs to its owners. Every transaction keeps that line in balance.

The system · Lesson 1 of 3 · 5 minutesBefore this: nothing. Start here.

Whose money is in the coffee cart?

Example, with made-up numbers. You start a coffee cart. You put in $10,000 of your own savings, borrow $5,000 from a bank and spend $8,000 on an espresso machine. What does the business own now, what does it owe, and how much of it is yours?

The idea

Accounting sorts everything into three buckets:

AssetsWhat the business owns or controls that will bring future benefit: cash, stock, machines, money customers owe it
LiabilitiesWhat it owes to others: loans, unpaid bills, wages due
EquityWhat's left for the owners: the money they put in, plus profits kept in the business
Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Read it as: everything the business has was paid for either with borrowed money or with the owners' money. The two sides are the same total, seen two ways.

The coffee cart after five transactions. The two bars are always the same height.

Quick check

The cart pays a $500 supplier bill in cash. What happens?

Worked example: five transactions

1. You put in $10,000 of your own moneyCash +10,000 · Equity +10,000
2. Borrow $5,000 from the bankCash +5,000 · Loan +5,000
3. Buy the machine for $8,000 cashCash −8,000 · Equipment +8,000
4. Buy $1,000 of beans, pay next monthBeans +1,000 · Owed to supplier +1,000
5. Sell coffee for $3,000 cash, using $600 of beansCash +3,000 · Beans −600 · Equity +2,400
Assets = Liabilities + Equity$18,400 = $6,000 + $12,400

Every line changes at least two things and keeps both sides equal. Step 3 only swaps one asset for another, so the totals don't move. Step 5 is a profit of $3,000 − $600 = $2,400, and profit belongs to the owners, so it raises equity.

Who sets the rules?

Two companies could record the same deal in different ways. Accounting standards fix the rules, so investors can compare one company with another. US companies follow US GAAP, set by the FASB. Most other countries use IFRS, set by the IASB. The two agree on the big ideas in this subject. Where they differ, like inventory, research costs and leases, the later lessons point it out.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Thinking equity is cash. Equity is a claim on all the assets, not money sitting in a drawer. The cart's equity is $12,400 but its cash is $10,000.
  • Counting profit as a liability. Profit belongs to the owners, so it raises equity.
  • Expecting both sides to move every time. Swapping one asset for another, like cash for a machine, changes two items on the same side and leaves the totals alone.

Remember

  • Assets = Liabilities + Equity, always.
  • Every transaction changes at least two items and keeps the two sides equal.
  • Owners' money and profits raise equity. Borrowing raises liabilities.
