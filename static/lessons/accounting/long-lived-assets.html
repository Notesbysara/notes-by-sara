Long-lived assets and depreciation

A machine that works for five years shouldn't hit profit all in the year it's bought. Its cost goes on the balance sheet, then moves to the income statement bit by bit as depreciation.

Key accounts · Lesson 4 of 7 · 6 minutesBefore this: How the statements link

A $100,000 roaster

Example, with made-up numbers. Brewline buys a coffee roaster for $100,000. It should last 5 years and sell for about $10,000 as scrap at the end. Should the whole $100,000 count as this year's cost?

The idea

First decide whether a cost is capitalized, meaning recorded as an asset, or expensed straight away:

CapitalizeCosts that bring benefits over several years: the purchase price, shipping, installation, major upgrades
ExpenseCosts that only keep things running: routine repairs, cleaning, maintenance

A capitalized asset is then depreciated: its cost, less what it will be worth at the end (the salvage value), is spread over its useful life. Two common patterns:

Straight-line: (cost − salvage) ÷ life  ·  Double-declining: 2 ÷ life × book value

Both methods end at the $10,000 salvage value. Double-declining front-loads the cost.

The asset's book value (or carrying amount) is cost minus accumulated depreciation. It's an allocation of cost, not a market price.

Quick check

Brewline pays $2,000 to fix a broken belt on the roaster. Capitalize or expense?

"Left" is the book value at the end of the year. Straight-line: (100,000 − 10,000) ÷ 5 = 18,000 a year. Double-declining: 2 ÷ 5 = 40% of the start-of-year book value, stopping at salvage, so year 5 is only 2,960. Total depreciation is the same. Only the timing differs.

Impairment and disposal

Impairment: if an asset can no longer earn back its book value, it's written down at once. Example, with made-up numbers. After year 2 (straight-line book value $64,000), a new roasting method makes the machine worth only $40,000 in use. Brewline records a $24,000 impairment loss. US GAAP never lets this be reversed. IFRS allows a reversal if value recovers.

Disposal: selling an asset removes its cost and accumulated depreciation. Any difference from the price is a gain or loss. If Brewline sells the roaster after year 3 (book value $46,000) for $50,000:

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Reading book value as market value. Book value is unspent cost. The roaster might sell for more or less.
  • Missing aggressive capitalizing. Recording everyday costs as assets lifts profit now. WorldCom's collapse in 2002 involved billions of costs capitalized this way.
  • Ignoring the assumptions. A longer useful life or higher salvage value means less depreciation and higher profit. Compare these across companies.

Remember

  • Capitalize costs that bring benefits over years. Expense the rest.
  • Straight-line: (cost − salvage) ÷ life. Double-declining: 2 ÷ life × book value. Same total, different timing.
  • Impairments write assets down at once. On a sale, price − book value is a gain or loss.

Check yourself

Three questions. Try them without scrolling up.

1. A van costs $50,000, will be worth $5,000 at the end and lasts 9 years. What's the yearly straight-line depreciation?

2. An asset with a book value of $30,000 is sold for $25,000. What's recorded?

3. A machine costs $20,000 with a 4-year life. What's year-one double-declining depreciation?
