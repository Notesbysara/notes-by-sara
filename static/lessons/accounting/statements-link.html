How the statements link

The three statements aren't separate reports. They're three views of the same books, tied together at a few points. Change one number and the others move with it.

The three statements · Lesson 4 of 4 · 6 minutesBefore this: The cash flow statement

One extra cost. What changes?

Example, with made-up numbers. Brewline's auditor decides the stores wear out faster, adding $10 million of depreciation. The tax rate is 25%. What happens to profit, to cash and to the balance sheet? It's a favorite interview question, because it tests whether you see the links.

The idea

Four links hold the statements together:

The same number marks the same item in each statement. The numbers match the four links below.

1. Net income → retained earningsProfit, less dividends, adds to equity
2. Change in cash → cashThe cash flow statement explains exactly how the cash line moved
3. DepreciationAn expense on the income statement, a lower asset value on the balance sheet, added back on the cash flow statement
4. Working capitalChanges in receivables, inventory and payables on the balance sheet show up in operating cash

Quick check

Brewline pays a $20 million dividend. Does net income change?

Worked example: $10 million more depreciation

Income statement
Depreciation up−10
Tax down 25% × 10+2.5
Net income−7.5
Cash flow statement
Net income−7.5
Add back depreciation+10
Cash+2.5
Balance sheet
Property and equipment−10
Cash+2.5
Assets−7.5
Retained earnings, so equity−7.5
Profit · cash · balance sheet−7.5 · +2.5 · balances

Cash goes up, because the only real cash effect is lower tax. Assets fall by 7.5 and equity falls by 7.5, so the balance sheet still balances.

The statement of changes in equity

Other comprehensive income holds certain gains and losses that skip the income statement and go straight to equity, such as currency effects on foreign subsidiaries. Brewline has none.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Forgetting tax. A pre-tax cost of 10 lowers net income by 10 × (1 − tax rate), not by 10.
  • Putting dividends on the income statement. They go through equity and financing cash flow only.
  • Getting working capital signs backwards. A rise in an asset like receivables uses cash. A rise in a liability like payables frees cash.

Remember

  • Net income flows into retained earnings and starts the cash flow statement.
  • The cash flow statement's change in cash equals the change in the balance sheet's cash.
  • Opening equity + net income − dividends + new shares ± other comprehensive income = closing equity.
