Intangible assets

Patents, software, customer contracts and brands can be worth more than any machine, but you can't touch them. Accounting records some of them, skips others, and treats goodwill as a special case.

Key accounts · Lesson 5 of 7 · 6 minutesBefore this: Long-lived assets and depreciation

$50 million for a company with $30 million of net assets

Example, with made-up numbers. Brewline buys Hilltop, a small roastery chain, for $50 million. Hilltop's assets, less its debts, are worth $30 million. Why pay $20 million more, and where does that $20 million go in the books?

The idea

An intangible asset has no physical form but brings future benefits. Whether it appears on the balance sheet depends mostly on how it was obtained:

Bought, or acquired with a companyRecorded at cost or fair value: patents, licenses, software, customer contracts, acquired brands
Built in-houseMostly not recorded. A brand built by years of advertising doesn't appear, however valuable
Finite lifeSpread over its life as amortization, like depreciation. A 10-year patent costing $12 million: $1.2 million a year
Indefinite lifeSome trademarks, and goodwill: not amortized, but tested for impairment every year

Goodwill is what's left when a company pays more for another business than the fair value of everything it can identify: staff, know-how, growth prospects, savings from combining.

Goodwill = price paid − fair value of identifiable net assets

The $50 million price, split. Identifiable net assets are $38 million of assets less $8 million of debts. Goodwill fills the gap.

Quick check

A company built a famous brand over 50 years of advertising. Is the brand on its own balance sheet?

Worked example: Brewline buys Hilltop

1. Fair value of Hilltop's identifiable assets28 + 6 trademark + 4 contracts = $38m
2. Less Hilltop's liabilities− $8m
3. Identifiable net assets$30m
4. Goodwill: price − net assets50 − 30 = $20m
5. Customer contracts, 5-year life4 ÷ 5 = $0.8m amortization a year
Goodwill on Brewline's balance sheet$20 million

Two years later, Hilltop's stores struggle. The unit is carried at $48 million, goodwill included, but is now worth only $40 million. Brewline records an $8 million goodwill impairment. Goodwill impairments can never be reversed, under either standard.

Research and development: a GAAP vs IFRS difference

Example, with made-up numbers. Brewline spends $10 million creating a new cold-brew system: $4 million on early research, $6 million on development after the product proved workable.

$ millions, this yearUS GAAPIFRS
Research, expensed44
Development, expensed60
Development, capitalized as an asset06
Expense this year104

US GAAP expenses almost all R&D as it happens, with exceptions such as some software. IFRS capitalizes development costs once strict tests are met, then amortizes them over the product's life.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Treating goodwill as something to sell. It can't be sold alone. It only has value as part of the business bought.
  • Skipping impairments. A large goodwill write-down is an admission that the buyer overpaid. Check the history of a company's deals.
  • Comparing R&D-heavy companies blindly. An IFRS company capitalizing development will show higher profit, and more assets, than a US GAAP rival spending the same.

Remember

  • Bought intangibles are recorded. Most built in-house aren't. Finite-life ones are amortized.
  • Goodwill = price − fair value of identifiable net assets. It's tested for impairment yearly, never amortized under IFRS or for public US companies, and never written back up.
  • US GAAP expenses R&D. IFRS capitalizes development once it meets the tests.

Three questions. Try them without scrolling up.

2. A patent bought for $12 million has 8 years of useful life left. What's the yearly amortization?
