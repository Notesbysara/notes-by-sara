Liabilities and provisions

Some debts come with an invoice. Others have no bill yet, an uncertain size, or may never happen at all. Accounting sorts them by how sure and how measurable they are.

Key accounts · Lesson 6 of 7 · 5 minutesBefore this: Accrual vs cash accounting

Warranties and a lawsuit

Example, with made-up numbers. Brewline sold 20,000 home espresso machines with a one-year warranty. A customer is also suing over a burn, and a supplier says Brewline broke a contract. None of these has a bill yet. Which belong on the balance sheet?

The idea

Liabilities run from certain to merely possible:

Accounts payableAn invoice has arrived. Amount and date known
Accrued liabilitiesOwed but not yet billed: wages, interest, the December electricity bill that arrives in January
ProvisionsLikely and measurable, but timing or amount uncertain: warranties, restructuring, likely legal losses
Contingent liabilitiesOnly possible, or impossible to measure. Explained in the notes, not recorded

How sure is the loss, and can it be measured? That decides where it goes.

The two standards set the bar slightly differently. IFRS records a provision when a loss is "more likely than not", above 50%. US GAAP uses "probable", generally read as a higher bar.

Quick check

December's electricity bill arrives on January 10. What does Brewline record at December 31?

Worked example: Brewline's year end

Warranties: 20,000 machines × 5% expected to fail × $80 repairProvision $80,000
Burn lawsuit: lawyers expect to lose, best estimate $500,000Provision $500,000
Supplier dispute: could cost $1 million, but lawyers think Brewline will probably winNote only
Provisions recorded$580,000

Each provision is an expense now (Dr Warranty expense or Legal expense, Cr Provision). When a machine is repaired, the cost uses up the provision instead of hitting profit again. The supplier dispute appears only as a note, so it never touches the numbers, but investors can still read about it.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Skipping the notes. Contingent liabilities never reach the balance sheet. A large one can matter more than many recorded items.
  • Missing "cookie jar" provisions. Setting provisions too high in a good year, then releasing them in a bad one, smooths profit. Watch for big releases.
  • Mixing payables and accruals. Both are owed. The difference is only whether a bill has arrived yet.

Remember

  • Payables have a bill. Accrued liabilities are owed but not yet billed.
  • Provisions: likely and measurable, so recorded as an expense and a liability now.
  • Contingent liabilities: only possible or not measurable, so disclosed in the notes. Remote ones are ignored.

Check yourself

Three questions. Try them without scrolling up.

1. A company sells 10,000 heaters. It expects 3% to need a $50 warranty repair. What's the provision?

2. A lawsuit could cost $3 million, but the company will probably win. How is it reported?

3. Staff earned $40,000 in the last week of the year, paid next month. What's on the year-end balance sheet?
