Associates and the equity method

Owning a big minority stake gives a company a say, but not control. It doesn't consolidate the other company's lines. It records one investment that grows with its share of the profits.

Advanced topics · Lesson 3 of 5 · 5 minutesBefore this: Consolidation

A 30% stake in a supplier

Example, with made-up numbers. Brewline buys 30% of Island Roasters for $30 million and gets two seats on its board. Island earns $20 million this year and pays $5 million of dividends to all its owners. What does Brewline report?

The idea

How an investment in another company is reported depends on how much say it brings:

20% and 50% are guides. What counts is the real influence, such as board seats.

A company with significant influence but not control is an associate. Joint ventures, run jointly with partners, are treated the same way. Both use the equity method:

StartRecord the investment at cost: one line on the balance sheet
Each year, add your share of profitIt also appears as one line in the income statement: "share of profit of associates"
Subtract dividends receivedThey're cash taken out of the investment, not income
Closing value = cost + share of profit − dividends received

Quick check

Brewline receives a dividend from Island Roasters. Does Brewline's net income go up?

Worked example: Brewline's stake in Island

1. Cost of the 30% stake$30.0m
2. Share of profit: 30% × 20+ $6.0m, in Brewline's income
3. Dividends received: 30% × 5− $1.5m, received as cash
Investment in associate at year end$34.5 million

Brewline's income statement shows $6 million on one line, not Island's sales and costs. Its balance sheet shows one $34.5 million asset, not Island's machines or debts. Island's debt never appears on Brewline's balance sheet.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Counting dividends as income. Under the equity method, dividends reduce the investment.
  • Missing hidden debt. An associate's borrowing stays off the investor's balance sheet. A company with big associates can carry more risk than its own debt suggests.
  • Treating 20% as a hard rule. A 15% stake with board seats can be an associate. A 25% stake with no say may not be.

Remember

  • Under about 20%: an investment. About 20% to 50% with significant influence: an associate. Over 50%: consolidate.
  • Equity method: cost + share of profit − dividends received, all on one line.
  • The associate's sales, assets and debts don't appear in the investor's statements.
