Financial Accounting

Double-entry bookkeeping

Every transaction is written twice: once as a debit, once as a credit, for the same amount. That simple rule keeps the accounting equation balanced and lets you check the books.

The system · Lesson 2 of 3 · 6 minutesBefore this: The accounting equation

How does the coffee cart keep track?

Example, with made-up numbers. In the last lesson, the coffee cart made five transactions and we tracked the totals by hand. A real business makes thousands a month. It needs a system that records each one the same way and can prove nothing was missed.

The idea

Each account has two sides. The left side is called debit (Dr) and the right side credit (Cr). Neither means good or bad. Which side makes an account go up depends on its type:

Debit increasesAssets, expenses, dividends
Credit increasesLiabilities, equity, revenue

Memory aid: DEA on the left, LER on the right ("DEALER"). Revenue is a credit because it raises equity. Expenses are debits because they lower it.

Every transaction becomes a journal entry with at least one debit and one credit, and the debits always equal the credits. The entries are then sorted into a ledger, one page per account, often drawn as a T-account:

Two ledger accounts from the coffee cart. Add each side, and the difference is the balance.

Quick check

The cart pays $400 of wages in cash. What's the entry?

Worked example: the coffee cart's journal

EntryDebitCredit
1. Owner puts in $10,000
Dr Cash10,000
Cr Owner's capital10,000
2. Borrow $5,000
Dr Cash5,000
Cr Bank loan5,000
3. Buy the machine for cash
Dr Equipment8,000
Cr Cash8,000
4. Buy beans on credit
Dr Inventory1,000
Cr Accounts payable1,000
5a. Sell coffee for cash
Dr Cash3,000
Cr Revenue3,000
5b. Beans used up
Dr Cost of goods sold600
Cr Inventory600

Credits are indented, by tradition. The sale is two entries: one for the money coming in, one for the beans going out.

Finally, list every account's balance in a trial balance. If the debits and credits were entered correctly, the two columns match:

AccountDebitCredit
Cash10,000
Equipment8,000
Inventory400
Cost of goods sold600
Owner's capital10,000
Bank loan5,000
Accounts payable1,000
Revenue3,000
Total debits = total credits19,000 = 19,000

The totals are $19,000, not the $18,400 of assets, because revenue and cost of goods sold are still listed separately. When the period closes, they combine into the $2,400 profit inside equity.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Reading debit as bad. A debit just means the left side. Debiting cash means cash went up.
  • Using your bank's wording. When your bank "credits" your account, it's recording a liability on its books: it owes you more. On your books, that cash is a debit.
  • Trusting a balanced trial balance too much. It catches one-sided or unequal entries. It won't catch a missing transaction or an amount put in the wrong account.

Remember

  • Debit is the left side, credit the right. Assets and expenses rise with debits. Liabilities, equity and revenue rise with credits.
  • Every journal entry has equal debits and credits.
  • Journal → ledger (T-accounts) → trial balance, where total debits must equal total credits.

Check yourself

Three questions. Try them without scrolling up.

1. A company buys $2,000 of equipment and will pay next month. What's the entry?

2. Which of these increases with a credit?

3. A trial balance shows debits of $50,000 and credits of $49,100. What does that tell you?
