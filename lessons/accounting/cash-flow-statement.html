The cash flow statement

Profit is an opinion built on accrual rules. Cash is a fact. The cash flow statement shows where a company's cash actually came from and where it went, in three groups.

The three statements · Lesson 3 of 4 · 6 minutesBefore this: The income statement, The balance sheet

$195 million of profit, $5 million more cash?

Example, with made-up numbers. Brewline earned $195 million last year, yet its cash rose only from $115 million to $120 million. Where did the rest go? The cash flow statement answers that line by line.

The idea

Every cash movement goes into one of three groups:

Operating activitiesCash from running the business: customers paying, suppliers and staff being paid, tax
Investing activitiesBuying or selling long-term assets: equipment, stores, other companies
Financing activitiesDealing with lenders and shareholders: borrowing, repaying, issuing shares, paying dividends

Brewline's cash over the year, in $ millions. Operating cash paid for the new stores, the debt repayment and the dividends.

Quick check

Brewline buys new espresso machines for cash. Which group is that?

Worked example: Brewline's cash flow statement

Most companies show operating cash with the indirect method: start from net income and undo the accrual effects.

Capital expenditure: new stores and machines(120)
Cash from investing activities(120)

Debt repaid(40)

Cash from financing activities(125)
Change in cash: 250 − 120 − 1255
Free cash flow = operating cash − capex = 250 − 120 = $130 million

Cash goes from 115 to 120, matching the balance sheet. Free cash flow is the cash left after keeping the business running and growing: what's truly available for lenders and shareholders. Valuation leans on it heavily.

The direct method lists the actual cash flows instead: $995 million received from customers, less $390 million to suppliers, $250 million in operating costs, $40 million of interest and $65 million of tax. It also reaches 250. IFRS and US GAAP both prefer it, but few companies use it.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Thinking depreciation creates cash. It's added back only because it was subtracted from profit without any cash leaving. The cash left when the asset was bought.
  • Praising any rise in operating cash. Delaying payments to suppliers lifts it for a while, but that can't go on forever.
  • Comparing groups across standards. Under US GAAP, interest paid is always operating. IFRS lets companies put it in operating or financing, so check before comparing two companies.

Remember

  • Three groups: operating, investing, financing. Together they explain the change in cash.
  • Indirect method: net income + non-cash costs ± changes in working capital = operating cash.
  • Free cash flow = operating cash − capital expenditure.
