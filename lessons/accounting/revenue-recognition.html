Revenue recognition

Revenue is usually a company's biggest number, and the easiest to push forward. One five-step model, shared by US GAAP and IFRS, decides how much counts and when.

Key accounts · Lesson 1 of 7 · 6 minutesBefore this: Accrual vs cash accounting

A $600 bundle. How much is revenue today?

Example, with made-up numbers. Brewline sells a home bundle for $600 paid upfront: an espresso machine, delivered today, plus 12 months of bean deliveries. Can Brewline book all $600 as revenue the day the customer pays?

The idea

Revenue counts when the company hands over what it promised. The five-step model applies that to any contract:

  1. Identify the contract with the customer.
  2. Identify the performance obligations: each separate promise, like a machine and a subscription.
  3. Set the price for the whole contract, including discounts and likely refunds.
  4. Allocate the price to each promise in proportion to its standalone selling price.
  5. Recognize revenue when, or as, each promise is fulfilled: at a point in time, or spread over time.

Machine revenue on day one, bean revenue spread over the year. The line is the deferred revenue still owed to the customer.

Quick check

Brewline sells a $50 gift card. When is the revenue recognized?

Worked example: splitting the bundle

Sold on their own, the machine goes for $400 and a year of beans for $300: $700 in total. The bundle's $600 is shared in that 4 : 3 ratio.

1–2. Contract with two promisesmachine · 12 months of beans
3. Price$600
4. Allocate: machine600 × 400 ÷ 700 = $342.86
4. Allocate: beans600 × 300 ÷ 700 = $257.14
5. Day one: machine deliveredrevenue $342.86 · deferred $257.14
5. Each month of beans257.14 ÷ 12 = $21.43
Revenue on day one$342.86, not $600

The entry on day one: Dr Cash 600, Cr Revenue 342.86, Cr Deferred revenue 257.14. Each month after: Dr Deferred revenue 21.43, Cr Revenue 21.43.

Long-term contracts

When the work stretches over years, like building a factory, revenue is recognized over time as the work progresses. A common measure is costs so far ÷ total expected costs. Example, with made-up numbers. A $10 million contract expected to cost $8 million has $2 million of costs so far: 25% done, so $2.5 million of revenue. If the cost estimate rises, past revenue is revised. Watch for that in company reports.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Booking the whole bundle upfront. It flatters this year's revenue at the expense of next year's. It's a classic red flag.
  • Allocating by list price. The split uses standalone selling prices: what each part actually sells for alone.
  • Trusting progress estimates blindly. Revenue over time depends on cost estimates. Big revisions can signal earlier optimism.

Remember

  • Five steps: contract, promises, price, allocate, recognize when each promise is fulfilled.
  • Bundles are split by standalone selling prices. Cash received for promises not yet fulfilled is deferred revenue.
  • Long projects recognize revenue over time, often by costs so far ÷ total expected costs.
