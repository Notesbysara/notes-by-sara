Inventory

When identical goods are bought at different prices, which cost goes to the income statement? The answer depends on the method, and it can change profit by a lot.

Key accounts · Lesson 3 of 7 · 6 minutesBefore this: The income statement

Same beans, three prices

Example, with made-up numbers. Brewline buys coffee beans three times in a year as prices rise: 1,000 kg at $10, then 1,000 kg at $12, then 1,000 kg at $14. It sells 2,000 kg for $25 a kg. The beans are identical. Which cost belongs to the 2,000 kg sold?

The idea

Every unit bought ends up in one of two places: sold, as cost of goods sold on the income statement, or still on the shelf, as inventory on the balance sheet.

Opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory = cost of goods sold

A cost flow method decides which costs go where. It doesn't have to match which beans physically left the warehouse:

FIFO: first in, first outThe oldest costs are sold first. Inventory holds the latest costs
Weighted averageEvery unit carries the average cost of all units available
LIFO: last in, first outThe newest costs are sold first. Allowed under US GAAP, banned under IFRS

The same $36,000 of beans, split three ways. The darker part goes to cost of goods sold, the lighter part stays as inventory.

Quick check

When prices are rising, which method shows the highest profit?

Gross profit gap, FIFO vs LIFO$4,000 on identical beans

Revenue is 2,000 kg × $25. FIFO: 1,000 × 10 + 1,000 × 12 = 22,000. Average: 36,000 ÷ 3,000 = $12 a kg, × 2,000 = 24,000. LIFO: 1,000 × 14 + 1,000 × 12 = 26,000. In every case, cost of goods sold + closing inventory = 36,000: the method only moves cost between the two statements and between years.

Write-downs

Inventory can't stay on the balance sheet above what it will fetch. If the value falls, it's written down to its net realizable value: the expected selling price less the costs to sell. Example, with made-up numbers. If the last 1,000 kg are damaged and now worth $9 a kg, FIFO inventory of $14,000 is cut to $9,000, and $5,000 goes straight to expense. IFRS lets a write-down be reversed if prices recover. US GAAP doesn't.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Thinking the method follows the goods. A grocer sells the oldest milk first, but could still use weighted average costs. The method is about costs, not shelves.
  • Comparing companies on different methods. A FIFO company looks more profitable than a LIFO rival when prices rise. US companies using LIFO disclose the difference, called the LIFO reserve.
  • Ignoring piling stock. Inventory growing faster than sales can mean slow sellers, and a write-down to come.

Remember

  • Opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory = cost of goods sold.
  • FIFO sells the oldest costs, LIFO the newest, weighted average the mean. LIFO is US GAAP only.
  • Inventory is written down when it's worth less than it cost. The write-down is an expense.
