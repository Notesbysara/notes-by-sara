Leases

Renting a store for five years is a lot like borrowing to buy it. Since 2019, both US GAAP and IFRS put most leases on the balance sheet: an asset for the right to use, and a debt for the payments owed.

Key accounts · Lesson 7 of 7 · 6 minutesBefore this: Present value, Long-lived assets and depreciation

$500,000 of rent, and no debt?

Example, with made-up numbers. Brewline signs a 5-year lease on a store: $100,000 a year, paid at each year end, with no way out. Under the old rules this showed up only as rent each year, with nothing on the balance sheet. But Brewline owes $500,000 it can't cancel. Isn't that debt?

The idea

The tenant, called the lessee, records two things on day one, both equal to the present value of the payments:

Right-of-use assetThe right to use the store for 5 years. Depreciated over the lease
Lease liabilityThe payments owed, discounted. Each payment covers interest first, then reduces the debt
Lease liability = present value of the lease payments

Leases of 12 months or less can stay off the balance sheet under both standards. IFRS also exempts low-value items like laptops.

The store lease's yearly cost in profit. Under IFRS, interest shrinks as the debt is paid down, so the cost is front-loaded. Both total $500,000 over the 5 years.

Quick check

Brewline rents a pop-up kiosk for 6 months. Must it record a right-of-use asset?

Worked example: the store lease

Brewline's borrowing rate is 6%.

1. Present value of 5 × $100,000 at 6%$421,236
2. Day one: right-of-use asset and lease liability$421,236 each
3. Year 1 interest: 6% × 421,236$25,274
4. Year 1 payment: interest 25,274, debt repaid$74,726
5. Year 1 depreciation: 421,236 ÷ 5$84,247
Year 1 lease cost under IFRS84,247 + 25,274 = $109,521

Here the standards differ in the income statement. IFRS treats every lease this way, so the cost is higher early and lower late as interest falls. US GAAP keeps most store leases as operating leases, with one even lease cost of $100,000 a year. The balance sheet asset and liability appear under both.

Effect on ratios

The US GAAP column is an operating lease. EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization. IFRS moves rent out of operating costs into depreciation and interest, so EBITDA jumps. Compare companies under different standards with care.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Using old data. Before 2019, operating leases sat off the balance sheet. Debt figures from before then aren't comparable with today's.
  • Comparing EBITDA across standards. An IFRS retailer shows much higher EBITDA than an identical US GAAP retailer with operating leases.
  • Ignoring the discount rate. A higher rate gives a smaller lease liability. Check what rate a company uses.

Remember

  • A lessee records a right-of-use asset and a lease liability, each equal to the present value of the payments.
  • The liability falls by each payment less interest. The asset is depreciated over the lease.
  • IFRS: depreciation + interest, higher early. US GAAP operating leases: one even cost. Both raise reported debt.

The problems aren't labeled with a lesson, so part of the work is choosing the right tool. Work each one on paper, then open the answer.

  1. A phone (sold alone for $600) and a 2-year plan (sold alone for $400) are sold together for $900. How much goes to each, and how much plan revenue is recognized each month?

    Show answer
    Revenue recognitionPhone 900 × 600 ÷ 1,000 = $540, recognized at sale. Plan $360, or $15 a month over 24 months.

  2. A $20 million contract is expected to cost $16 million. Costs so far are $4 million. How much revenue is recognized?

    Show answer
    Revenue recognition4 ÷ 16 = 25% done. 25% × 20 = $5 million.

  3. The aging schedule calls for a $6,000 allowance. The allowance is currently $2,000. What's the bad debt expense?

    Show answer
    ReceivablesTop-up: 6,000 − 2,000 = $4,000.

  4. Receivables are $50 million and yearly revenue is $400 million. What are receivable days?

    Show answer
    Receivables50 ÷ 400 × 365 ≈ 46 days.

  5. A shop buys 100 units at $5, then 100 at $7, and sells 150. What's cost of goods sold under FIFO and under LIFO?

    Show answer
    InventoryFIFO: 100 × 5 + 50 × 7 = $850. LIFO: 100 × 7 + 50 × 5 = $950.

  6. A machine costs $60,000, has a $6,000 salvage value and a 6-year life. What's straight-line depreciation?

    Show answer
    Long-lived assets(60,000 − 6,000) ÷ 6 = $9,000 a year.

  7. After 4 years, that machine (accumulated depreciation $36,000) is sold for $20,000. Gain or loss?

    Show answer
    Long-lived assetsBook value 60,000 − 36,000 = 24,000. 20,000 − 24,000 = a $4,000 loss.

  8. A company pays $120 million for a business whose identifiable assets are worth $150 million and liabilities $60 million. What's goodwill?

    Show answer
    Intangible assetsNet assets 150 − 60 = 90. Goodwill 120 − 90 = $30 million.

  9. 50,000 toasters are sold. 2% are expected to need a $30 repair. What's the warranty provision?

    Show answer
    Liabilities and provisions50,000 × 2% × 30 = $30,000.

  10. A lease has 4 yearly payments of $25,000 at year end, discounted at 5%. What's the lease liability on day one, and the first year's interest?

    Show answer
