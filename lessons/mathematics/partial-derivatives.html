Partial derivatives

Most prices depend on several things at once. A partial derivative asks about one of them: change just this input, hold the rest still, and see how fast the answer moves.

Advanced tools · Lesson 1 of 5 · 6 minutesBefore this: Derivative rules you need

Two dials on one price

Example, with made-up numbers. A bond pays $100 in 10 years. At a 5% yield its price today is $61.39. Two things will change: yields may move, and time will pass. Which matters more, and how much does each one move the price?

The idea

The price is a function of two inputs: PV(r, t) = 100 ÷ (1 + r)t. A partial derivative takes the slope in one direction only. Hold t fixed and the price becomes an ordinary curve in r. Hold r fixed and it's a curve in t. Each slice has its own slope:

Two slices through the same price, each with its tangent at today's point (5%, 10 years).

∂PV/∂r: slope as r moves, t held fixed  ·  ∂PV/∂t: slope as t moves, r held fixed

The curly ∂ says "partial": other inputs are frozen. To find one, treat every other letter as a constant and use the ordinary rules.

Quick check

What's ∂f/∂x for f = 3x²y?

Worked example: the bond's two sensitivities

1. Price today, r = 5%, t = 10100 ÷ 1.0510 = $61.39
2. ∂PV/∂r = −t × 100 ÷ (1 + r)t+1−10 × 100 ÷ 1.0511 = −584.7
3. Per 1 percentage point (÷ 100)−$5.85
4. ∂PV/∂t = −ln(1 + r) × PV−0.0488 × 61.39 = −$3.00 a year
Yield +1 point · one year closer−$5.85 · +$3.00

Step 4 is negative because more years to wait means a lower price. A year passing shortens the wait, so the price rises about $3. For small changes in both, add the effects: yield +0.1 point and one month passing gives −5.85 × 0.1 + 3.00 × 1/12 = −$0.34. The exact change is −$0.33.

Where you'll see them

Finance names its partial derivatives. For an option, delta is the slope in the share price, vega in volatility, theta in time and rho in interest rates. For a bond, duration comes from the slope in yield. Each one answers the same question: "if only this moves, by how much does the value move?"

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Letting the other inputs move. A partial holds everything else fixed. Real markets rarely do, which is why you add effects.
  • Mixing units. A slope per 1.00 of yield (100 points) is 100 times a slope per point. Say which you mean.
  • Adding partials for big moves. Adding the effects works for small changes. For big ones, curvature and interactions matter, so reprice exactly.

Remember

  • A partial derivative is the slope in one input with all others held fixed. Write it with ∂.
  • To find one, treat the other letters as constants and use the ordinary rules.
  • For small changes, total change ≈ sum of (partial × change in that input). The Greeks and duration are partials.
