The number e and continuous growth

Compound more and more often, and growth doesn't run away. It settles on a limit, and that limit is built on a number called e.

Calculus · Lesson 4 of 6 · 5 minutesBefore this: Exponents, Derivative rules you need

Does compounding more often keep paying more?

Example, with made-up numbers. You deposit $1,000 at 12% a year. Paid once a year, you end the year with $1,120. Paid monthly, at 1% a month, you get $1,126.83. What if it were paid every day, or every second?

The idea

Compounding more often does pay more, but each step adds less than the one before. The balance creeps toward a ceiling:

$1,000 at 12% for one year (the axis starts at $1,110 to show the small gaps). Going from monthly to daily adds under 65 cents, and going from daily to continuous adds only about 2 cents more.

The ceiling is called continuous compounding: growth paid at every instant. Its formula uses e:

FV = PV × er × t
e≈ 2.71828rthe yearly rate, as a decimaltthe number of years

Check: 1,000 × e0.12 = $1,127.50, the top of the chart. On a calculator, the key is ex. In a spreadsheet, type =EXP(0.12).

Where e comes from

Take 100% growth over one year and split it into n payments. You end with (1 + 1/n)n times your money. As n grows, that number settles:

Finance likes e for a second reason, from the last lesson: ex is its own derivative. Something growing at ert always grows at r times its current size, which makes the math of growth very tidy.

Worked example

Example, with made-up numbers. $5,000 earns 6% compounded continuously for 10 years. How much is there at the end, and how does it compare with yearly compounding?

1. Multiply rate by time0.06 × 10 = 0.6
2. Raise e to that powere0.6 = 1.8221

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Using the percent as a whole number. 6% is 0.06 in the power, not 6.
  • Mixing rate types. A 6% continuous rate and a 6% yearly rate are different. Convert before comparing: e0.06 − 1 = 6.18% a year.
  • Expecting huge gains. Continuous compounding is a ceiling, not a jackpot. It adds only a little over daily compounding.

Remember

  • Compounding more often pays a little more each time, up to a ceiling.
  • That ceiling is continuous compounding: FV = PV × ert, with e ≈ 2.71828.
  • A continuous rate r equals a yearly rate of er − 1.
