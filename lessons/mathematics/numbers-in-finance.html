Numbers in finance

Financial reports shrink huge numbers, hide minus signs in brackets and mix units in one table. Before any formula, you need to read the numbers right.

Numbers and percentages · Lesson 1 of 5 · 5 minutesBefore this: nothing. Start here.

How much did this company make?

Example, with made-up numbers. Here are three lines from a company's annual report. Before reading on, decide: did it make money or lose it, and how much?

($ in millions, except per-share data) 1
Income statement2026
Revenue4,215.6
Net income(312.4) 2
Earnings per share ($)(1.87) 3

It lost $312.4 million on $4.2 billion of sales, which is $1.87 per share. Three small details tell you that. Here's how to read each one.

The idea

  • 1The unit line. "In millions" means every number is shorthand: 4,215.6 really means $4,215,600,000. Always find this line first. It's usually at the top of the table, in small print.
  • 2Brackets mean negative. Accountants write −312.4 as (312.4). Here it means a loss.
  • 3Per-share figures are not in millions. "Except per-share data" means earnings per share is in plain dollars: a loss of $1.87 for each share.

Thousands, millions and billions

You'll seeMeansWritten out
kthousand1,000
m, mn, MMmillion1,000,000
bn, Bbillion1,000,000,000
tn, Ttrillion1,000,000,000,000

Each step is 1,000 times the last, so to move one step down you multiply by 1,000: $4.2bn = $4,200m. Watch out for "M": in some reports it means thousand and "MM" means million.

Rounding, currencies and basis points

Rounding. Reports round each line, so totals can be off by a little ("figures may not add due to rounding"). In your own work, round only at the end. Rounding in the middle of a calculation lets small errors grow.

Currencies. A Japanese company reporting ¥ in millions and a US one reporting $ in thousands can't be compared until both are in the same currency and unit.

Basis points. Interest rates move in tiny steps, so finance uses basis points: 1 bp = 0.01%, and 100 bp = 1%. You'll practise these in Percentage traps.

Worked example

Example, with made-up numbers. Company X reports revenue of 850,000 in a table marked "$ in thousands". Company Y reports revenue of $1.2bn. Which is bigger?

1. X in millions850,000k = $850m
2. Y in millions$1.2bn = $1,200m
3. Compare$1,200m > $850m
Bigger revenueCompany Y

At a glance, 850,000 looks far bigger than 1.2. Converting to one unit first is what keeps you from that mistake.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Skipping the unit line. The same 4,500 means $4,500, $4.5 million or $4.5 billion depending on the header.
  • Reading brackets as positive. (312.4) is a negative number: a loss, a cost or money going out.
  • Applying "in millions" to per-share figures. Earnings per share and share prices are in plain currency.

Remember

  • Find the unit line first: thousands, millions or billions.
  • Brackets mean negative. Per-share figures are in plain currency.
  • Convert to one unit and one currency before you compare. Round only at the end.
