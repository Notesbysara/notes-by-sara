Reading formulas

Many people who say "I'm bad at math" are really stuck on the symbols. A formula is a sentence written in shorthand. This lesson teaches you to read it.

Growth and equations · Lesson 4 of 6 · 6 minutesBefore this: Linear equations and graphs

Can you read this?

This is the formula for net present value, one of the most used in finance. It looks scary. By the end of this page you'll be able to read it out loud in plain English.

NPV =nΣt = 1CFt(1 + r)t− C0

The idea

Formulas use three kinds of shorthand: letters that stand for amounts, small labels that say which one you mean, and signs that say what to do. Learn a handful of each and most finance formulas open up.

Letters you'll see again and again

rrA rate: interest, return or discount rate
t, nt, nTime: t is a particular period, n the total number of periods
CFcash flowMoney in or out in a period
Σcapital sigma"Add them all up"
σsigmaStandard deviation, the usual measure of risk or volatility
μmuThe average, or expected value
βbetaHow much something moves with the market
Δdelta"The change in", as in ΔPrice

Small labels and the Σ sign

A small number or letter below and to the right is a subscript. It's a label, not math. CF1 means "the cash flow in year 1", CFt means "the cash flow in year t", and rf means "the risk-free rate". C0 is the cost at time 0: today.

The big Σ means "add up". The labels above and below say where to start and stop. Here's what it unfolds into:

Put t = 1, then 2, then 3 into whatever follows the Σ, and add the results.

Quick check

Cash flows are CF1 = $4, CF2 = $6 and CF3 = $10. What is Σ CFt for t = 1 to 3?

Now the NPV formula reads like a sentence:

"For each year t from 1 to n, take that year's cash flow and divide it by (1 + r) to the power t. Add them all up, then subtract today's cost."

That's discounting each future amount, which you met in exponents, then adding them.

Rearranging a formula

A formula is written to find one letter, but you often need another. Rearrange it with the rule from the last lesson: do the same thing to both sides until the letter you want is alone.

Worked example. The growth formula FV = PV × (1 + r)n finds the future value. Rearrange it to find the yearly rate r.

FV ÷ PV = (1 + r)nDivide both sides by PV
(FV ÷ PV)1/n = 1 + rRaise both sides to the power 1/n
r = (FV ÷ PV)1/n − 1Subtract 1 from both sides
You've rebuiltthe CAGR formula

Check it with lesson 5's numbers: (80 ÷ 50)1/5 − 1 = 9.86%. One formula, rearranged three ways, gives you future value, present value and growth rate.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Reading a subscript as multiplication. CF1 is "cash flow in year 1", not CF × 1.
  • Confusing Σ with σ. Capital Σ means "add up". Small σ means standard deviation.
  • Rearranging only one side. If you divide one side by PV, divide the other side too.

Remember

  • A formula is a sentence in shorthand. Read it out loud.
  • Subscripts are labels. Σ means add up, from the bottom number to the top one.
  • To rearrange, do the same to both sides until your letter is alone.
