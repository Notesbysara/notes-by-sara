IRR and payback

Two popular alternatives to NPV: the return a project earns as a single percentage, and how long it takes to get your money back. What each tells you, and when to trust NPV instead.

Time value of money · Lesson 8 of 8 · 6 minutesBefore this: Net present value

In plain words

The internal rate of return, or IRR, is the yearly return an investment earns, expressed as one percentage. Payback is simply how many years it takes to earn back what you put in. Both are easy to explain, which is why they are popular, but both can point you the wrong way.

When finance professors John Graham and Campbell Harvey surveyed 392 chief financial officers, they asked which methods they always or almost always used to evaluate projects:

75.7%used the internal rate of return
vs
74.9%used net present value. More than half also used payback.

Source: Graham and Harvey, "How do CFOs make capital budgeting and capital structure decisions?"

Textbooks rank NPV first. In practice, IRR is used at least as often, because a percentage is easier to compare and explain than a dollar figure. That makes it worth knowing exactly what it measures, and where it misleads.

The IRR is the discount rate that makes NPV exactly zero. There is no formula for it; it is found by trial and error, which every spreadsheet does with an IRR function. For the café oven from the last lesson, a $10,000 cost returning $3,000 a year for five years, the IRR is 15.2%: the point where its NPV line crossed zero.

The rule: accept the project if its IRR is above the hurdle rate. The oven earns 15.2% against a 10% hurdle, so it passes. For a single, ordinary project, IRR and NPV always agree.

Payback counts the years until the cash coming in equals the cash spent. The oven pays back in $10,000 ÷ $3,000 = 3.3 years. It is quick and intuitive, and it has two blind spots:

It ignores timingA dollar in year 3 counts the same as a dollar today. Discounted payback fixes this: the oven's discounted cash flows take 4.3 years to cover its cost at 10%.
It ignores everything afterCash arriving after the cutoff counts for nothing. A project that pays back in 4 years and then earns for 20 looks no better than one that stops in year 4.

IRR and NPV can disagree when you must choose between projects. Suppose you can do only one of these, with a 10% hurdle (illustrative figures):

Cost todayCash in one yearIRRNPV at 10%
Small project$100$15050%$36
Large project$1,000$1,30030%$182

You can only do one. Which makes you richer?

IRR measures the rate of return; NPV measures the amount of value created. A high percentage on a small sum can be worth less than a lower percentage on a large one. The same conflict appears when projects differ in timing, because IRR favors quick returns, and in projects with cash flows that switch sign more than once, which can have two IRRs or none.

IRR also quietly assumes that cash received along the way can be reinvested at the IRR itself. For very high IRRs that is rarely realistic.

How professionals use it

Private equity funds
Report performance mainly as IRR, alongside the multiple of money returned, since IRR alone can be flattered by returning cash early.
Corporate finance teams
Present IRR to boards because it is easy to compare with the cost of capital, while checking NPV before deciding.
Smaller and cash-tight firms
Lean on payback because getting money back quickly matters when funding is scarce.
Project financiers
Watch both IRR and how quickly debt can be repaid from project cash.

In everyday terms

The solar panels from the last lesson pay back in 10 years and earn an IRR of about 7.8% (illustrative figures). Ten years sounds long, but the IRR shows they beat a 6% alternative.

Use payback to judge how long your money is tied up, IRR to compare returns, and NPV to make the final call.

IRR gives the rate. NPV gives the value. When they disagree, follow NPV.

The problems aren't labeled with a lesson, so part of the work is choosing the right tool. Work each one on paper or in a spreadsheet, then open the answer.

  1. $2,500 is invested at 7% a year for 15 years. What is it worth at the end?

    Show answer
    Future value and compounding2,500 × 1.0715 = $6,898.

  2. Roughly how long does money take to double at 9%? How close is the estimate?

    Show answer
    Future value and compoundingRule of 72: 72 ÷ 9 = 8 years. Exact: 8.04 years.

  3. What is $20,000 due in six years worth today at 5%?

    Show answer
    Present value and discounting20,000 ÷ 1.056 = $14,924.

  4. A $15,000 loan at 7.2% a year is repaid monthly over four years. What is the monthly payment?

    Show answer
    Annuities and loan paymentsr = 0.6% a month, n = 48. C = 15,000 × 0.006 ÷ (1 − 1.006−48) = $360.59.

  5. A preferred share pays $8 a year forever and investors require 6.4%. What is it worth? A different share will pay $2 next year, growing 3% a year, at an 8% required return. What is that one worth?

    Show answer
    Perpetuities and growing perpetuities8 ÷ 0.064 = $125. 2 ÷ (0.08 − 0.03) = $40.

  6. A card charges 18% a year, compounded monthly. What is the effective annual rate?

    Show answer
    Stated vs effective rates1.01512 − 1 = 19.56%.

  7. An investment pays $1,000, $2,000 and $4,000 at the end of years 1, 2 and 3. What is it worth today at 10%?

    Show answer
    Uneven and growing cash flows909.09 + 1,652.89 + 3,005.26 = $5,567.

  8. You expect $5,000 a year for three years, stated in today's money. Nominal rates are 8% and inflation 3%. What rate should you discount at, and what is the stream worth?

    Show answer
    Uneven and growing cash flowsThe flows are real, so use the real rate: 1.08 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 4.85%. Value: $13,653.

  9. A machine costs $50,000 and adds $14,000 of cash a year for five years. The hurdle rate is 9%. What is its NPV? Should it be bought?

    Show answer
    Net present value14,000 × 3.8897 − 50,000 = +$4,455. Positive, so yes.

  10. For the same machine, what are the IRR and the simple payback period?

    Show answer
    IRR and paybackIRR ≈ 12.4%, above the 9% hurdle. Payback: 50,000 ÷ 14,000 = 3.6 years.
