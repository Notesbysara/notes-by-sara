Financial assets

Deposits, bonds, stocks and funds: what each one entitles you to, and where it stands when money runs short.

Money and markets · Lesson 2 of 5 · 6 minutesBefore this: What finance is and how the system works

In plain words

A financial asset is a right to receive money from someone else later. The kind of right you hold decides how much you can gain, how much you can lose, and whether you get paid before or after everyone else.

General Motors filed for bankruptcy on June 1, 2009. Two groups of investors had put money into the same company and came out very differently:

Bondholders$27 billion of unsecured bonds exchanged for 10% of the new GM, plus warrants for 15% more
vs
ShareholdersReceived nothing. Their shares became worthless.

Sources: US Treasury, 2009, Kiplinger.

Same company, same collapse. What separated them was the kind of claim they held.

Real assets produce things; financial assets are claims on what they produce. GM's factories, land and designs are real assets. Its bonds and shares are financial assets: rights to part of the cash those factories generate.

Every financial asset is someone else's liability. Your deposit is a debt of your bank; a bond is a debt of its issuer. Add up the whole economy and financial assets and liabilities cancel out. The wealth underneath is the real assets.

The four you will meet most often:

Cash and deposits

Claim
A fixed amount, payable on demand
Upside
Interest only
Main risk
Inflation; bank failure above the insured limit

Bonds

Claim
Fixed interest and repayment on set dates
Upside
Capped at what was promised
Main risk
The issuer defaults; rising rates cut the price

Stocks

Claim
A share of whatever is left after everyone else, plus a vote
Upside
No limit
Main risk
Can fall to zero

Funds

Claim
A slice of a pool of other assets
Upside
Whatever the holdings earn, minus fees
Main risk
Whatever the holdings carry

A fund is a wrapper, not a separate kind of asset. A bond fund behaves like bonds; a stock fund like stocks.

A company's profits double this year. What happens to the interest it pays its bondholders?

When cash runs short, claims are paid in a fixed order. Each group is paid in full before the next receives anything:

↓ Paid first

  1. 1
    Secured lendersBacked by specific assets they can seize
  2. 2
    Unsecured bondholdersOwed fixed payments, with no particular asset pledged
  3. 3
    Preferred shareholdersA fixed dividend, ahead of ordinary shareholders
  4. 4
    Common shareholdersEverything that is left, which may be nothing

Back to GM. There was not enough value to repay the bondholders in cash, so they took a stake in the new company instead. Shareholders sat at the bottom of the queue, and nothing reached them.

How professionals use it

Credit analysts
Judge whether a borrower can keep paying, and what bondholders would recover if it could not.
Equity analysts
Estimate what will be left for shareholders after every prior claim is met.
Corporate treasurers
Choose the company's mix of debt and equity. More debt means fixed payments that must be met in bad years.
Portfolio managers
Combine deposits, bonds, stocks and funds to match how much risk a client can bear.

In everyday terms

Your bank account is a deposit. A pension is usually a set of funds holding bonds and stocks. Each one is a different promise.

"Safe" means safe from default, not from inflation. A deposit paying 2% while prices rise 3% loses buying power every year, even though the balance never falls.

Every financial asset is a promise. What matters is your place in the queue.
