Future value and compounding

How money grows when interest earns interest, why time matters more than the rate, and a quick way to estimate doubling.

Time value of money · Lesson 1 of 8 · 6 minutesBefore this: Exponents, What an interest rate is

In plain words

When you leave interest in an account, next year you earn interest on that interest too. The pile grows a little faster every year. Over a long time, this effect, called compounding, does most of the work.

When Benjamin Franklin died in 1790, he left £1,000 each to Boston and Philadelphia, to be lent out at 5% and left to grow for two centuries. He did the arithmetic himself:

£131,000his projection for each city after 100 years, from £1,000 at 5%
then
£4,061,000his projection after 200 years, from the £31,000 kept invested

Sources: Philanthropy Roundtable, Mental Floss.

His figures were right: £1,000 compounded at 5% for 100 years is £131,501. A thousand pounds becomes over a hundred thousand, without anyone adding a penny. What happened in reality comes at the end of this lesson.

Simple interest is paid only on the original amount. Compound interest is paid on the growing balance. At 6%, $1,000 earns $60 a year under simple interest, every year. Under compounding it earns $60, then $63.60, then $67.42, and so on, because each year's interest joins the base.

CompoundSimple

$1,000 at 6% a year for 30 years. The shaded gap is interest earned on earlier interest.

The formula raises the growth factor to the number of years:

FV = PV × (1 + r)n

PV is the amount today, r the rate per period and n the number of periods. For the chart: $1,000 × 1.0630 = $5,743.

$1,000 is invested at 8% a year and left alone for 45 years. Roughly what is it worth at the end?

The Rule of 72 estimates how long money takes to double: divide 72 by the rate in percent. At 6%, about 12 years. It is accurate for everyday rates and drifts at high ones:

RateRule of 72Exact years to double
2%36.035.0
6%12.011.9
10%7.27.3
24%3.03.2

The rule works in reverse for inflation. At 3% a year, prices double, and the buying power of cash halves, in about 24 years.

Future value:

Back to Franklin. By 1990, Boston's fund was worth about $4.5 million and Philadelphia's about $2 million, far short of his £4 million each. The arithmetic was not wrong. Compounding assumes the rate holds and every bit of interest is reinvested. Over two centuries the funds did not earn a steady 5% on every pound, and any shortfall compounded too.

How professionals use it

Financial planners
Project how savings will grow to a retirement date, and show clients what starting later costs.
Analysts
Summarize growth as a compound annual growth rate (CAGR): the steady yearly rate that links a starting value to an ending one.
Lenders
Compound unpaid interest on loans and cards, so a balance left alone grows the same way savings do.
Actuaries
Compound contributions and investment returns over decades to check that pension funds can meet their promises.

In everyday terms

$5,000 invested at 7% at age 25 grows to about $74,900 by 65. The same $5,000 invested at 35 grows to about $38,100 (illustrative figures). Ten years' delay halves the result.

Compounding works against you on debt. An unpaid card balance grows on the same curve.

In compounding, time does most of the work.
