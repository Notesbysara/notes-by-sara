Professional standards, inside information and gray areas

What professional codes ask of people in finance, the rule on inside information that catches even senior figures, and a method for decisions no rule settles.

Ethics and trust · Lesson 3 of 3 · 6 minutesBefore this: Conflicts of interest and fiduciary duty

In plain words

People in finance follow written codes of conduct. One rule matters above most: if you know something important about a company that the public doesn't, you must not trade on it or pass it on. And when no rule gives a clear answer, you need a reliable way to work out the right thing to do.

On September 23, 2008, Goldman Sachs's board approved a $5 billion investment from Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway, to be announced after the market closed. One board member was Rajat Gupta, a former head of McKinsey. According to prosecutors:

16 secondsafter Gupta left the board call, his assistant phoned hedge fund manager Raj Rajaratnam
then
≈ $27 millionof Goldman shares bought by Rajaratnam's funds, two minutes before the market closed

Sources: FBI and US Attorney, 2011; SEC, 2013.

Gupta did not trade himself. He was convicted of insider trading, sentenced to two years in prison and fined $5 million, and the SEC later added a $13.9 million penalty. Passing on the information was enough.

Professional bodies in finance publish codes of ethics and standards of conduct. The wording differs, but the duties fall into three groups:

To clientsLoyalty, care, fair treatment of all clients, keeping their information confidential, and recommending only what suits them.
To employersActing in the firm's interest, not taking its clients or information for personal gain, and disclosing outside work that could conflict.
To marketsNot trading on inside information, not manipulating prices, and not spreading false or misleading information.

Inside information is defined by two tests, and it takes both.

MaterialA reasonable investor would consider it important to a decision to buy or sell, or it would likely move the price. A takeover, a large loss, a major contract.
NonpublicIt has not been released to the market in a way that gives investors generally a fair chance to act on it.

If information is both, the rule is simple: don't trade on it, and don't pass it on. It doesn't matter how you came across it, whether you were told it was secret, or whether the amount involved is small.

At dinner, a friend who works at a listed company mentions, unprompted, that next week's results will show a large loss. You were not asked to keep it quiet. Can you sell your shares in the company tomorrow?

Analysts are allowed to build a mosaic. Combining public information with small pieces of nonpublic information that are not material on their own is legitimate research, even if the conclusion turns out to be valuable. The line is crossed when one of the pieces is itself material and nonpublic.

Most ethical problems are not that clear. A client asks for a trade you think is unsuitable. A supplier offers tickets to a final. A manager asks you to soften a negative report. For gray areas, a consistent method works better than instinct:

  1. Get the factsWhat exactly is being asked, and what do you know for certain?
  2. Name who is affectedClients, your firm, colleagues, the market, and you.
  3. Find the duties and rulesWhich of your duties to clients, employer and markets apply? What does the law or your firm's policy say?
  4. Weigh the optionsThere are usually more than two. Which one meets every duty?
  5. Test it, then actWould you be comfortable explaining your choice to the client, your regulator and a newspaper? If not, choose again, or raise it with compliance.

Speaking up is part of the job. Firms are expected to have routes for raising concerns, starting with a manager or the compliance team. In the US, the SEC also runs a whistleblower program that protects and can reward people who report securities violations.

How professionals handle it

Compliance teams
Keep restricted lists of companies staff may not trade, and pre-approve employees' personal trades.
Investment banks
Separate deal teams that hold inside information from traders and analysts, with information barriers between them.
Listed companies
Set blackout periods before results, when directors and staff may not trade the company's shares.
Research analysts
Build mosaic conclusions from public and immaterial information, and stop if they receive anything material and nonpublic.

In everyday terms

If you work for a listed company, check its trading rules before buying or selling its shares, and don't share what you hear at work with family or friends who might trade on it.

Passing on a tip can break the law even if you never trade yourself.

If you couldn't comfortably explain it to the client, don't do it.

Ethics and trust
3 short questions and 3 case studies from lessons 19 to 21 · about 15 minutes

Think each one through, ideally using the five-step method, before opening the suggested answer. In the cases, more than one reasonable answer can exist; what matters is the reasoning.

  1. A fund manager reports remarkably steady returns, year after year, and holds clients' assets at a firm he controls. Which two warning signs from this section apply?

    Show answer
    Why trust holds the system togetherReturns that never wobble, and no independent custodian. Both were features of the Madoff fraud.

  2. An adviser tells a client in writing that she earns a higher commission on Fund X, then recommends Fund X. Has she done enough?

    Show answer
    Conflicts of interest and fiduciary dutyNot necessarily. Disclosure is required but not sufficient. As a fiduciary, she must also be able to show Fund X is genuinely in the client's best interest, not just that the conflict was declared.

  3. An analyst learns from a company's public filings, a store visit and conversations with suppliers that sales are slowing, and concludes the shares are overvalued. None of the individual pieces is material on its own. Can she recommend selling?

    Show answer
    Professional standards, inside information and gray areasYes. This is the mosaic approach: public information plus non-material nonpublic details. It would change if any one piece were itself material and nonpublic.

  4. Case. A long-standing client, aged 74, insists on putting most of his retirement savings into a single speculative technology stock. He says it's his money and his decision. What do you do?

    Show answer
    Gray areas · duty of careGet the facts on his finances and needs. Your duty of care means explaining clearly, and in writing, why the trade is unsuitable for him. If he still insists, you may be able to carry it out as his explicit instruction, documented as against your advice, and following firm policy. You should not quietly comply, and you should not pretend to agree. If you suspect he is being pressured or is not fully able to decide, raise it with compliance.

  5. Case. A fund company whose products you often recommend offers you two tickets to a sold-out final. Your firm has a gifts policy but it isn't clear whether this exceeds the limit. What do you do?

    Show answer
    Conflicts of interest · gray areasName who is affected: your clients, who rely on your recommendations being independent. Check the policy and ask compliance before accepting. If accepting could reasonably look as though it influences your recommendations, decline, or accept only with approval and disclosure. The newspaper test is useful here.

  6. Case. Your manager asks you to remove a paragraph from a research report that is critical of a company. The company is a major banking client of your firm. What do you do?

    Show answer
    Professional standards · speaking upYour duty is to clients and markets, who need objective research. Ask for the reason; if there is a factual error, correct it. If the request is about protecting a banking relationship, don't make the change, and raise it with compliance. Research independence from banking is a basic professional standard.
