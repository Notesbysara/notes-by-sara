What finance is and how the system works

Who supplies money, who uses it, and the two routes it takes between them.

Money and markets · Lesson 1 of 5 · 6 minutesBefore this: Numbers in finance

In plain words

Some people have money they don't need yet. Others need money they don't have yet. Finance is the system that moves money from the first group to the second, and sets a price for the time and the risk involved.

On March 9, 2023, customers of Silicon Valley Bank, then one of the largest banks in the US, tried to withdraw their money all at once:

$175 billionin deposits held by the bank
and
$42 billionrequested back in a single day

Sources: Fortune, Federal Reserve.

The bank could not pay, and regulators closed it the next morning. The money had not disappeared. It had been put to work, which is what a bank is for. To see why that is both the point of the system and its weak spot, start with what finance does.

Finance studies two things: how money is allocated over time, and how decisions are made under risk. It has three branches, and they use the same tools:

Personal financeHouseholds saving, borrowing, investing and insuring.
Corporate financeCompanies deciding what to invest in and how to pay for it.
Public financeGovernments taxing, spending and borrowing.

On one side are savers, on the other borrowers. Taken as a group, households spend less than they earn and have money to place. Businesses and governments usually want to spend more than they have, on factories, roads, research. The financial system connects them, by one of two routes.

Money moves from savers to borrowers. In return, savers receive a claim: a bond, a share, or a deposit.

Direct finance: the saver holds a claim on the borrower. Buy a bond issued by Apple, and Apple owes you money. Buy its shares, and you own part of the company. Markets are where these claims are issued and traded.

Indirect finance: an intermediary stands in the middle. Your deposit is a claim on the bank, not on the business the bank lends to. The bank carries the risk of that loan; you carry the risk of the bank.

You keep $10,000 in a bank account. Where is most of it right now?

Deposits are also most of what we use as money. When a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account, which creates a new deposit. The coins and notes in circulation are a small fraction of the total.

Bank of England, Money creation in the modern economy (2014).

Intermediaries exist because they do four things a single saver cannot.

PoolingThousands of small deposits become one loan large enough to build a factory.
Screening and monitoringBanks assess borrowers and watch them after lending. Few savers have the information or time.
Spreading riskOne loan defaulting hurts a bank with ten thousand loans far less than it hurts one lender.
Turning short into longDeposits can be withdrawn any day; loans and bonds run for years. This is called maturity transformation.

The intermediary is paid through the spread between what it earns and what it pays. A simplified bank with $100 million of loans, funded entirely by deposits (illustrative figures):

Interest earned on loans at 7%$7.0M
Interest paid on deposits at 2%−$2.0M
Spread$5.0M
Expected loan losses−$1.5M
Staff, branches and systems−$2.0M
Profit$1.5M

Most of the 5% spread is not profit. It pays for screening borrowers, absorbing defaults and running the bank.

Back to Silicon Valley Bank. It had invested much of its deposits in long-dated government and mortgage bonds. When interest rates rose in 2022, those bonds fell in value. To meet withdrawals it sold $21 billion of them at a $1.8 billion loss, the news spread, and depositors ran.

Why rising rates cut bond prices is covered later, in Present value and discounting.

That is maturity transformation failing. The system depends on depositors not all asking at once, which is why deposit insurance, capital rules and central banks exist.

How professionals use it

Commercial bankers
Take deposits and make loans. Their job is pricing the spread and judging which borrowers will repay.
Investment bankers
Help companies and governments raise money directly from markets by issuing bonds and shares.
Asset managers
Pool savers' money into funds and decide which claims to buy with it.
Regulators and central bankers
Set the rules that keep intermediaries able to pay, and lend to banks when depositors run.

In everyday terms

You are already in this system on both sides. Your savings account and pension make you a saver; a mortgage or car loan makes you a borrower.

When you choose where to keep money, you are choosing whose promise to rely on. Know who holds your money, what they do with it, and what protects you if they fail.

Finance moves money through time and puts a price on the risk along the way.
