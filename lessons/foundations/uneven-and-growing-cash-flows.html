Uneven and growing cash flows

Valuing payments that change in size and timing, the shortcut for steadily growing ones, and how to keep inflation consistent.

Time value of money · Lesson 6 of 8 · 6 minutesBefore this: Annuities and loan payments, Perpetuities and growing perpetuities

In plain words

Real payments are rarely equal or evenly spaced. When they are not, there is no single formula. You list each payment on a timeline, work out what each one is worth today, and add them up.

In December 2023, baseball player Shohei Ohtani signed a 10-year contract with the Los Angeles Dodgers. The headline and the economics told different stories:

$700 millionheadline value: $2 million a year to 2033, then $68 million a year from 2034 to 2043, with no interest
vs
≈ $460 millionpresent value as calculated by Major League Baseball

Sources: NBC, USA Today via Yahoo.

Of the $700 million, 97% arrives after he stops playing for the team. Uneven payments like these have no shortcut formula. They have to be valued one by one.

The method has three steps, and it works for any pattern of cash flows:

1. Lay them outPut every payment on a timeline at the date it actually arrives.
2. Discount each oneDivide each by (1 + r)t, where t is its own distance from today.
3. AddOnly now are the amounts in the same units, today's dollars, so they can be summed.
Payment as writtenValue today at 5%

Ohtani's contract as a timeline, in $ millions. Each green bar is that year's payment discounted at 5% to the signing date (approximate: payments assumed at year-end).

Added up at 5%, the 20 payments are worth about $338 million at signing. At 8% they are worth about $225 million. MLB's $460 million comes from the method set out in baseball's labor agreement, so it differs from a simple calculation like this one. A present value is always "as of" a date and "at" a rate. Change either and the number changes.

At 6%, which is worth more today: $100,000 now, or $20,000 a year for six years starting next year ($120,000 in total)?

When payments grow at a steady rate for a fixed number of years, there is a shortcut. It is a growing perpetuity minus the part that would have come after year n:

PV = C1r − g× [ 1 − (1 + g1 + r)n ]

Rent of $10,000 next year, rising 3% a year for five years, discounted at 8%, is worth $42,204 (illustrative figures). Discounting the five rents one by one gives the same answer; the formula just saves the arithmetic.

Keep inflation consistent. Cash flows that already include expected price rises are nominal and must be discounted at a nominal rate. Cash flows stated in today's money are real and need a real rate. Mixing them is a common and expensive mistake:

$10,000 a year in today's money, for 10 yearsValue today
Discounted at the real rate, 3.88%$81,581
Wrongly discounted at the nominal rate, 7%$70,236

Real rate from Fisher: 1.07 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 3.88%, with 3% inflation. The mistake undervalues the stream by 14%.

How professionals use it

Equity analysts
Forecast five to ten uneven years of cash flow one by one, then add a growing perpetuity for everything after.
Project finance teams
Value power plants and toll roads whose costs come early and revenues arrive unevenly for decades.
Sports and entertainment agents
Compare contract offers with signing bonuses and deferrals on a present value basis, not the headline.
Litigation experts
Value lost future earnings in damages cases, choosing real or nominal rates to match the forecast.

In everyday terms

A settlement offers $50,000 now, or $10,000 a year for six years. At 5%, the yearly option is worth about $50,757 today, almost exactly the same (illustrative figures). The higher total of $60,000 is mostly the cost of waiting.

Whenever an offer is spread over time, ignore the total and compare present values.

Put every payment on the timeline before you add anything.
