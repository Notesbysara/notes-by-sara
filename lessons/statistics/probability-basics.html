Statistics for Finance

Probability basics

Finance runs on uncertain events: a loan defaults, a deal closes, rates rise. Probability puts a number on how likely each one is, and three short rules combine them.

Probability · Lesson 1 of 7 · 6 minutesBefore this: Percentages and percentage change

Two loans, two chances

Example, with made-up numbers. A bank's records show that 3% of its loans default within a year. You hold two loans to unrelated borrowers. What is the chance that both default? And that at least one does?

The idea

A probability is a number from 0 to 1: 0 means impossible, 1 means certain. You can write it as a percentage too: 0.03 is 3%. It usually comes from counting (3 loans in every 100 default) or from a long record of past events.

Three rules combine probabilities. The picture shows two events: rates rising (40% likely) and a company missing its earnings forecast (30% likely), with the two not affecting each other.

The whole square is every possible outcome. The overlap is "both", and anything shaded is "at least one".

NotP(not A) = 1 − P(A)Rates don't rise: 1 − 0.4 = 0.6.
AndP(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)Only when the events don't affect each other (they're independent). Both: 0.4 × 0.3 = 0.12.
OrP(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)Subtract the overlap so it isn't counted twice: 0.4 + 0.3 − 0.12 = 0.58.

Quick check

A deal has a 20% chance of falling through. What is the chance it goes ahead?

Worked example: the two loans

Both default: 0.03 × 0.030.0009, or 0.09%
Neither defaults: 0.97 × 0.970.9409
At least one defaults: 1 − 0.94090.0591
Chance at least one defaults5.91%

Just adding 3% + 3% gives 6%, which counts the "both" case twice. The "at least one" trick, 1 minus the chance of none, is often the quickest route. One warning: the multiply rule only works if the loans really don't affect each other. In a recession, defaults tend to come together, which is part of what went wrong with mortgage bonds in 2008.

Common mistakes
Three slips beginners make.
  • Adding when events overlap. 3% + 3% counts "both default" twice. Subtract the overlap.
  • Multiplying events that affect each other. The "and" rule needs independence. Loans in the same town, or shares in the same industry, often move together.
  • Mixing percentages and decimals. Multiply 0.03 × 0.03, not 3 × 3.

Remember

  • A probability runs from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).
  • Not: 1 − P. And, for independent events: multiply. Or: add, then subtract the overlap.
  • "At least one" = 1 − P(none).
